# Chapter 6

## The Weight of the Crown

The air inside the Gull & Gasket’s private back-room smelled of rancid tallow, damp oak, and the sharp, metallic tang of cold-blooded treason. A single lumen-globe hung from the low rafters, its tarnished brass cage black with soot, casting harsh, unforgiving light across the scarred table. The timber was salvage—a massive deck-plank ripped from the corpse of a sunken naval dreadnought, stained black with decades of spilled rum, dried sea-blood, and clandestine bargains.

Mara sat across the timber. Her spine remained rigidly straight, her ink-stained fingers folded over a heavy, iron-clasped ledger. She wore the drab grey wool of a dock-clerk, a drab skin chosen to dissolve into Blackwater Quay’s background noise. It failed entirely to soften the predatory stillness of an elite handler. Mara had outlived three grand-dukes and two guild massacres by knowing precisely when to hold her tongue and when to slide a stiletto between rib-joints.

"Possibly," Mara said.

The word dropped into the quiet like a lead sinker into a deep well. Heavy. Unyielding. Offering no purchase for comfort.

"That does not trouble you?" Banki asked. His voice was a flatline, testing the tensile strength of her resolve.

"Less than a crown that calls a man's refusal treason."

The Crown Citizen Contract rested between them on the dreadnought wood—a thick sheet of cured vellum, stiff and arrogant, bearing the heavy, crimson wax seal of the Capital Chancery at the bottom margin. The text mapped out the exact geometry of absolute submission: exorbitant port tithes on all salvaged stock, mandatory conscription quotas for the coastal fleet, and the immediate, compulsory surrender of any unsanctioned arcana or wild-casters. It was bureaucratic ironwork—a legal vice engineered to crush the independent captains of Blackwater Quay into obedient dust.

Banki dragged the vellum across the table. The rasp of dry hide against rough oak was loud as a blade clearing a scabbard. Unstorking an iron-glass inkwell, the bitter reek of crushed gall-nuts and fermented vitriol cut through the room's stale oil. He dipped his iron quill. The nib clicked against the rim, holding a fat, shivering drop of black ink.

He brought the point down, three inches below the Chancery's designated signature mark. The nib bit deep, scratching violently into the fibers. He offered no wordy preamble. He drafted no polite grievance for a court magistrate. He added a single, jagged line of script, driving the point so hard the ink bled through the hide like a fresh wound.

*Refusal of the Crown is not betrayal.*

He laid the quill down. The black ink glistened under the lumen-globe—a ragged scar sliced across the kingdom’s absolute mandate.

Mara watched the nib finish, reading the upside-down script without moving an eyelid. Her jaw did not set; her throat did not twitch. She simply gave a single, icy nod, accepting the lethal reality of the stroke. Her first rebellion against the throne carried no heraldry. It was merely the first strike in a very quiet, very bloody war.

## The Eldritch Hour

Stillness mimicked a cage too closely for comfort. When the tavern floorboards above finally stopped groaning under the boots of drunk sailors, when Blackwater Quay’s endless reek of fish-scale and night-soil subsided into a fitful slumber, Banki went down into the dark.

The cellar beneath the Gull & Gasket waited in pitch silence.

Solitary training was his only true refusal. Refusal to sleep while an ambitious rival might measure his neck for iron. Refusal to let his body devolve into passive waiting. Refusal to learn, when the cold steel was already in his guts, that he had left a weakness unpatched.

He snapped his fingers, sparking a pinhead flame that lit the subterranean vault. The yellow glare caught the marrow-runes he had cut into the floor flagstones over six brutal weeks. Ground from the scorched femurs of predatory drakes and tempered with quicksilver, the sigils spiraled across the floor in lethal defensive arcs. Bone-dust vectors marked out clean kill-lanes. Blood-iron nails had been hammered into the load-bearing pillars, anchoring the volatile magic straight into the bedrock. Three mechanical deadbolts and two biometric blood-latches held the ironwood door. Sable’s acoustic baffles—compressed cork, woven ghost-silk, and deadened air-pockets—sheathed the stone, sealing the vault in a tomb of silence.

The room met standard parameters for safety—a meaningless metric. Safety was a fantasy sold to weak men to keep them docile. Absolute, overwhelming dominance was the only currency that held value.

Banki stepped into the exact center of the bone-dust circle.

He raised his right hand. Magic answered instantly, raw and violent. The spell coalesced in his palm—a black-violet mass of kinetic malice that stripped the heat from the surrounding air. The Weave Spike twisted into the shape of a screaming, hollow-eyed skull, shedding a sharp reek of scorched copper and ozone. He bypassed the safety limiters, altering the formula mid-manifestation, burning structural stability to maximize sheer armor-piercing velocity.

He released it.

The bolt crossed the vault in a fraction of a heartbeat, slamming into the target-wardstone anchored in the far wall. The impact detonated like a siege-engine firing inside a tomb. The massive block of granite shuddered to its roots, glowing a dull, angry cherry-red as the kinetic and arcane violence was siphoned off by the blood-iron anchors and dumped harmlessly into the earth.

Again.

He drew mana faster, ignoring the sharp, electrical burn tearing along his forearms. He compressed the screaming visage of the spell, crushing the volatile energy into a dense, silent needle. The Tri-Weave clicked into alignment—a ruthless fusion of three contradictory disciplines. Arcane geometry dictated the rigid flight path. Sovereign alignment asserted absolute right-of-way, stripping the target's natural spell-shedding properties. Psionic force braided the dual currents into an adaptable, piercing drill.

He released it.

The lance punched straight into the glowing red crater left by the previous strike. The Tri-Weave adapted dynamically to the stone's thermal density on contact, shifting frequencies in a microsecond. The strike drove a smoking, two-inch hole straight through twenty-four inches of warded granite. The noisy spectacle of the skull was gone, replaced by pure, terrifying penetration. A violent answer for heavy plate, drake-hide, or vault doors.

Again.

For two unbroken hours, the cellar filled with the stench of scorched stone and the sour, battery-acid taste of spent power. Banki adjusted his footwork continuously, shifting his center of mass. He engaged full tell-suppression—swallowing incantations, killing micro-gestures, forcing high-tier magic to detonate without a fraction of warning. Every tweak was cataloged in his mental ledger alongside every other lesson the world had beaten into his flesh. Burning fatigue built in his deltoids and hamstrings—a heavy, toxic sludge he systematically bled off through Iron Halo thresholds. He monitored his heart rate, holding it at a steady, rhythmic drive.

Then came the fine work.

He glided to a scarred workbench at the periphery of the array. A set of steel picks, a double-edged throwing dagger, and a sealed glass vial of black ink sat on the raw pine.

He extended his mind. A telekinetic vise clamped around all three objects at once. They lifted, floating suspended at eye level. This was micro-torque manipulation—a brutal departure from the heavy impact of the Weave Spike. It required splitting his consciousness into three independent processing threads: weight, rotation, and fine surface pressure.

One thread worked the tension-hook through the tumblers of an invisible lock, applying precisely three ounces of upward torque while feathering a pick. A second thread turned the throwing knife on its axis, maintaining a slow, fluid spin while holding the needle-point dead center on a speck of dust drifting through the damp air. The third thread cradled the glass vial in a perfect, hovering arc, adjusting force so precisely that the black fluid inside never rippled against the glass.

Beneath the steady breathing, a shadow stirred in his deep memory.

The old room rose to the surface. Not as a thought, but as a suffocating sensory breach. The sharp, chemical stink of carbolic acid and rust burning his nostrils. The cold, unyielding iron grid biting into his naked spine. The glaring blue-white glare of the alchemical lamps blinding his eyes. Surgeon-Prefect Vane standing over his restrained form, silver calipers in hand, evaluating Banki's exposed ribcage not as a child's flesh, but as volatile raw stock.

*“Remarkable neural tolerance,”* Vane’s voice echoed, razor-clean and hollowed of humanity. *“Administer the secondary paralytic. We will map the psychic overload limit before the heart ruptures.”*

A specimen had no vote. A specimen owned no flesh. A specimen waited in agony until the men with knives decided the trial was done. The thick leather strap biting into his throat. The cold needle sliding beneath his collarbone, pumping liquid fire directly into his spine.

He had sworn long ago that no trial would ever end until he held the blade.

The glass vial cracked.

The sharp *snap* shattered the vault's quiet like a pistol shot. A hairline fracture spider-webbed across the glass, and a fat bead of black ink welled along the seam. His telekinetic grip had spasmed around the dagger the instant the memory breached his focus, transferring a micro-ton of pressure to the vial. A minor error.

Unforgivable.

Instinct demanded immediate, violent self-punishment. Add ten pounds of telekinetic load. Extend the session by two hours. Repeat the drill until the nerves, the magic, and the memory bowed to absolute command. The urge to break his own bone to enforce discipline burned hot in his throat.

He held the weeping vial in the air for five long seconds. A single drop of ink fell, hitting the pine bench with a wet tap.

He forced his hands to lower.

The restraint chafed like raw iron on open flesh. Letting a flaw pass unpunished felt like leaving a city gate wide to an army. But the crack in the glass had come not from a soft hand, but from an old flaw: treating his own mind as an enemy to be beaten into submission without considering the stress-fractures left in the weapon. A blade honed too hard simply shattered under strain.

The pick, the dagger, and the ruined vial settled softly onto the bench.

For ten seconds, Banki let the room hold absolute silence. He tracked his breath. In. Out.

The vault walls did not close in. The masonry remained dry stone, refusing to dissolve into the rusted iron cages of his youth. Above, Edric Mallon’s tavern slept its greasy sleep, the heavy floorboards groaning under the weight of slumbering cutthroats. Beyond that, Sable managed her ledgers, salt-lines, and the fragile lives of people coming to view these walls as a fortress. The psychic weight of the city pressed down—real, heavy, and grounded in cold dirt.

He took a square of coarse burlap and scrubbed the pine bench, dragging the cloth back and forth until the black ink was forced deep under his fingernails. Magic could have erased the stain in a breath. Manual labor left a physical record. It proved he occupied the room on his own terms.

He closed his eyes and unlatched his inner sight.

The physical vault dissolved into a complex lattice of lingering energy signatures. Old wards hummed with a thick amber pulse. Force-scars from yesterday’s Weave Spikes burned like deep blue bruises against the granite. Sable’s salt-lines, buried deep within the mortar, thrummed with a steady, defensive white hum. The brutal impact-points of every Tri-Weave strike hung in the air like glowing smoke. Rooms lied after violence; power always left footprints. Reading them faster than an invading squad meant the difference between setting an ambush and walking into a kill-box.

He refined the tracking technique until gray dawn light scraped through the iron street grates high overhead. As the first cart wheels rumbled above, he brushed bone-dust from his knees, verified the lock-tumblers, and pulled his psychic baffles tight around his mind.

Upstairs, Edric Mallon was already burning coffee with impressive dedication.

The tavern master stood behind the long bar, a greasy leather apron wrapped around his gut, pouring boiling water over grounds that smelled like scorched grain and beetles. Edric slid a chipped clay mug across the counter. He caught sight of the black ink rammed under Banki’s nails and swallowed whatever question he had primed. He picked up his own cup, holding it in both scarred hands, though the saucer rattled against the wood anyway.

"Rough shift?" Edric muttered.

"Useful one."

Banki drank the bitter brew, letting the heat scorch away the lingering taste of ozone and dried blood. Three hours of sleep, four of discipline. Sleep kept the body from making fatal errors; discipline reminded the flesh that submission was a death sentence.

## The Caravan Launches

The Sablehook staging yard drowned in predawn mist—a thick, coastal wall rolling off Blackwater Quay that reeked of salt-damp, rotting kelp, and old copper. The one-week acquisition run operated under strict military doctrine. No public fanfare. No inventory details leaked to street runners. A dull commercial transport posture, executed with the cold precision of a black-budget raid.

The yard hummed with disciplined movement.

Kestrel hovered over the heavy Expedition Dossier, which lay flat across a stack of sealed ironwood crates. Her scout-barding fit tight across her shoulders, the matte-black hide treated to swallow stray light and deaden the rustle of leather. She traced a gloved talon along the mapped route, her jaw set hard. Beneath her sleeves, the raised, scar-thickened tracks of her grafted bone-blades waited for a signal.

Vesper paced the outer perimeter, his eyes continuously reading the fog. He moved with the oily grace of a retired Crown assassin, a pair of custom repeater crossbows riding high on his thighs, loaded with heavy armor-piercing quarrel-heads.

Gravewire inspected the steel axle-tree bolts on the heavy transport wagons. His massive frame dominated the yard—less a man and more a siege-golem forged of scarred muscle, iron pins, and deep-cut marrow-tattoos. A immovable anchor ready to drop on any obstacle.

Tally, the disgraced guild clerk who viewed numbers with religious terror, shuffled rapidly through a deck of blank customs manifests, muttering under his breath as he forged the bureaucratic camouflage to fool Crown inspectors.

And Kessa stood before the six dire rhinos.

The beasts were siege-engines of flesh. Each stood six feet at the humped shoulder, armored in thick, overlapping plates of hide that resembled rusted iron plate. They blew thick plumes of white vapor into the freezing air, shifting their massive weight, their iron-shod hooves striking the cobbles with bone-jarring thuds. The thick stink of damp leather, dung, and aggressive musk filled the yard, cutting through the salt-fog. Kessa ran her bare hands over the heavy leather traces, testing the tension of the iron buckles. She maintained a quiet pain-sharing link with the beasts, keeping their rising anxiety clamped down before it could turn to rage.

Fifteen veterans. Three heavy, steel-ribbed wagons. Six dire rhinos.

Banki stood on the high loading dock, watching the gears of his machine turn. It was time to link the network.

He closed his eyes and built a telepathic pulse. He cast no open shout. He forged a single, sharp needle of thought, compressed it into a high-frequency burst, and drove it simultaneously into the minds of his squad leads.

*Status. Single line. Failures only.*

The mental network flexed under the load. Reports returned in a rapid, disciplined volley, sorted instantly by his cognitive filters.

*Route green,* Kestrel transmitted, her mental signature sharp as a razor-edge. *First leg Brinewood border. ETA locked.*

*Counter-surveillance green,* Vesper sent—a smooth, oily slide across the bond. *No tail attached to the gate. Two watchers at the split-latch displaying passive count behavior. Low-tier gang runners. No pursuit vectors.*

*Perimeter green,* Gravewire thrummed—a heavy, metallic pulse like a hammer hitting an anvil. *Gate authority verified. No forced contact.*

*Documentation dull,* Tally reported—a nervous flutter of numbers quickly suppressed. *Cover holds. We are hauling low-grade textile scrap.*

*Containment SOP active,* Kessa finished, her connection thrumming with the heavy, rhythmic heartbeat of the beasts. *Traces fitted. Empty cradles prepped for live stock. Preservative jars sealed. Zero stress spikes on the rhinos.*

All green. No criticals.

Sable stepped onto the dock. She wore her old field leathers—the scuffed gear from their days in the deep dark—her black hair bound high with a leather thong. She offered no soft farewells to the morning. An iron-bound ledger was tucked beneath her left arm. She would hold Blackwater Quay, managing the auction floor, paying the bribes, and keeping her boot on the city's throat while he drove into the wild.

"One week," Banki told her, his voice low enough that the fog drank the words before they reached the team. "Brinewood border, then the Shattercliff gap, then the turn-back. Keep the clerk's desk operating as logged. Two signatures on all high-coin paper. Maintain observe-only on the Wakeblack leads. Do not strike if they touch the perimeter."

"The floor stays boring and absolute," Sable answered. Her fingers tapped a slow, steady cadence against the ledger's iron binding. "Nobody moves without my stamp. It will be here when you return. Bigger. Colder."

Banki gave a single nod. He swung down from the dock and mounted the bench of the lead wagon. The ironwood seat groaned under his weight. He took the thick leather reins, feeling the cold, hostile tension of the rhinos at the other end.

He cracked the leather.

The rhinos leaned into the traces. Massive shoulders bunched. Iron traces pulled taut with a sharp, metallic screech. The heavy caravan rolled, iron-clad hooves striking the cobbles like a muffled drumbeat. The thick wheels ground gravel to dust as they passed through Sablehook’s outer gates, leaving Blackwater Quay to rot quietly in its sea-fog.

The city fell away past the final guard-tower, and the minds went with it.

One moment—sixty, seventy cognitive sparks burned bright against his awareness. The baker’s wife tallying grain-sacks, her arithmetic tight and sharp. A dock-hand nursing a crushed thumb, the throb of it pulsing dull and red in the static. Edric Mallon behind his bar, mug hot against his calluses, worrying about his tap-house stock. The dense, familiar pressure of human thought—warm, chaotic, relentless—pressing against his skull like a pulse he had learned to navigate by feel.

The lead wagon cleared the last slum-shackle.

The sparks died. All of them. In a single instant.

The inside of his skull went dead silent with a violence that made his teeth snap together. His ears rang with a high, thin whine—the phantom protest of a sense stripped of its medium. His balance pitched hard on the wooden bench, a sudden, sickening vertigo as the invisible framework he had leaned on for weeks vanished, leaving him tilting into the void. The absence was not quiet. It was *deafening*—the pressurized silence of a deep vault after an explosion, heavy enough to set his jaw aching and force his hands to lock onto the reins until his knuckles turned white.

He blinked twice, fighting the sensation that his vision had gone flat. The fog-slick road ahead looked stripped of depth, like a bad painting missing its shadow.

The rhinos kept pulling. The traces screeched. Mud sucked at the heavy wheels. And nothing else. No warmth. No hum of fear, greed, or ambition pressing against his mind. Only the vast, indifferent hostility of a landscape that did not think, did not care, and did not register his existence.

Something primal in his brainstem, older than language, read the silence as an ambush.

The reins stabilized. His breathing flattened. By the second mile, the void had compressed into a dull, thrumming ache behind his eyes—the psychic phantom of an amputated limb. The wild offered its own static eventually. Thin, alien signatures: the frantic pulse of a vole bolting through scrub, the icy patience of a hawk riding a thermal, the slow, vegetative hum of ancient roots drinking salt-water. Nothing like the dense architecture of the city. Noise without grammar.

Caravan discipline calcified immediately into brutal routine. Rise an hour before dawn. Audit the leather traces for rot-cracks. Check the glass preservation jars for micro-fractures. Maintain total silence for the first mile of travel to establish a clean auditory baseline. The grinding monotony stripped away city politics, leaving only mud, cold iron, and the mechanical drive of the acquisition squad pushing into the dark.

## The Brinewood

By the third day, the Brinewood swallowed them whole.

The forest did not grow; it festered. Twisted, black-barked trunks the color of dried blood choked the trail, their gnarled canopy weaving a ceiling so dense it strangled the sun, dropping the forest floor into a perpetual, blood-tinted twilight. The air beneath the boughs was heavy and hot, reeking of wet rot and the sharp, vinegar stink of corrosive moss that rusted the wagon bolts on contact. Razor-bramble—thick as a man's wrist and spiked with iron-hard thorns—coiled across the roots like sleeping vipers, ready to flay the legs of any careless scout.

The caravan slowed to an agonizing crawl. The dire rhinos snorted violently, their small red eyes rolling in their sockets as they caught the scent of apex predators bleeding through the vegetation. The corrosive moss hissed softly as the ironwood wheels crushed it, releasing small clouds of choking green gas. The weight of the forest settled over the crew like a wet shroud. The woods were old, hostile, and starving.

Deep within the gloom, a spike of disciplined emotion shot through the bond—Kestrel’s signature, tight with controlled adrenaline.

*Target. East flank. Heavy. Reedsinger.*

Kestrel dropped from a high branch without a sound, her boots finding a mossy root. She signaled her vector marks, confirming the line.

The Reedsinger. A serpentine terror logged in Chancery bestiaries as highly lethal and immensely valuable on the black-market auction circuit. The manifest demanded a live capture. Through the tangled briars, Banki caught its outline.

Its fourteen-foot body lay coiled across a flat, sun-baked slab of granite that had punched through the canopy. Its scales shifted in a mesmerizing optical cascade—transitioning from a dull, mottled emerald that matched the rot-moss to a brilliant, mirror-finish silver as it registered micro-vibrations in the dirt. The beast carried no venom. Its jaws possessed little crushing power. It required neither. The massive, ribbed throat-sac pulsing beneath its lower jaw was a biological engine of destruction. It could shatter granite, turn internal organs to jelly, and reduce a man’s brain to liquid paste with a focused sonic scream. The rock beneath it was ringed by thirty yards of pulverized stone and splintered timber—the debris of its last kill.

"Standard iron nets will trigger the throat-sac the moment they hit the scales," Vesper whispered, crouching behind a root cluster with his crossbows raised, though they were useless here. "We need it locked down before it knows it’s hunted. One scream in this timber, and the reverberation will crush our ribcages."

"Hold line. Maintain visual. Do not engage."

Banki stepped clear of the wagons. He pulled the ambient shadows from the underbrush, weaving them into a void-cloak that erased his silhouette from the light. He initiated the internal shifts. He hollowed his bones, pulling the marrow density into an arcane reserve, swallowing the sudden wave of nausea. He compressed his muscle fibers, shrinking his physical profile. He severed his cognitive weight-anchor, letting gravity release its grip on his boots.

The heavy air carried him. He drifted forward, floating three feet above the rotting moss, passing over trip-vines and dry twigs without disturbing a single molecule of air.

He hovered directly above the Reedsinger’s armored head.

The beast's mind held no words. When Banki touched its surface, it thrummed with raw, sensory impulses: *Sun-rock. Belly-heat. Meat-hunger. Air-shift. Scale-lock. Prepare-throat.* The silver scales flashed brighter, achieving a blinding mirror sheen in the dim light. The ribbed throat-sac expanded violently, drawing in a massive chest of air. It registered the shift in the environment, even if its eyes could not track the shadow hovering above its crown.

Banki unleashed the Tri-Weave.

He cast no three separate spells. He deployed a single, unified metaphysical strike engineered for absolute subjugation.

First, the arcane formula struck. It mathematically altered the space around the Reedsinger’s skull, locking the local air into a frictionless vacuum, stripping the beast of its physical evasion angles.

Second, the sovereign alignment crushed its rising panic. Banki drove an absolute, cold tranquility into the cracks of its terror—the unyielding command of a god demanding total stillness, overriding the reptilian fight-or-flight reflex.

Third, the psionic fracture severed its motor control. A telekinetic vise—honed in the cellar on glass vials and phantom locks—clamped around the beast’s jaw and the muscular resonance chambers of its throat.

The air in its lungs froze into an invisible cage.

The Reedsinger violently tried to force breath into its throat-sac to scream. The massive neck muscles spasmed against the constraint. The command from its brain met a wall of absolute static. The lower jaw refused to drop. The throat-sac remained crushed, bulging against the invisible pressure. Banki felt the strain down his own spine, micro-fibers tearing in his deltoids as he matched the beast's immense physical power with pure, focused will.

The silver scales rapidly lost their shine, fading back to a dull, lifeless emerald. Its slit pupils rolled back, fighting a losing battle against the enforced tranquility. Consciousness dropped from the violent drive of a predator into the dark stupor of an induced coma. The massive body went limp, sliding an inch down the warm granite.

"Target secured."

Banki's voice broke the quiet—dry, raspy, and devoid of heat.

Kessa moved in with the heavy capture team, executing containment SOP with practiced efficiency. Gravewire hauled a reinforced, lead-lined transport cradle into the clearing, his boots sinking into the pulverized rock. Kessa slapped a heavy alchemical muzzle over the beast's jaw, mechanically locking the throat-sac in a compressed state. Rubberized seals were verified; zero direct skin-contact permitted. The beast was logged on Tally’s manifest as LIVE-01—a prime asset destined for Sablehook’s quarantine vaults.

Banki dropped his shadow-cloak, restoring his natural mass. His boots hit the damp earth with a heavy thud. A dull, thrumming ache bloomed behind his eyes—the cost of driving the Tri-Weave without vocal components.

"Load it," Banki rasped, turning back to the trail. "Next target."

## Campfire in the Dark

Camp Eight sat at the high border of the Brinewood, perched on a rocky shelf where the canopy finally gave way to cold stone. The air here was thin, smelling of exposed granite and the sharp, clean bite of high altitude.

The fire burned low, fueled by dry cedar to eliminate smoke.

Kestrel sat at the light’s edge, silent and hyper-focused. She was breaking down her blade-mounts and foreclaws with an oiled cloth. The steady *scrape, click, slide* of steel served as a rhythmic counterpoint to the howling wind in the peaks. From the wagons came the soft, nerve-straining clatter of glass preservation jars settling, the heavy, drugged breathing of LIVE-01 in its lead cradle, and the restless shifting of the rhinos on the cold stone.

The firelight touched Banki's face without warming his skin. The cold road routine held the camp in its grip. Out in the dark, Gravewire walked the perimeter, his heavy boots deliberately masking the subtle snaps of dry brush. Near the hearth, Tally updated his manifests by lantern-light, keeping the paper trail dull and unshakeable. Kessa completed her rounds, inspecting the salt-lines and containment bolts around the live wagons.

"The Shattercliff pass," Kestrel said, her voice rough, scraped raw by cold air and three days of strict silence. She didn't look up from her claws, applying rust-inhibitor to the steel. "The gap through the Ironhorn Spur cuts two days off the return leg. Minimizes exposure with LIVE-01 and spares the rhinos the long climb over the teeth."

She paused, testing the edge of a claw against her leather-wrapped thumb.

"The locals won't touch it. They swear the gap is haunted by things that drink ambient light and remember your true name."

"Magical fauna," Banki replied, staring into the embers. "Border folk rarely separate supernatural phenomena from natural apex predators. They lack the academic taxonomy for anomalous beasts, so they build ghost stories."

"Can you verify?" Kestrel asked, finally looking up. Her golden eyes caught the firelight, glinting with predatory focus.

Banki closed his eyes and cast his psychic net out into the dark.

He bypassed the camp perimeter, driving past the standard tactical boundaries. He sacrificed near-sight clarity for distance, pushing his awareness up the sheer incline into the wind-scoured throat of the Ironhorn Spur.

The mountain night opened in his mind. Small, bright signatures of life: insects dormant in stone cracks, the sudden panic of a high-altitude vole, the cold hunger of an owl gliding through the pines. He drove deeper, into the narrow gap itself.

The noise died.

No human minds waited in the pass. No bandit ambush prepared cold steel. No necromantic void radiated the freezing static of undead.

Yet at the absolute limit of his reach, something hovered. Not a mind in any biological sense. It was the psychic footprint of something that had once possessed consciousness, or perhaps an entity that fed directly on thought itself. An echo. A complex, self-replicating pattern residing in the void between memory and absence. It felt like standing on the lip of an endless drop into an icy ocean. Hungry, ancient, and terrifyingly still. It consumed the ambient mana around it, creating a localized void.

He snapped the telepathic link back hard before the entity could trace the strand back to his brain.

He opened his eyes. A sharp, cold smile touched his mouth—devoid of humor.

"Something occupies the pass," Banki said softly over the fire’s crackle. "Not a ghost. An anomaly that leaves a massive psychic imprint while lacking a readable brain. A localized void-predator."

Kestrel’s jaw tightened. She knew that smile. The optional route had just become mandatory.

"I’ll prep the gear," Kestrel said, sliding her final claw into its mount with a hard click. "Heavy flares and triple salt-lines on all transport cradles."

"Do it. We move at dawn."

Banki looked past the camp boundary into the black maw of the peaks. The darkness waited—ancient, cold, and lethal. It held no terror for him. It was merely unharvested stock, another asset to be cataloged, bound, and eventually turned against the Crown.
