# Blackwater Quay — The Complete Omnibus

## The Lantern That Did Not Cast Light

He wore a man’s face when it pleased him. A pleasant one, often enough. Not handsome in the bright heroic fashion of songs, but sharp with the sort of competence that made clerks answer carefully and liars feel the room turn colder. Yet the face was a door only. What waited behind it was will, and behind will there was hunger. Not only for meat, or gold, or victory. Hunger for structure. For leverage. For the secret joints by which a town could be made to kneel without ever knowing whose hand pressed on the back of its neck.

Sable understood this early. Mara understood it later, and more dangerously, because she understood what might be built with such a man if one survived standing near him. Others learned slower. Some did not live long enough to learn at all.

So the story began, not in a court or a throne-room, but on a road with a broken axle, an anxious driver, a guard too eager by half, and a wind that smelled of wet dirt and old iron.

Greybridge first opened its throat there.

## The Shape of a Lie

The first lie Banki ever told was about his own skin.

Banki learned the first useful lie while he was still small enough for a crow to swallow.

He did not scream for the colony. He studied the black beak, the angle of the branch, the way hunger chose targets. The lesson arrived before language had finished arranging itself in his skull: survival did not belong to the faster thing. It belonged to the thing that could become unrecognizable.

Years later, when shining men came to cleanse the forest, he learned the second lesson. They killed the plainly dangerous fey first, then kept going. Once purity had a definition, everything outside it became contamination.

Banki watched from the canopy until he understood the shape of their mercy.

Good people were the most dangerous people in the world.

The paladin died first. Banki was fourteen. He left the forest the next day with doctrine in place of childhood: strike the healer, blind the detector, break the leader, and wear whatever skin the world required.

Now he wore a human shape the way other men wore cloaks: habitually, comfortably, with the absent ease of long practice that made the garment disappear into the wearer. A man of middling height and unremarkable build, somewhere in his mid-thirties if you judged by the lines beginning to etch themselves around grey-green eyes that held precisely the right amount of weariness for a sellsword between engagements.

Beneath the illusion—beneath the polymorph's cunning reshaping of flesh and bone and proportion—there was something altogether different. Something with wings like stained glass and a spine like a needle and a mind that burned with a fire that had nothing to do with warmth. A pixie of the old forest, ancient by the standards of mortal reckoning, carrying in his hazelnut-sized skull more accumulated knowledge, more tactical doctrine, more raw magical potency than most archmages achieved in a lifetime of dedicated study.

But the world did not need to know that. The world needed to see a man walking a dirt road in late afternoon, with cold wind in his face and nothing in his pockets worth stealing.

The world needed to see a lie.

And Banki had always been extraordinarily generous with those.

The road to Greybridge was the kind of road that existed because someone, centuries ago, had needed to get from one miserable place to another slightly less miserable place, and had worn a track through the hills by sheer bloody-minded repetition.

Banki approved of it.

Not of the road itself—he had walked better surfaces in sewers—but of its honesty. Every road told you something about the people who built it. A paved road said: we are civilised, we trade, we maintain. A neglected road said: we exist, but only barely, and we do not expect you to visit. The Greybridge road said something more specific, something that Banki's long experience with the underbellies of a hundred settlements had taught him to read like a sailor reads weather: This road says no one is watching. This road says that whatever happens on it stays between the participants and the crows.

He had been walking for three hours.

In the invisible architecture of perception he kept around himself, the world was crowded. Mind-Sense found thoughts; Aether-Sight found the forces shaping them. Together they did not give him truth. They gave him raw materials, and Banki was very good at assembly.

The road ahead had been empty for the last mile. Then, over the next rise, three minds suddenly appeared like candles lit in sequence.

One nervous and bright with pain. One sharp and self-satisfied. One controlled and cold and watching.

And then, a moment later, three more—dimmer, further away, moving fast through the brush to the southeast, carrying something heavy.

Six minds. Three on the road, three in the scrub. An arrangement that Banki recognised the way a locksmith recognises a pin-tumbler: by its internal logic, by the way the pieces fit, by what the arrangement was designed to achieve.

Someone had broken a wagon on the road ahead. Someone was using a guard to control the scene. And someone else—three someones—had used the distraction to slip away with whatever was worth stealing.

Banki's lips didn't move. His stride didn't change. His eyes—grey-green, steady, belonging to a man who had absolutely nothing to do with any of this—swept the road ahead with the bored attention of a traveller who had seen too many overturned wagons to find them interesting.

But behind those eyes, behind the mask, behind the lie, something ancient and precise and utterly without mercy began to calculate the Divine Calculus.

## Cold Wind and Bad Intent

The road into Greybridge lay under a cold sky and a mean wind.

Banki did not step out onto the road.

He crouched instead among scrub and winter grass and let his mind go long and thin. Thought reached outward first, tasting. Then sight, then instinct. Fresh tracks bit away into the brush. More than one set. The axle had been struck, not worn. The guard was not improvising outrage. He was keeping time. Under Banki's left shoulder, where old restraints had once taught muscle to expect metal, a phantom band tightened and vanished.

The wagon was a wreck, and it was a lie.

Banki saw both truths in the same instant, the way a man who has trained himself to see in the dark eventually stops distinguishing between sight and intuition. The vehicle was canted at a sick angle across the road, its left rear wheel snapped clean from the axle with the splintered ends still pale and raw—fresh wood, fresh break, the colour of exposed bone. Three figures stood around it in the arrangement of a morality play: the Victim, the Authority, and the Witness, each playing their role with the conviction of people who did not realise they were performing.

The Victim was a driver.

The Authority was a guard. Town militia by the look of his tabard—a faded blue thing with the Greybridge crest stitched in thread that had once been silver and was now the colour of old dishwater.

The Witness was a cloaked traveller.

Sixty feet separated Banki from the scene. He stood in scrub brush that rose to his illusory human thighs and watched with the absolute stillness of a predator still deciding whether the day deserved blood.

The wind cut across the hillside and pulled at his shadow-stitched cloak, and the cloak drank the motion the way it drank light: silently, completely. Enchantment woven into every thread with marrow runes and bone-dust by the hands of an industrial mage-craftsman who had been dead for two centuries and whose ghost, if it existed, would have been horrified to learn what use its finest work was being put to.

He assessed with more than eyes.

Mind-Sense found the driver's panic first, sharp as glass underfoot. The guard's mind ticked beside it, clockwork control trying to pass for outrage. The traveller was different: a locked door with a pulse, trained enough to hide fear without quite erasing it. Worth watching. Worth preserving, if possible.

Beyond them, three minds moved through the brush with stolen weight and badly managed urgency: the carrier, the watcher, the fist. Banki tasted the arrangement before he named it.

The axle break was fresh and mundane. No spell. No accident. Someone had built the stage by hand.

Someone had designed this scene the way a playwright designs a stage: the wagon to block the road, the guard to control the narrative, the driver as a prop, and the traveller as either an unexpected complication or a secondary target. And while everyone stared at the stage, the real actors had slipped behind the curtain with whatever the performance was designed to conceal.

Banki remained still. The brush rustled around him in the wind, and his cloak moved with it, and for all the world he was a shadow caught on a thorn, nothing more.

He considered walking on. Safe. Simple. Useless.

Six minds meant six sources of information, and someone worth knowing had arranged the scene. Complication, properly harvested, was leverage before anyone else knew a game had begun.

The decision arrived with the mechanical click of the Divine Calculus.

He would involve himself. He would take control. When he walked into Greybridge, he would carry a piece of every player on this road before any of them knew the game had changed.

The three minds in the brush were heading towards a bend in the road. The guard's thoughts had just flickered towards a signal there.

Banki could reach the bend first.

He began to move.

## The Choir of Frightened Minds

The spell was a whisper and a breath. Most spellcasters made magic theatrical. Banki made it disappear. One small movement of his fingers became a man adjusting his grip on a walking stick, and the working slipped into the gesture like poison into wine.

Mind-Sense intensified.

No one noticed. The guard continued his performance. The driver continued his suffering. The traveller continued their terrified, disciplined stillness. And none of them felt the invisible hand that had reached into their skulls and begun turning pages.

Banki held his position in the scrub and listened.

The guard's thoughts were the richest vein. Surface thoughts first—the ceaseless, unguarded monologue:

*...keep him talking... blame sticks if I keep the crowd framed... where are they... don't let the cloaked one bolt... Captain wants the "goods" quiet...*

Captain. Not boss, not chief: a title with command weight behind it.

And "goods." Not loot or haul. Goods, with the bureaucratic neutrality of someone trained to think of stolen property as inventory. This guard was a functionary, which meant the ambush had machinery behind it.

The driver's surface thoughts were simpler and infinitely more painful:

*...I told them I didn't have it—gods, my jaw—why would they hit me—just let me go—please don't take the wagon...*

The driver worried one loose tooth with his tongue and tried not to whimper. Banki filed the pain completely, without sentiment: collateral damage, non-combatant, no operational value.

The traveller's surface thoughts were the most interesting:

*...prints too fresh... three of them... don't look at the break—look at the guard's hands... if I move now, I'm next... where's my focus—stay calm...*

The traveller was reading the scene. Not by magic, but by hard experience: the hands, the fresh break, the wrong stillness around the guard. Useful mind. Dangerous mind, if left unshepherded.

Competent. Disciplined. Potentially useful.

Banki made his next decision with the same instant, mechanical precision that had characterised the first.

He would contact the traveller.

Better to give them something to hold onto. A plan. A direction. A reason to stay still and play calm while Banki repositioned.

And then he would go to the bend. And he would be there first. And when the guard's reinforcements arrived, they would discover that the script had been rewritten by an author whose handwriting they couldn't read.

Telepathic contact was different from Mind-Sense the way a sniper's rifle is different from a surveillance camera.

Banki threaded the thought outward with the precision of a surgeon threading a needle through a patient's cornea.

The message was simple, because simple messages are the ones that survive the shock of first contact with a frightened mind:

*I am anchoring your mind psionically so you can fight without restraint. The guard is stalling for partners and goods. Three sets of fresh prints in the brush. I will take the flank; you hold the center.*

He felt the message arrive. The traveller's mind experienced a silent cascade of shock and recalibration—a half-second of startle that lit up Banki's telepathic field like lightning illuminating a landscape: the jagged peaks of terror, the deep valleys of incomprehension, and then, rising from both like a fortress built on a cliff face, the ironclad discipline of someone who had been surprised before and had learned that the first second after a surprise is the one that kills you.

The panic did not disappear. It found posture. The traveller's hand twitched once beneath the cloak, then stilled; a breath caught, stopped, and returned under discipline.

*...not guard... help... okay... breathe... don't look for the source... just stand still and don't die...*

Good.

Outwardly, the traveller did nothing.

Banki allowed himself one breath of professional appreciation. The traveller had received terror and turned it into stillness. Useful.

He sent one more thought, brief and final:

*When you hear violence at the bend, take the guard. Seize the narrative publicly. Make the driver your witness.*

The traveller's response was a single, tight pulse of understanding. Not words. Not even images. Just the clean, compressed acknowledgment of a peer who has received tactical data and intends to exploit it: *understood.*

Banki withdrew the contact and began to move.

Movement was the most dangerous moment.

Stealth was easy when you were still. Motion made bargains with gravel, cloth, air, and bad luck. Banki paid each one in silence.

He moved through the brush like water through a crack in a dam — slow, purposeful, and absolutely silent.

Forty feet of brush. Less than a minute of movement. An eternity measured in heartbeats and the spaces between them.

He counted the guard's attention cycles—the rhythmic sweeps of the man's eyes, the four-second intervals between scans, the slight hesitation at the far end of each sweep where the guard's gaze lingered on the treeline before swinging back. Four seconds of opportunity in every cycle. Four seconds in which Banki could advance without his position crossing the guard's focal zone. Four seconds, repeated, exploited, each one carrying him closer to the bend with the accumulated inevitability of drops of water wearing a groove in stone.

No one shouted. No one saw. No one felt the displacement of a hundred and eighty pounds of disguised fey predator gliding through their peripheral vision like a thought they forgot to think.

The bend was exactly what the guard's mind had shown him.

A shallow cut in the terrain where the road dipped and curved around a low rise, creating a natural blind spot roughly twenty feet wide. On one side, a low rock outcrop broke through the earth like the spine of a buried giant, offering waist-high cover behind grey stone already furred with lichen that had been growing since before Banki was born—which, given his age, meant that the lichen was very, very old indeed.

And between the rock and the thorns, a clear approach lane. Twenty feet of open road where anyone coming around the bend would be exposed, committed, and unable to retreat without turning their back on whatever was waiting.

A kill lane. Not geography by itself, but geography taught what it was for by a mind willing to wait at the far end.

Banki settled behind the rock outcrop and waited.

Through the sustained field of Mind-Sense, he could feel the approaching minds.

Close now. Very close.

Banki placed his hands flat on the stone in front of him and felt the rock's cold solidity against his palms—his illusory palms, the lie's palms—and selected his opening move from a repertoire of violence that most people would have found excessive and that Banki considered merely thorough.

His heartbeat was steady. His breathing was controlled. His mind was a still, dark lake in which everything was reflected and nothing was disturbed.

He heard boots on packed dirt.

And he smiled.

## The Kill Lane

They came around the bend like men who believed the road was theirs.

There were three of them, as the boot prints had promised, as the minds had confirmed. The first was lean and taut, a man built like a bowstring—all tension and no spare flesh—wearing a patched leather coat that had seen better decades and carrying a shortbow with the casual readiness of someone who drew and fired the way other men scratched itches.

The second was bigger. Broader.

The third was behind both of them, struggling.

Twenty-five feet. Twenty. Closing.

Banki watched them enter the kill lane the way a spider watches a fly find the web.

He had chosen his spell in the heartbeat before they appeared.

Screaming Skull could finish. This moment needed a cage.

Evard's Black Tentacles, an industrialized High Magic protocol.

The spell lived in his marrow runes as much as his memory. He did not so much cast it as open an industrialized vent the world should have locked.

No one was watching. But Banki believed in redundancy the way he believed in gravity: not as an ideal but as a law, and one he violated at his peril.

The spell released.

The ground erupted.

Quietly, which made the horror worse.

There was no explosion, no crack of displaced earth, no thunderclap of arcane detonation that would have given the approaching men even a half-second of warning. The spell manifested with the organic, terrible silence of something living emerging from soil where nothing living had any right to be—the way mushrooms appear overnight on a corpse, the way roots split stone without sound, the way the deep things of the earth move when they are called by a will that speaks their language.

black tentacles, slick with arcane slag and smelling of burnt ozone, burst from the packed dirt of the road.

They were black as the bottom of a well is black, as the space between stars is black, as the inside of a coffin is black when the lid closes and the mourners walk away. Not a colour but an absence, leaking arcane slag. A negation. The visual equivalent of silence, smelling of ozone and bone-dust, if silence could reach out and grab you by the throat.

They filled the lane in a wet black forest, thick as a man's thigh at the root, blind tips questing through dust and boot leather. The road smelled suddenly of old stone and deep water.

The bowman caught the worst of it.

He was in the lead, moving fast, and three tentacles found him simultaneously in the first half-second of the spell's manifestation. One wrapped around his waist with the casual, unhurried strength of a python that has decided this particular warm thing will be dinner.

His shortbow clattered to the ground. His mouth opened. What came out was not a scream—the tentacles had compressed his thorax too rapidly and too completely for his lungs to produce enough air pressure to generate a proper scream. What came out was a wheeze. A wet, whistling exhalation that carried all the terror of a scream without any of its volume, like a shout trapped behind glass.

Then the tentacles constricted, and the sound that came from his chest was not human at all.

The carrier went down next.

The carrier went down hard. Face, gravel, teeth, blood. The burden rolled from his arms and lay in the road while the tentacles found his legs and taught him the ground had changed sides.

The bruiser was luckier. Or tougher. Or both.

He was at the edge of the spell's area of effect when the tentacles erupted, and his first step into the kill lane was met by a single tentacle lashing across his shins—not the three-pronged embrace that had caught the bowman, but a single, glancing blow that would have felled a smaller man but that the bruiser powered through on sheer mass and momentum. He stumbled.

He staggered forward out of the worst of it. Bloodied. Staggering. But standing.

Standing, and looking for something to hit.

The bruiser's eyes went wide and wild. His cudgel searched the air for a target hidden behind stone, shadow, and enchantment.

The bowman tried to scream again. The tentacles took the air before it became sound. Something cracked; something softer answered; then even panic had no room left to speak.

Time to introduce himself.

Banki raised his right hand above the stone outcrop.

Screaming Skull.

The bolt of bone-dust was no longer merely a weapon. Banki had refined it into a thesis of horrific High Magic force, in the Screaming Skull form, unmistakably his.

He aimed at the bowman.

The Screaming Skull launched.

The Screaming Skull crossed the lane faster than breath. It hit the bowman's already-compressed chest and turned force into instruction.

The bowman's chest was already under thousands of pounds of tentacle pressure.

The bowman's sternum fractured.

*...no...*

For one useless instant the bowman was a boy in a practice yard, trying to be quick enough to matter. Then the thought went out.

Banki was already sighting his second target.

Down by the wagon, the cloaked traveller executed.

Whether by plan or by instinct—and Banki suspected instinct refined by experience, which was the best kind of plan—the traveller chose the moment of the tentacles' eruption to act.

The traveller took the half-second of distraction and wielded it like a blade.

Their voice rose. Not a shout—shouts can be dismissed, shouts can be talked over, shouts belong to the hysterical and the helpless and the guard was trained to control both. This was a declaration, pitched at the precise volume and register that cuts through chaos the way a knife cuts through rope: loud enough to be heard, calm enough to be believed, certain enough to be contagious.

"HE DID THIS! The guard—he set this up!"

The words hit the scene like a stone hitting the surface of a pond. Ripples everywhere. The driver flinched so hard that his entire body recoiled from the accusation, not because it scared him but because it validated something he had already suspected and had been too terrified to say aloud. The guard whipped his head back towards the traveller, and his mask slipped.

The guard's official face fell away. Beneath it: rage, calculation, and the awful speed of a man deciding whether to double down on the lie or run.

His surface thoughts confirmed it. Banki caught them like sparks from a bonfire:

*...how did they—who told—control this, CONTROL this—Captain's going to—*

The guard chose the lie.

"LIES. You're with them!"

But the accusation had no teeth. The traveller's words had planted a seed, and the driver—the beaten, terrified, desperate driver—was looking at the guard with new eyes. Not brave eyes. Not defiant eyes. Just the eyes of a man who has been told that the wall he's been leaning on is actually a door, and behind it is a room full of things he was not supposed to see.

The guard was losing control of the scene. He could feel it slipping away from him like sand through wet fingers, and his thoughts were a wildfire of tactical revision:

*...contain, redirect, signal NOW—*

He began moving towards the bend. Towards Banki's position. His hand drifted towards something at his belt—a whistle, a signalling device—Banki sent a second Screaming Skull.

Not at the guard. Not yet. At the bruiser, who had extricated himself from the edge of the tentacle field and was now standing in the open, wounded and furious and making the fatal mistake of searching for his attacker in every direction except the right one.

The Screaming Skull of force crossed the distance before the bruiser could finish understanding danger. It struck his shoulder, burst armour at the seams, and painted the cold air red.

The bruiser dropped to one knee. His cudgel hit the ground beside him. His thoughts, which had been a chaos of aggression and confusion, suddenly clarified into a single, crystalline realisation:

*...I am outmatched and I am going to die here unless I stop trying to fight and start trying to survive...*

It was, Banki noted with clinical appreciation, the first intelligent thought the man had had all day.

The guard was twelve feet from the bend now. Running. Hand closing on the whistle.

Banki shifted his aim, and this time, the Screaming Skull found a target that mattered.

## What the Guard Knew

The Screaming Skull caught the guard between the shoulder blades.

He was mid-stride when it hit—one foot forward, the other leaving the ground, his body committed to the momentum of a run that his spine was about to inform him was no longer structurally advisable. The blast struck with the sound of a wet slap amplified to the volume of a thunderclap, and the force of the impact did what force always does when it encounters a body in motion: it added its vector to the existing momentum, accelerating the guard's forward progress while simultaneously destroying his capacity to manage it.

His feet left the ground. Not gracefully. Not the way a man leaves the ground when he jumps, with purpose and control and the intention of returning to it on his own terms. This was the way a man leaves the ground when the ground has been rendered irrelevant by an external force that doesn't care about his relationship with it. He flew forward—three feet, four feet—arms windmilling, the whistle spinning away from his fingers in a tumbling arc that caught the grey light and turned it briefly silver before the small metal cylinder vanished into the scrub with a sound like a coin dropped into a well.

He hit the road face-first.

He tried to rise. Made it to his hands and knees. His thoughts were a shrieking carousel of pain and fury and denial:

*...what hit—magic—who—can't—Captain, Captain, CAPTAIN...*

Banki stepped out from behind the rock.

He did not emerge dramatically. He did not pose, or announce himself, or deliver a prepared speech. He simply appeared—one moment the road was empty, the next there was a man standing on it, a man of middling height and unremarkable build wearing a traveller's cloak that seemed to drink the light, with eyes the colour of cold river water and a hand that still crackled with the fading residue of eldritch force.

"Stay down," Banki said.

His voice was quiet.

The guard stayed down.

Banki walked to where the guard lay and stood over him with the casual, terrifying calm of an accountant reviewing a spreadsheet.

"Captain Veller," he said.

The guard's eyes went wide. Not the performative shock of a guilty man pretending to be surprised—genuine, raw, animal terror. The kind that comes from hearing a stranger speak a name you believed was buried so deep that not even your own reflection in the mirror would dare to whisper it.

*...how does he—no one knows—who is this—HOW DOES HE KNOW—*

"The south culvert," Banki continued. "The Greybridge tollhouse ledger."

Each name landed like a separate blow. The guard flinched from each one as if the words themselves were physical impacts, and in a way they were: each secret spoken aloud was a demolition charge placed against the walls of the guard's composure, and Banki was detonating them in sequence, each explosion opening a breach through which the next could enter. And as the walls fell, the information behind them flooded into Banki's telepathic field with the uncontrollable urgency of water through a dam breach.

Captain Veller. Guard captain. Greybridge garrison. The operation. The ledger. The culvert. The names, the routes, the schedules, the bribes, the threats, the bodies buried in foundations that would never be dug up because the men who poured the concrete were the same men who had put the bodies there.

Banki took all of it. Every name, every route, every fear. He filed it in the vast, dark archive behind his amber eyes and felt the familiar, cold satisfaction of acquisition—the dragon's pleasure of adding to the hoard.

When he was done, the guard was weeping.

Banki looked down at the tears and felt nothing in particular about them.

The traveller reached the bend four minutes later.

By then, the road was a battlefield's aftermath rendered in the grey palette of a cold afternoon. The tentacles had dissipated—Banki had dropped concentration, the spell unraveling back into the earth like dark water returning to a dark well—and what remained was the wreckage of lives that had been rearranged by forces they had never anticipated.

Banki had bound all three.

The traveller stopped ten feet away and pushed back their hood.

A woman.

She was armed. Not obviously—no sword at her hip, no bow across her back—but Banki's Aether-Sight had already catalogued the faint abjuration ward woven into her cloak (protection, minor), the subtle transmutation aura around her boots (speed, minor), and the thin evocation thread running through a ring on her right hand that he recognised as a last-resort weapon: a stored spell, single-use, intended to be discharged in the moment between recognising lethal danger and being killed by it.

She studied him with a professional stillness he respected. The ward in her cloak, the stored spell in her ring, the way her weight stayed ready: all of it said she had survived men who mistook a quiet woman for available prey.

She was categorising Banki. He could feel it in her thoughts, which he was still reading because he always read thoughts because information was oxygen and he did not hold his breath when air was freely available.

"You anchored me," she said. "In my head. You provided an invisible safety net."

"Yes," Banki said. He did not elaborate. Elaboration was a gift, and he had not yet decided whether this peer required such gifts.

A pause. She studied the bound men. Studied the road. Studied the pattern of violence that had been written on the landscape in blood and tentacle-marks and the craters where eldritch force had met human bodies and won.

"What are you?" she asked.

"A traveller," Banki replied. The lie came with the ease of breathing. "One who happened to have the right skills at the right time."

Her eyes narrowed. Not with suspicion—with calculation. She was performing the same kind of tactical assessment that Banki had performed on her, and she was arriving at a similar conclusion: this person is extraordinarily dangerous, this person was useful, and the ratio between those two facts will determine whether I live or die.

"My name is Sable," she said. "And I owe you a debt I did not ask to incur."

"Then we understand each other perfectly," Banki said. "Unsolicited debts are the foundation of the Asmodean Pact and every useful relationship."

He smiled. It was the smile of a man who has just acquired something valuable and is already calculating the interest.

Somewhere behind them, the bowman stopped breathing.

Neither of them looked.

## The Captain's Shadow

Banki did not interrogate the guard.

Interrogation implied questions, resistance, and a subject with choices left to spend. What Banki performed was a reading. He set one hand on the bound man's chest, pushed Mind-Sense past the surface panic, and opened the lower rooms of the man's memory.

The guard broke in silence.

Names came first. Routes followed. Bribes, drop points, ferry marks, culvert doors, cargo schedules, and the fear at the centre of all of it: Captain Veller, Greybridge garrison, tollhouse ledger, south culvert, patron above.

Sable watched the guard weep into his gag.

The guard would remember the pressure later only as weather inside his chest. One of the few mercies Banki left him.

Sable did not look away from the guard while he shook. She had seen men brutalised before; Blackwater Quay had never lacked for that kind of education. This was different. The body remained mostly whole while the room inside it had been searched, labelled, and left with drawers open.

"He will remember enough to be afraid," she said.

"Good."

"Afraid people run."
