"Then we give him somewhere useful to run."

The network arranged itself behind Banki's eyes, a sprawling map of complicity and commerce.

The tollhouse served as the hinge. Through it moved money, protection, smuggled goods, and people who had become cargo once enough respectable officials learned to spell profit with clean hands. Shellbreak Cove took payments through a chapel pew. Greybridge merchants washed coin through suppliers existing only on paper. Veller's confidence stemmed from a source much higher than a town guard captain should be able to reach.

"The driver goes free," Banki instructed. "He remains a tool, a mere participant."

"And these three?" Sable gestured toward the bound men.

"Assets. For now."

She fixed a hard stare on the guard. "He knows you violated his mind."

"Yes."

"That matters."

Banki turned towards her, the road wind moving through the tall grass between them. "It matters operationally. Shame makes poor witnesses."

"That is exactly my point."

The guard's awareness made him inconvenient.

They carried that knowledge into Greybridge by dusk.

The town had been built by optimists and maintained by pessimists. Wide streets narrowed under decades of neglect. Stonework that once promised civic pride wore thick coats of mildew and soot. At the East Gate, Veller's lookout leaned against a wall, his attention drifting toward the taverns.

Banki altered his height, shifting his hair color and the drape of his cloak before crossing the threshold. Sable entered later from another angle. The lookout’s gaze slid past them both, a mercy born of incompetence rather than illusion.

They convened in a square where the fountain had died years ago.

"The tollhouse," Banki murmured. "North side of the bridge. The real ledger sits beneath the counter. Mechanical lock. Minor ward."

"Tonight?"

"After midnight."

Sable adjusted her collar. "Then make it quiet."

A thin smile touched Banki's lips. Silence remained his sharpest tool.

At the Broken Wheel, they took adjacent rooms. Banki sifted through the guard's stolen memories while Sable mapped exits, rooflines, and the innkeeper's level of curiosity. Various routes wove through the night, but one path led to a cabinet Veller believed safe. The corrupt often confused secrecy with ownership.

At midnight, Banki slipped out through the window.

The tollhouse keeper snored softly over empty bottles. The roof possessed a gap no adult would consider and no pixie could resist. Banki flowed inside in his true form, wings folded tight against his spine, and descended to the cabinet beneath the counter.

A standard ward sealed the ordinary iron lock.

He left the door closed. Pressing his mind against the internal mechanisms, he forced the bolt aside, grinding a sliver of space to pass through without waking the arcane alarm. The effort drew a sharp, metallic sting across his temples, a small price for the intrusion.

Inside waited papers, wax seals, and the leather-bound journal that mattered.

The ledger.

He absorbed every page, taking nothing but knowledge.

Veller's routes ran deeper than the guard had realized. Three adjacent smuggling networks. Monthly payments. Coded protection. The patron's mark repeated often enough to form a signature, allowing Banki to compare it against his sprawling mental archive.

Lord Castellan Aldren Prost.

The regional governor's chief of staff.

The man possessed the authority to redirect investigations, reassign guards, and force the law to defend the crimes it had been written to punish.

Banki closed the ledger, re-engaged the bolt with a telekinetic nudge, and left the ward sleeping undisturbed.

Back at the inn, he sat on the edge of the bed in total darkness until the mental map ceased its chaotic shifting and solidified into actionable leverage. A single knock rapped through the plaster wall.

"Alive?" Sable asked.

"Productive."

He considered layering a lie over his exhaustion, but chose utility instead. "No alarms. No bodies."

After a long pause, she tapped the wall once. Acknowledged.

Through the thin plaster, Sable's breathing remained too even, too shallow for sleep. He knew she kept a knife under her pillow, hoarding questions she did not yet trust herself to ask. A rustle of blankets betrayed her shifting weight. Alive did not mean unhurt, and the absence of bodies did not mean no one had been damaged. He filed her guarded vigilance for later.

Banki lay back, studying the shadowed ceiling. He possessed the patron's name, Veller's routes, and the first useful shape of a regional machine.

At dawn, he would begin deciding which parts of the machine to buy, which parts to break, and which parts to wear.

### The Checkpoint

The approach to Eastgate-on-Mere smelled of river muck, woodsmoke, and the sharp copper tang of too many people crowded behind stone walls. Ahead, the checkpoint gate loomed as a choke point of lanterns and spiked iron. Carts and wagons bottlenecked under the arches, oxen lowing in the fog.

Banki sat beside Kreel on the driver’s bench. The wagon shifted, its suspension groaning under the weight of covered cages and sealed jars. Inside those tarps rested a fortune in illegal and regulated blink-fauna.

Kreel eased the reins. The wagon slowed to a crawl. "They're thorough tonight," the driver muttered, his gaze fixed on the gate guards pulling apart a peddler’s cart.

The hum of the mind-space—the telepathic web Banki always kept spun out around him—caught the thoughts of the gate guards before the wagon even rolled to a halt. He let his focus widen, catching the psychic runoff of the men in tabards.

*Do it by the book. Log the wagon. Don’t spook the horses.* That was the sergeant, leaning against a halberd, irritated and tired.

*Permits. Manifests. Look for mismatches. If it’s specimen work, verify seals. Try to find a reason to squeeze.* That was the clerk, a pale man with ink-stained fingers and greedy eyes.

Not opportunistic bandits. Bureaucrats. Far more dangerous.

"We play this straight," Banki murmured. "Mara."

The girl slid off the back of the wagon before it fully stopped, smoothing the front of her simple merchant-apprentice tunic. She carried a leather folio thick enough to stop an arrow.

The clerk—Hask, according to the bronze tag on his chest—stepped up, raising a hand. "Hold. Manifest and cargo declaration. We’re doing full inspections for contraband. Illithid scare on the Low Wharf means command wants every crate cracked."

Hask’s mind flickered with the anticipation of finding something wrong. A broken seal. An unlisted cage. A reason to demand thirty gold to look the other way.

Mara didn't give him a single inch of breathing room. She stepped forward, opening the folio, her voice a relentless, practiced drone. "Of course. Here are our receipts from Greyfen Cross, Sablemarch Gate, and Harrowmile. Here’s our stamped tariff class, and here’s our running ledger with totals. You’ll note the third page details our sealed specimen transport bonds, pre-cleared by the Creature Gate Yard."

She began laying documents onto Hask's portable desk. Neat stacks. Stamped wax. Perfectly tallied ledgers. It was a paperwall, thick and utterly impenetrable.

Hask blinked. His mind faltered, the greed replaced by the sheer, crushing boredom of auditing a flawlessly kept ledger. He pawed at a page. "Mm. I still need to see the back of the wagon. The cages—"

Banki shifted on the bench. He let the cowl of his cloak fall back just enough to reveal calm, unblinking eyes. He didn't push into Hask's mind. He just let the weight of his presence settle over the clerk like a collapsing roof. "We’re happy to comply," Banki said, his voice flat, reasonable, and cold as a grave. "Just make sure you’re writing the inspection log correctly. I’ll wait."

Hask swallowed. The psychic pressure of a predator mimicking a merchant made the hair on his arms stand up.

The gate sergeant, already losing patience with the line of cursing teamsters behind them, stepped over. "Hask. Either inspect them or clear them. We’re not holding the gate all night for your hobby."

Hask’s irritation flared, but compliance won out. He slammed a heavy iron stamp down on Mara's top sheet. "Fine. Tariff only. Twelve gold."

Banki tossed a small leather purse down. It clinked on the wood. Hask scooped it up, not meeting his eyes, and waved them through.

The wagon rolled under the portcullis. Eastgate-on-Mere opened up before them, a sprawling, chaotic maze of opportunity and eyes.

### The Hearth & Ledger

They passed the official caravan yard—too much scrutiny, too many wardens—and ignored the cheap stable yards smelling of rot and desperate men. Banki directed Kreel toward Blackbarrow, a district of merchants who preferred quiet wealth over loud displays.

The Hearth & Ledger sat off a main thoroughfare, boasting a clean common room, private tables, and an iron-gated yard in the back. It asked for coin, not stories.

Banki paid the innkeeper nine gold for three nights, securing three rooms on the second floor. One for himself, one for Mara and Elowen, one for Sable. He carried the heavy lockbox and the Bag of Holding up the stairs himself.

Once the door of his room clicked shut, he summoned the crew.

They stood in the center of the clean, wood-paneled room. Mara clutched her folio to her chest. Elowen looked exhausted, her clothes still carrying the faint musk of the cages. Kreel stood by the door, arms crossed. Sable lounged against the window frame, a shadow pretending to be a man, his eyes flicking toward the street below.

"We have a three-day window," Banki said. His voice didn't rise above a murmur, but it commanded the room completely. "We have secure wagons, rested animals, and beds. This is a frontier city. We have money to spend, and we will do it quietly."

He met each of their eyes in turn.

"House rules. No one drinks. Loose tongues get us killed or audited, and I prefer the former. Mara, Elowen, you do not wander the streets alone. You go out in pairs, or you take Sable. Sable, you rotate between yard checks and inn presence. Nothing moves near the wagons without you knowing about it."

Sable gave a single, tight nod. "Understood."

Banki patted the heavy leather satchel slung across his chest. "The Bag of Holding stays closed in public. We are boring merchants resting between hauls. You buy cold-weather gear, spare tack, and whatever provisions we need for the Border Wilds. You act like the money we have is the only money we possess."

Mara and Elowen both exhaled, the tension bleeding out of their shoulders. The strict boundaries were a comfort. Structure meant safety.

"Get some sleep," Banki said. "I have a delivery to make."

### The Creature Gate Yard

The air in the Creature Gate Yard tasted of ozone and terrified animals. It was a sprawling, screened-in labyrinth of holding pens, appraisal tables, and heavily warded vaults.

Renn’s screened bay sat in the back, shadowed by a canvas awning.

Banki stepped in, dropping the heavy reinforced crate onto the scarred wooden table. He kept his telepathy tuned tight, a pinpoint beam focused entirely on Renn. No wandering into the yard-hands' minds.

Renn stood across the table, a jeweler's loupe strapped to his right eye, his hands stained with alchemical ink. He didn't look at the paperwork. He watched Banki's face.

*He wants the numbers fast,* Renn's surface thoughts whispered into Banki's mind. *He wants to know if this lane is real.*

No deception. Just a broker doing his job.

Renn leaned in slightly. "Break the seal. Let’s see what the mud dragged in."

Banki cracked the wax seal on the crate and folded back the padded lid. Violet light spilled out, flickering and erratic.

Sixty jars sat nested in the velvet-lined dividers. Each contained a piece of the blink-fauna ecosystem. Skitters, mostly—multi-legged things that vibrated in the fluid, occasionally popping out of existence only to reappear an inch to the left with a soft *thwip* against the glass.

Renn sucked in a breath. He pulled a sheet of heavy vellum from a drawer. "The Academy Blink Pricing Grid. A thirty-day standing lane. Read it. Then I’ll translate what the words actually mean."

Banki scanned the document. It was written by a clerk obsessed with conditions. Standard skitter jars fetched a hundred and ten gold. Premium certified, one thirty-five. Higher fauna—hoppers and newts—pushed past two hundred. Deductions for poor water quality, compromised seals, or stressed specimens.

"They're rewarding volume," Renn said, tapping a line near the bottom. "Consistency bonus. If you deliver fifty or more jars in a single submission, they add ten percent to the total payout. That is what being a supplier means."

Renn began pulling the jars out, holding them up to the lantern light, checking the seals, tapping the glass.

"Fifty-eight skitter jars," Renn tallied, his voice a low hum of calculation. "Two hopper jars. Two riftleaf newts."

"That's sixty-two," Banki noted.

"The newts are part of the sixty," Renn corrected smoothly. "We classify them under Tier Two, but they count toward the volume bonus. Let's do the conservative math."

Renn dipped a quill and scratched numbers onto a slate. The scratching filled the quiet bay.

"Fifty-eight skitters at one-ten. Six thousand, three hundred and eighty. Two hoppers at two-twenty. Four-forty. Two newts at one-eighty. Three-sixty. Base subtotal is seven thousand, one hundred and eighty gold."

Renn drew a sharp line under the number.

"Consistency bonus. Ten percent. Adds seven hundred and eighteen." He circled the final figure. "Projected Academy payout: seven thousand, eight hundred and ninety-eight gold. And that’s if none of these grade out as premium."

Nearly eight thousand gold. A staggering sum. It was the kind of money that bought armies, ruined noble houses, or in Banki’s case, paved the road to a much larger game.

Renn looked up. His mind hummed with steady, professional satisfaction. *He's going to like this. This is steady.*

"They’ll remit in two days through the usual channel," Renn said, wiping the quill clean. "I can hold it as CGY credit, or I can front it from my float and settle with the Academy later. Your call."

"Hold it as credit," Banki said. A man carrying eight thousand gold in a bag was a target. A man with an ironclad line of credit at the CGY was a power broker. "I have a few specialized purchases to make."

### The Veilbound Needle

M. Vell did not keep a storefront. He kept a cellar beneath a disused chandlery on the edge of the dockward. The air down here tasted of burned copper, dried sage, and old blood.

Banki sat in a high-backed leather chair. The desk between him and Vell was entirely bare, save for a single brass candlestick.

Vell was a thin, gray man. He looked like an under-clerk who had spent entirely too much time in the archives. But his mind was an iron fortress. Banki’s telepathic sweeps slid off Vell’s consciousness like water off oiled glass. The man was heavily warded.

"I need an infiltrator," Banki said. "A scout. Something that doesn't set off common wards and doesn't leave footprints. Something a few tiers above an imp."

Vell nodded slowly. He steepled his fingers. "No preference means you’re pragmatic. Scout and combat means you’re ambitious. Willing to pay means we can actually talk."

Vell unlocked a drawer and pulled out a thin, black-bound ledger and a sheet of vellum. He began sketching a binding circle on the paper with a charcoal pencil.

"I’ll give you the truth," Vell murmured, not looking up from his sketch. "'A few tiers above an imp' is Tier Two if you want it to stay quiet, and Tier Three if you want power with catastrophic consequences. Tier Two is the sweet spot. Real utility. Real bite. Not so loud that every interested party in the city turns their head when you use it."

"What are my options?"

"Three archetypes," Vell said. "The Ashwing Censor. Flies, sniffs out abjuration magic, can shatter minor wards. The Saltglass Sentinel. A bodyguard. Tough, camouflaged, takes a hit meant for you."

Vell paused, his pencil hovering over the paper.

"And then there is the Veilbound Needle."

"Tell me about the Needle."

Vell looked up. His eyes were entirely black in the dim light. "Infiltrator. Silent kill assist. Courier. It has a short-range blink. It can render itself invisible to mundane and minor magical sight. It possesses a venom strike that can paralyze rather than kill, if you require a breathing captive. Its stealth is... exceptional."

It was perfect. A ghost to pair with Banki’s telepathy. A surgical tool to remove problems before they ever realized they were in danger.

"I want the Needle," Banki said.

Vell nodded. He wrote a series of numbers beside the circle. "The ritual fee is three thousand, five hundred gold. Materials—circle external inks, specific salts, a lens-crystal, and anchor nails—will run one thousand, five hundred. And I require a collateral of one thousand gold, deposited into a sealed escrow sigil. It returns to you in thirty days, provided you do not breach the pact."

Six thousand gold to begin.

Banki reached into his coat, retrieving a heavy pouch of cut gems and minted platinum. He set it on the desk. It landed with a dense, heavy thud. "Timeline."

Vell didn't touch the gold. He looked at it respectfully. "Two nights to prepare the circle and acquire the specific entity through my channels. The binding ritual occurs on the third night. It will take three hours."

Vell slid a fresh sheet of paper forward. "You will sign three non-negotiable clauses. First, the Discretion Clause. No public manifestation in populated areas unless it is a matter of immediate life or death. Second, the Obedience Clause. It follows commands, but it will refuse a suicidal order or an order that breaks this contract. Third, the Release Clause. If the bond is breached, the entity returns to my circle, not loose into the world."

Vell dipped a pen in an inkwell that smelled sharply of iron.

"I am adding a fourth clause, free of charge. The Loyalty Clause. Betrayal of the master triggers binding pain and forced recall. It tightens your control."

Banki took the pen. The ink felt thick, almost warm against the parchment. He signed the name of his current cover, binding his intent to the paper.

"Three nights," Banki said, standing up.

"Three nights," Vell agreed, finally resting a hand on the pouch of gold. "Try not to die before then. The entity will be quite disappointed."

Banki walked back out into the fog of the city. He had the money. He had the crew. And in three nights, he would have an invisible killer bound to his will. 

Eastgate-on-Mere didn't know it yet, but the city belonged to him.


## The Signature That Bled

Cities pretend they are stone, but Blackwater Quay had been built on ledgers before pilings, resting entirely on the patient fiction that ink could force hunger to behave.

Banki claimed the Yard, though the ink had yet to dry. Payment remained the vulgar part. Coin crossed tables, changed purses, embarrassed honest men, and comforted dishonest ones. Ownership required witness, and witness required paper. Paper demanded the Municipal Transfer Registry, and the Registry demanded a clerk whose conscience had survived just long enough to become inconvenient.

His name was Caspel Rooke.

He was forty-six, narrow in the shoulders, careful with his cuffs, and possessed of the exhausted moral rigidity common to minor bureaucrats lacking the power to become grandly corrupt. His record of bribery amounted to two minor infractions: a widow's dock permit moved up three weeks for a feverish son, and a fishmonger's tax arrears marked paid because the man wept and Caspel remembered childhood hunger. These sins comforted him, merciful enough to be renamed.

Tonight, mercy rendered him useless.

"The Butcher's Alley compound is under investigation," Caspel announced. His registry lamp smoked between them, painting his face with yellow edges. "No transfers until the governor's office clears the title."

Sable stood by the door. Mara anchored herself near the file shelves, one hand resting on the folio that contained every legal path available should the man concede. Twenty-three paths existed. None survived a refusal entered before midnight.

An ink stain marked the clerk's second knuckle. A mild tremor shook his hand, bearing a paper-knife scar on the thumb. A pulse fluttered visibly at his throat, beating far too fast for the cool room. He was a man frightened by his visitors, not the request.

"Who ordered the hold?" Banki asked.

"The record states provisional review."

"That was not my question."

Caspel swallowed hard. "Lord Prost's office."

A wire in the machine hummed to life.

Bribery would fail. Fear had already purchased the clerk's loyalty. Threats against his family would be inefficient and vulgar.

He needed a signature.

The room narrowed.

Banki drew upon a three-fold binding spell, waking the magic without a single gesture. Ozone sharpened the stagnant air. The pressure dropped just enough for Mara to touch her ear and for the lamp flame to bow towards Banki, gravity yielding to power. The spirit's refraction split the clerk's outline into three overlapping phantoms: the body that sat at the desk, the terror struggling to flee it, and the obedience Banki prepared to stitch between them.

Caspel gasped, his hands spasming against the desk. The mind always recognized violation before it understood the method.

"Please," the clerk whispered.

Banki despised the word for how it sought to recruit witnesses.

Sable remained perfectly still. Mara's jaw tightened.

Banki opened his inner sight to the crystalline architecture of the man's soul.

Caspel's mind arranged itself in sharp, defined lines: ledger-loyalty, fear of Prost, pride in the registry, love of procedure, and a deep resentment of nobles who used clerks as shields. Beneath it all lay a buried wish, small and sour, to sign one document in his life because it was correct rather than because someone above him had leaned on his spine.

Creating obedience from nothing demanded too much brute force. Instead, Banki found the buried wish and pulled.

The spell translated the man's conscience into tactile sensations against Banki's mind: dry vellum, brittle twine, damp wool, a brass key worn smooth by generations of hands. Banki threaded compulsion through the textures with the precision of a surgeon suturing a nerve, ignoring the sharp copper ache building behind his own eyes.

Sign because the hold is improper.

Caspel whimpered.

Sign because procedure requires a clean title when the investigation concerns the former owner, not the property.

His fingers twitched toward the pen.

Sign because Lord Prost has already corrupted the process, and you have always hated corrupted process.

The clerk wept with quiet professionalism, treating his tears as another document to be filed in duplicate.

Banki pushed one layer deeper.

Erasing Caspel Rooke would be wasteful. Instead, Banki removed the man's ability to feel the wound. He altered the memory's angle, reweighted causality, softening the place where terror would have become resistance and hardening the resentment into courage. When the weaving finished, Caspel would remember being afraid, then angry, then correct. He would believe the signature had been his first honest act in years.

The pen scratched across the parchment, filling in a name, two dates, and securing three wax seals.

The Yard opened under Banki's hand.

When the final impression cooled in the wax, Caspel sat back with the empty dignity of a man who had been robbed, thanking his robber for balancing the account.

"The transfer is lawful," he whispered.

"Yes," Banki agreed softly. "It is."

Outside, rain ticked against the registry windows like fingernails on coffin wood.

Mara took the folio, her voice clipped. "We have title."

Sable remained by the door, her hand resting near her blade. "That was a person."

Banki studied the signed document, the ink still wet enough to shine.

"Tonight," he said, "he was both."

Sable's expression remained frozen, but the air in the room grew colder than the rain outside.

By dawn, wagons would move. Locks would change. Work crews would enter a compound that had belonged to a dead criminal yesterday and belonged to Banki Sovereign now, simply because ink, witness, and a violated clerk had taught the city what to call it.

## A Knife Wakes Sharp

Twelve days later, Banki woke sharp, his mind cutting straight through the morning fog.

The Yard hummed in the pre-dawn dark. Wagons loomed under dew-heavy tarps while perimeter lanterns burned low. The compound smelled of damp wood, oiled leather, and the sour metallic tang of creatures despising their cages.

He stood at the window of the temporary office—a converted loft above the primary stable block—and studied the shifting shadows below. Blackwater Quay required constant calibration. Ownership remained a hypothesis tested daily by thieves, rivals, and the city watch, who were often just thieves with better tailoring.

"The perimeter is secure," Mara stated from the desk behind him, refusing to look up from her ledger. She never lifted her eyes when a number could be bullied into compliance instead. "Sable's people caught two spotters from the Tidegate gang last night. Both were discouraged."

"Lethally?"

"Structurally. One broke an ankle falling off a roof he wasn't on. The other decided he'd rather work on a whaling ship and is currently halfway to the deep water."

Banki turned from the window. "Efficient."

"I am an accountant, Banki. Waste offends me." She closed the ledger. "We face a problem with the magistrate’s office. The noise complaints regarding the loading bays have escalated."

"Who?"

"Magistrate Vane. He is ambitious, underpaid, and clearly signaling that he wants a retainer."

"Put him on retainer."

"It sets a precedent."

"Precedents are just prices by another name," Banki countered. "Pay him. Ensure the payment arrives in a manner suggesting we could easily take it back if he becomes inconvenient."

Mara nodded, scratching a note. "I will have Renn handle it. He possesses a gift for making bribes feel like threats."

"And the recruits?"

Sable entered the office, silent as smoke, carrying a sheaf of paper smelling of cheap ink and wet stone.

"Sorted," she announced, dropping the papers onto the desk. "Seventeen viable. Five useless. Three informants."

"For whom?" Banki asked.

"Two for the city watch. One for Lord Prost."

Banki’s eyes narrowed. The ambient temperature in the room dropped a few degrees.

"Prost," he murmured. "He is watching the Yard."

"He is watching the ink dry on Caspel's signature," Sable corrected. "He knows the transfer was forced. He doesn't know how, but he knows."

"Then we let him watch," Banki said. "Give the informant something to report. Let them see a legitimate business operating with aggressive efficiency. We give them no shadows to chase, only ledgers they cannot balance."

Sable crossed her arms. "And if they don't want ledgers? If they want blood?"

"Then we ensure it is their own."

Mara tapped her pen against the wood. "I have filed the operational structure with the guild. As far as the city is concerned, we operate as a logistics and procurement firm specializing in exotic goods. It offers a broad enough category to cover most of what we do, and remains vague enough to annoy anyone attempting to tax us."

"And the physical expansion?" Banki asked.

"The adjacent lots are ready for purchase," Mara replied. "But we require more leverage to keep the price down."

"I will acquire it," Banki said.

"By coercing another clerk?" Sable asked. Her voice remained flat, devoid of judgment, but the question hung in the air like a blade on a thread.

"By understanding what the current owners value more than property," Banki replied smoothly. "Sometimes that is money. Sometimes safety. Sometimes the illusion of choice."

He walked back to the window, staring out at the Yard as the pale light of dawn bled through the fog.

"We are building a machine," he murmured, more to himself than to them. "Every person in this city acts as a gear, a spring, a lever. Most of them do not realize it. Our job is not to break them. Our job is to place them in the correct position so the machine turns the way we require."

"And if a gear doesn't fit?" Sable asked.

Banki kept his gaze on the yard. "Then we file it down until it does."

Mara closed her second ledger with a sharp snap that echoed like a gunshot. "I will draw up the acquisition papers. We move on the lots tomorrow."

"Agreed," Banki said.

The machine turned. The gears slipped into place. Blackwater Quay had bared its throat to him, and he intended to swallow it whole.

## The Assessment

The morning wore on, the fog lifting to reveal the grim, purposeful activity of the Yard. Handlers moved beasts in reinforced cages, clerks tallied manifests with frantic precision, and guards walked the perimeter with hands resting comfortably on weapon hilts. It functioned as a symphony of controlled aggression, with Banki serving as its conductor.

He spent the next three hours in the lower holds, reviewing the latest acquisitions. A brace of shadow-stalkers from the deep woods prowled their enclosure, eyes glowing like embers in the gloom. A young wyvern hissed venom that sizzled against the stone floor, its wings bound tight against its sides. A pair of basalisk eggs rested in lead crates packed with enchanted ice.

These creatures represented value, danger, and above all, leverage.

He stood before the wyvern's cage, calculating the profit margin on its venom, when his awareness snagged on a mind that didn't fit the ambient hum of the Yard.

Sharp. Tight. Counting.

*...third pin... the scored one... swap before she checks...*

Not the idle drift of a bored handler. The thoughts carried the hooked edge of rehearsal—a plan being executed, not imagined.

Banki kept his head still. His focus locked onto the source like a compass needle finding north.

Near the rigging racks beside the shadow-stalker enclosure, a handler crouched over a harness assembly. Unremarkable build. Steady hands. The surface thoughts spun in a tight, fragmented loop:

*...grease on the third coupling... scored pin goes in, good pin comes out... fingers slipping—wipe them... don't rush... the stalker pulls hard on the trial run, coupling shears, cage door swings wide... just looks like wear... just wear...*

The man's pulse thudded through his psychic signature, fast and thin. His thoughts didn't flow—they stuttered, circled back, tangled with the physical texture of the task. The rough bite of the pin against his thumb. The cold of the coupling. A desperate awareness of every footstep in the hold behind him.

*...how long has it been... someone's coming... no, just the clerk... keep going...*

Banki's witch-sight, anchored by the runes carved into his very bones, caught the rest: a faint transmutation aura clinging to the tool roll. Cheap charm-work stamped out with bone-dust—the kind any mid-sized market sold—but deliberate. A scored pin enchanted to hold just long enough to pass inspection, then shear under load.

When the shadow-stalker hit the harness at speed, the coupling would fail. The cage door would swing. A creature capable of gutting three men in the dark would pour into the holds, and in the chaos—

*...slip out the back stairs... they'll be busy with the blood...*

Banki stopped fifteen feet away. He did not speak. He stood in the damp air and let his presence fill the space between them like water rising in a sealed room—slowly, completely, drowning everything else.

The handler stiffened. The muscles in the back of his neck contracted before he even looked up. His hands slowed, stopped, and retreated from the harness with the careful withdrawal of a man who'd just touched something lethal.

His eyes came up. Found Banki's.

*...he's looking—don't think—DON'T—routine—my hands are clean—*

The mental equivalent of a man trying to hide by screaming his own location.

Banki let the silence stretch. Five seconds. Ten. The handler's thoughts spiraled into frantic, weakening loops of denial, each thinner than the last. His composure stood like a sandcastle in a rising tide—technically intact, visibly doomed.

"Show me the tools," Banki said softly. "The real ones."

The handler's hands trembled. He opened the tool roll.

There, nestled beside the legitimate pins: three scored replacements, their transmutation aura flickering in Banki's witch-sight. And beneath them, stamped into the leather of the roll itself—a dockmark. Two crossed fish-hooks over a tide line, pressed into the hide with a heated brand.

Tidegate.

The mark told him everything. The handler's fracturing mind confirmed it in parallel—jagged flashes of a rain-slick warehouse near the southern quay, a figure whose face the handler had been careful never to fully see, coin pressed into his palm with the specific weight of someone else's agenda.

He reached out and tapped the man on the chest. A gesture so casual it was devastating.

"You will go to the paymaster," Banki instructed, his voice dropping to a quiet rasp that echoed like thunder inside the handler's skull. "You will collect your wages. Then you will leave this Yard and never return. If you show your face in Blackwater Quay again, I will not kill you. I will simply remove your ability to understand language."

The handler stumbled backward, nodding frantically, and fled toward the stairs.

Banki tracked his retreat. The shadow-stalker growled behind him, a low rumble vibrating deep in its chest. He met those glowing ember eyes, acknowledging the raw, hungry intelligence behind them.

"We understand each other," he murmured.

He turned and headed back up to the office. The machine remained fragile in its early stages, requiring constant maintenance.

## The Second Ring

The office sat quiet when he returned. Sable stood by the window, gazing out over the Yard.

"You handled the saboteur," she stated without turning.

"I removed a defective gear," Banki replied, taking his seat at the desk and pulling a fresh sheet of vellum toward him.

"You let him live."

"Dead men tell no tales, and they certainly do not spread fear," Banki noted, dipping a quill in ink. "He will run back to Tidegate. He will describe what happened. They will realize their clumsy disruptions are useless, forcing them to try something more sophisticated. Or, preferably, they will give up and pay tribute."

Sable turned, her expression unreadable. "You play a long game."

"The only game worth playing," Banki replied. "Short-term gains belong to thieves and politicians. We build an institution."

He began to write, his hand moving in precise, elegant strokes.

"What is that?" Sable asked, nodding toward the vellum.

"A formal invitation," Banki said. "To Magistrate Vane. It is time we had a face-to-face conversation about his retainer."

"In person? Here?"

"No. On neutral ground. The Whispering Lamp, in the Silk District. Public enough to make him feel safe, expensive enough to flatter his ego."

"I will arrange security," Sable offered.

"Do," Banki agreed. "And Sable?"

She paused at the threshold.

"Ensure the security is highly visible. I want him to know we can reach him, but I also want him to know we choose not to. Let him feel the restraint. It often proves more persuasive than violence."

"Understood," she replied, vanishing into the corridor.

Mara stepped back into the office, carrying a finalized stack of acquisition papers. Banki finished his letter, signed it with a flourish, and set it aside to dry.

"And if I had preferred leverage?" Banki asked, referencing their earlier conversation regarding the guild filing.

"Then you would have won one frightened clerk and lost a legal category," Mara said smoothly, organizing her files. "I won the category."

The shape of her strategy crystallized in his mind. She hadn't built a favor, a bribe, or a threat. She had constructed a public word the city now had to use when speaking about him: Containment.

Mara took the notice back and filed it before he could decide whether to be angry. By noon, Caspel's signature had become a thing clerks repeated carefully. No one said coerced, and no one said clean. They simply said *filed*, a word that in Blackwater Quay often proved more dangerous than the truth.

Sable returned to the doorway, her voice calm as a closing latch. "The recruits on probation are responding. Some are eager. Some are lying. I can sort the rot out early if you give me permission."

"Sort them," Banki said. "But leave the liars intact until we know who they're lying *for*."

An old laboratory memory stirred when he noted the complex ward drawn across Mara's ledger. He chose not to dissect it. Sable's guarded mind, present and deliberately shielded, offered enough difficulty for one morning.
