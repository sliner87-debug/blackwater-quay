They carried that cold knowledge into Greybridge by dusk.

The border town had been raised by optimists three centuries ago and maintained by generations of hardened pessimists. Wide stone avenues built for grand parades had narrowed under decades of municipal rot, encroached upon by leaning timber hovels. Stonework that once promised civic pride wore thick, greasy coats of mildew, soot, and dried blood. At the East Gate, Veller's lookout leaned against a weeping granite arch, his greasy hair plastered to his forehead, his attention drifting toward the glowing windows of the nearest taproom.

Banki adjusted his form on the fly. He shifted his height by two inches, altered the texture of his hair, and darkened the drape of his marrow-rune cloak before crossing the threshold. Sable slipped through a separate turnstile fifty paces to the west. The lookout’s gaze slid past them both—a mercy born of human incompetence rather than arcane illusion.

They convened in a sunken square where a stone fountain had choked on leaves and dead dogs a decade prior.

"The tollhouse," Banki whispered, his words vanishing into the smog. "North side of the river bridge. The secondary ledger—the real one—sits in an iron cabinet beneath the counter. Mechanical lock. A minor abjuration ward on the latch."

"Tonight?" Sable asked, adjusting her collar against the soot-heavy drizzle.

"After midnight. When the guard-shift rots."

"Then make it quiet."

A thin, razor-sharp smile touched Banki's stolen lips. Silence remained his most lethal working.

At the Broken Wheel, a squalid hostelry smelling of damp straw and cheap tallow, they took adjacent rooms on the top floor. Banki sat cross-legged on the floorboards, sifting through the guard's stolen memories, while Sable mapped exits, roof-pitches, and the innkeeper's specific level of mercenary curiosity. Dozens of threads wove through the city's dark underbelly, but one clear path led straight to an iron cabinet Veller believed impregnable. The corrupt routinely confused secrecy with absolute ownership.

At midnight, Banki slipped out through his room's narrow dormer window.

The tollhouse keeper snored heavily over three empty bottles of sour apple brandy. The slate roof possessed a structural gap where a stone chimney had settled—a gap no mortal man could consider and no pixie could resist. Banki folded his human guise away, shedding his mortal weight. He slipped inside in his true, jagged form, obsidian wings tightly pressed against his needle-sharp spine, gliding down the rafters to the cabinet beneath the counter.

A standard alarm-ward sealed the heavy iron lock.

He left the iron door closed. Pressing his cold, miniature consciousness against the internal tumblers, he exerted a subtle, precise telekinetic force, grinding a sliver of space past the brass pins without triggering the dormant arcane alarm. The intrusion drew a sharp, metallic ache behind his optic nerves—a trivial tax for total access.

Inside waited parchment manifests, official wax seals, and the leather-bound journal that contained the region's true anatomy.

The ledger.

He absorbed every line, taking no physical paper, leaving only the shadow of his eyes on the ink.

Veller's operation ran far deeper than a single corrupt captain. Three interconnected smuggling syndicates. Monthly kickbacks. Systemic protection fees paid to city magistrates. The patron's seal recurred frequently enough to form an unmistakable signature—a mark Banki cross-referenced against his vast, ancient mental archive.

Lord Castellan Aldren Prost.

The regional governor's chief of staff.

The man possessed the absolute authority to quash investigations, reassign garrison regiments, and force the law to butcher anyone who attempted to enforce it.

Banki closed the ledger, re-engaged the iron tumblers with a telekinetic click, and left the alarm-ward sleeping undisturbed.

Back at the inn, he sat on the edge of the straw mattress in pitch darkness until his internal mental map ceased its chaotic shifting and solidified into actionable leverage. A single, rhythmic knock rapped through the thin plaster wall.

"Alive?" Sable's low voice called through the lathe.

"Productive." He considered wrapping his exhaustion in a lie, but chose utility instead. "No alarms triggered. No bodies left behind."

After a long pause, she tapped the plaster once. Acknowledged.

Through the thin wall, Sable's breathing remained far too shallow, far too disciplined for sleep. He knew she kept a long steel blade beneath her pillow, hoarding questions she did not yet trust herself to ask. A soft rustle of blankets betrayed her shifting weight. *Alive* did not mean unhurt, and the absence of corpses did not mean no one had been ruined. He filed her vigilant distrust away for later extraction.

Banki lay back, staring up at the shadowed ceiling rafters. He possessed the patron's name, Veller's routes, and the first functional blueprint of a regional machine.

At dawn, he would begin deciding which gears to purchase, which gears to break, and which gears to wear.

## The Checkpoint

The approach to Eastgate-on-Mere smelled of stagnant river muck, burning peat, and the sharp copper tang of too many desperate people crowded behind decaying stone fortifications. Ahead, the primary checkpoint gate loomed as a narrow choke-point of iron lanterns and rusted portcullis spikes. Carts and livestock wagons bottlenecked under the granite arches, oxen lowing in the yellow fog.

Banki sat beside Kreel on the driver’s high bench. The heavy freight wagon groaned, its leaf springs straining under the weight of canvas-covered cages and sealed glass jars. Inside those padded crates rested a fortune in illegal and heavily regulated blink-fauna.

Kreel eased the reins, his knuckles white. The draft horses slowed to a crawl. "They're thorough tonight," the teamster muttered, his eyes fixed on four gate guards ripping apart a peddler’s salt-cart.

The hum of the mind-space—the wide telepathic web Banki always kept spun out around his radius—snagged the thoughts of the gate guards long before the wagon rolled to a halt. He broadened his mental focus, catching the psychic runoff of the men in blue tabards.

*Do it by the book. Log every crate. Don’t spook the draft horses.* That was the gate sergeant, leaning heavily against a dented halberd, exhausted and irritable.

*Permits. Manifests. Look for mislabeled cargo. If it’s specimen work, check the Guild seals. Find a discrepancy to squeeze thirty silver out of them.* That was the clerk, a pale, narrow-shouldered man with ink-stained knuckles and greedy eyes.

Not bloodthirsty bandits. Bureaucrats. Far more dangerous, because their violence arrived backed by parchment and iron.

"We play this entirely straight," Banki murmured. "Mara."

The girl slid off the rear of the wagon before it came to a complete stop, smoothing the front of her plain merchant-apprentice tunic. She carried a heavy leather folio thick enough to stop a crossbow bolt.

The clerk—Hask, according to the brass nameplate pinned to his greasy doublet—stepped up, raising a gloved hand. "Hold. Manifests and cargo declarations. Complete inspection for illegal contraband. Illithid panic on the Low Wharf means command wants every crate cracked."

Hask’s mind flickered with the eager anticipation of finding a flaw. A broken seal. An unlisted cage. A reason to demand forty gold to look the other way.

Mara didn't grant him a single inch of breathing room. She stepped into his space, snapping open the folio, her voice a flat, relentless drone of bureaucratic competence. "Of course. Here are our cleared receipts from Greyfen Cross, Sablemarch Gate, and Harrowmile. Here is our stamped tariff classification, and here is our running ledger with itemized totals. You’ll note the third parchment details our sealed specimen transport bonds, pre-cleared by the Creature Gate Yard."

She began slapping documents onto Hask's portable writing desk. Flawless stacks. Stamped sealing wax. Perfectly balanced ledgers. It was a paper wall—dense, legalistic, and utterly impenetrable.

Hask blinked, his mouth opening slightly. His mind faltered, the quick surge of greed crushed under the sheer, suffocating boredom of auditing a flawlessly kept manifest. He pawed blindly at a page. "Mm. I still need to inspect the rear of the wagon. The cages—"

Banki shifted on the high bench. He let the cowl of his cloak fall back just enough to expose his calm, unblinking eyes. He didn't force his way into Hask's mind; he simply loosened his restraint, letting the cold, crushing weight of his ancient predator's presence settle over the clerk like a collapsing roof. "We are entirely happy to comply," Banki said, his voice flat, reasonable, and cold as a frozen grave. "Just ensure you are logging the formal inspection report correctly. I will wait."

Hask swallowed hard, his throat clicking. The psychic pressure of an apex predator mimicking a prosperous merchant made the hair on his arms stand up in sudden horror.

The gate sergeant, losing his remaining patience with the growing line of shouting teamsters behind them, marched over. "Hask. Either crack the crates or clear the wagon. We aren't holding the main gate all night for your paperwork."

Hask’s irritation flared, but self-preservation won out. He slammed a heavy iron seal down onto Mara's top ledger page. "Fine. Standard tariff only. Twelve gold pieces."

Banki dropped a small leather purse onto the desk. It clinked heavily. Hask snatched it up without meeting Banki's eyes and waved them through with a frantic gesture.

The wagon rolled under the iron portcullis. Eastgate-on-Mere opened before them—a sprawling, filthy maze of opportunity, crime, and watching eyes.

## The Hearth & Ledger

They passed the official caravan yards—too much municipal scrutiny, too many Guild wardens—and ignored the cheap stable yards reeking of horse-rot and desperate cutthroats. Banki directed Kreel toward Blackbarrow, a district of established merchants who preferred quiet wealth over loud displays.

The Hearth & Ledger sat off a secondary thoroughfare, boasting a clean common room, private dining alcoves, and a heavily fortified, iron-gated yard in the rear. It demanded heavy coin, not personal histories.

Banki paid the innkeeper nine gold pieces for three nights, securing three private rooms on the second floor. One for himself, one for Mara and Elowen, one for Sable. He carried the heavy iron lockbox and the enchanted Bag of Holding up the stairs himself, his spine unbending under the weight.

Once the iron latch of his room clicked home, he summoned the crew.

They assembled in the center of the clean, wood-paneled chamber. Mara clutched her folio to her chest like a breastplate. Elowen looked thoroughly spent, her clothes still bearing the musk of the beasts. Kreel stood by the door, arms folded across his thick chest. Sable lounged against the window frame—a shadow pretending to be a guest, her eyes scanning the street below for spotters.

"We have a three-day window," Banki said. His voice did not rise above a whisper, yet it commanded the room completely. "We have secure transport, rested beasts, and warm beds. This is a frontier city. We have significant coin to spend, and we will do it with ruthless quiet."

He met each pair of eyes in turn.

"House rules. No one drinks spirit-liquor. Loose tongues get us audited or murdered, and I prefer the former. Mara, Elowen, you do not walk the streets alone. You venture out in pairs, or you take Sable. Sable, you rotate between yard sweeps and inn security. Nothing approaches the wagons without your knowledge."

Sable gave a single, tight nod. "Understood."

Banki patted the heavy leather satchel slung across his chest. "The Bag of Holding stays closed in public. We are boring merchants resting between hauls. You buy winter gear, replacement tack, and provisions for the Border Wilds. You act as though the coin in your belts is the only money we possess."

Mara and Elowen exhaled in unison, the rigid tension bleeding from their shoulders. Strict boundaries offered safety. Structure meant survival.

"Get some rest," Banki said. "I have a delivery to execute."

## The Creature Gate Yard

The air in the Creature Gate Yard tasted of ozone, crushed herbs, and terrified beasts. It was a sprawling, screened-in labyrinth of holding pens, appraisal tables, and heavily warded subterranean vaults.

Renn’s screened bay sat in the far corner, shaded by a rot-resistant canvas awning.

Banki stepped into the bay, setting the heavy, reinforced crate down onto the scarred oak workbench with a dense thud. He kept his telepathy focused like a lantern beam directly on Renn, ignoring the background noise of the yard-hands outside.

Renn stood across the table, a brass loupe strapped over his right eye, his fingers stained dark with alchemical ink. He ignored the parchment manifests. He studied Banki's face.

*He wants the numbers fast,* Renn's surface thoughts whispered into Banki's mind. *He wants to know if this supply lane is real or a fluke.*

No deception. Just an appraiser doing his duty.

Renn leaned forward. "Break the wax seal. Let us see what the mud yielded."

Banki cracked the crimson wax seal on the crate and folded back the velvet-padded lid. Sickly violet light spilled out, flickering erratically against the dark wood.

Sixty glass jars sat nested in velvet dividers. Each contained a vibrant piece of the blink-fauna ecosystem. Skitters, mostly—multi-legged, translucent things that vibrated in the preserving fluid, occasionally popping out of physical existence only to reappear an inch to the left with a soft, wet *thwip* against the glass.

Renn sucked in a sharp breath. He pulled a heavy vellum sheet from a side drawer. "The Academy Blink Pricing Grid. A thirty-day standing contract. Read the terms. Then I will translate what the language actually means."

Banki scanned the vellum. Written by an archivist obsessed with purity. Standard skitter jars fetched one hundred and ten gold pieces. Premium certified specimens, one thirty-five. Higher fauna—hoppers and newts—pushed past two hundred. Heavy deductions applied for murky water, compromised seals, or stressed specimens.

"They reward volume," Renn said, tapping an ink line near the margin. "Consistency bonus. If you deliver fifty or more viable jars in a single submission, they append a ten percent volume bonus to the total payout. That is the threshold between a peddler and a true supplier."

Renn began pulling the jars out, holding each up to the gas lantern, checking the wax seals, tapping the glass with a brass rod.

"Fifty-eight skitter jars," Renn tallied, his voice a low hum of professional calculation. "Two hopper jars. Two riftleaf newts."

"That totals sixty-two," Banki noted.

"The newts are classified under Tier Two," Renn corrected smoothly, "but they count toward the volume threshold. Let us perform the conservative math."

Renn dipped a steel nib and scratched figures onto a slate. The harsh scraping filled the quiet bay.

"Fifty-eight skitters at one-ten. Six thousand, three hundred and eighty gold. Two hoppers at two-twenty. Four hundred and forty. Two newts at one-eighty. Three hundred and sixty. Base subtotal stands at seven thousand, one hundred and eighty gold pieces."

Renn drew a thick line beneath the total.

"Consistency bonus. Ten percent. Appends seven hundred and eighteen gold." He circled the final sum. "Projected Academy payout: seven thousand, eight hundred and ninety-eight gold. And that assumes none of these grade out as premium."

Nearly eight thousand gold pieces. A staggering fortune. It was the kind of coin that purchased mercenary companies, ruined noble houses, or in Banki’s case, paved the road toward a far grander game.

Renn looked up. His mind hummed with professional satisfaction. *He's going to like this. This is a real lane.*

"They will remit in two days through the standard clearinghouse," Renn said, wiping his steel nib. "I can retain it as CGY credit, or I can advance it from my float and settle with the Academy later. Your choice."

"Retain it as credit," Banki said. A man carrying eight thousand gold pieces in a sack was a target for every cutthroat in Blackwater Quay. A man with an ironclad credit line at the Creature Gate Yard was a power broker. "I have specialized acquisitions to make."

## The Veilbound Needle

M. Vell did not maintain a storefront. He occupied a subterranean cellar beneath a disused chandlery on the edge of the dockward. The damp air down here smelled of scorched copper, dried nightshade, and old, arterial blood.

Banki sat in a high-backed leather chair. The ironwood desk between him and Vell was bare, save for a single brass candlestick casting long shadows.

Vell was a narrow, grey-skinned man who resembled a clerk who had spent two centuries rotting in an archive. But his mind was an iron fortress. Banki’s telepathic sweeps slid off Vell’s consciousness like oil off wet slate. The man was protected by powerful, ancient wards.

"I require an infiltrator," Banki said flatly. "A scout. Something that bypasses standard abjuration wards and leaves no physical footprints. Something several tiers above an imp."

Vell nodded slowly, steepling his long, ink-stained fingers. "No aesthetic preference means you are pragmatic. Scout and combat capability means you are ambitious. Willingness to pay means we can actually negotiate."

Vell unlocked a brass drawer, pulling out a thin, black-bound ledger and a sheet of heavy vellum. He began drawing a binding circle on the parchment with a charcoal pencil.

"I will offer you truth," Vell murmured without looking up. "'Several tiers above an imp' means Tier Two if you require stealth, and Tier Three if you desire raw power coupled with catastrophic collateral consequences. Tier Two is the optimal fulcrum. Genuine utility. Lethal precision. Not so loud that every archmage in the district turns his head when you unbind it."

"What are my options?"

"Three archetypes," Vell said. "The Ashwing Censor. It flies, detects abjuration magic, and shatters minor wards. The Saltglass Sentinel. A bodyguard. Dense, camouflaged, designed to intercept a strike intended for your spine."

Vell paused, his pencil hovering over the vellum.

"And then there is the Veilbound Needle."

"Detail the Needle."

Vell looked up, his eyes completely black in the dim candlelight. "Infiltrator. Silent kill assist. Courier. It possesses a short-range shadow-blink. It renders itself invisible to mundane and minor magical sight. It carries a neurotoxic venom strike that paralyzes rather than kills, should you require a breathing captive. Its capacity for silent infiltration is... absolute."

It was perfect. A ghost to pair with Banki’s wide-cast telepathy. A surgical scalpel to remove threats before they ever realized they were in danger.

"I choose the Needle," Banki said.

Vell nodded once, writing figures beside the binding circle. "The ritual fee is three thousand, five hundred gold. Materials—external circle inks, rare salts, a focal crystal, and iron anchor nails—will require one thousand, five hundred. And I demand a collateral deposit of one thousand gold, placed into a sealed escrow sigil. It returns to you in thirty days, provided you do not breach the pact."

Six thousand gold pieces to begin.

Banki reached into his coat, retrieving a heavy velvet pouch filled with cut gems and minted platinum bars. He set it onto the desk, where it landed with a dense, heavy thud. "Timeline."

Vell didn't touch the gold. He looked at it with cold respect. "Two nights to prepare the circle and summon the entity through my private channels. The binding ritual occurs on the third night. It requires three hours of continuous work."

Vell slid a fresh parchment forward. "You will execute three non-negotiable clauses. First, the Discretion Clause. No public manifestation in populated districts unless your life is immediately threatened. Second, the Obedience Clause. It obeys all tactical commands, but will refuse a suicidal order or an order that breaches this contract. Third, the Release Clause. If the bond is severed by your death, the entity returns to my circle, not loose into the streets."

Vell dipped a steel nib into an inkwell smelling sharply of iron filings.

"I am appending a fourth clause, free of charge. The Loyalty Clause. Betrayal of the master triggers immediate binding agony and forced recall. It hardens your leash."

Banki took the pen. The ink felt thick, warm, almost alive against the parchment. He signed the name of his current cover, binding his intent to the vellum.

"Three nights," Banki said, standing up.

"Three nights," Vell agreed, finally resting a grey hand on the pouch of gold. "Try not to get butchered before then. The entity would be terribly disappointed."

Banki stepped back out into the sulfurous fog of the city streets. He possessed the coin. He possessed the crew. And in three nights, he would possess an invisible killer bound to his absolute will.

Eastgate-on-Mere did not know it yet, but the city was already slipping into his jaw.

## The Signature That Bled

Cities pretend they are built of stone, but Blackwater Quay had been constructed upon ledgers before pilings—resting entirely on the patient fiction that ink could force human hunger to behave.

Banki claimed the Yard, though the ink had yet to dry on the deed. Payment was merely the vulgar prerequisite. Coin crossed scarred tables, changed hands, embarrassed honest men, and comforted dishonest ones. But true ownership required witness, and witness demanded parchment. Parchment required the Municipal Transfer Registry, and the Registry demanded a clerk whose fragile conscience had survived just long enough to become inconvenient.

His name was Caspel Rooke.

He was forty-six, narrow in the shoulders, obsessively neat with his cuffs, and possessed of that exhausted moral rigidity common to minor bureaucrats who lacked the courage to become grandly corrupt. His record of bribery consisted of two minor infractions: a widow's dock permit moved up three weeks for a sick child, and a fishmonger's tax arrears marked paid because the man wept and Caspel remembered childhood starvation. Those small sins comforted him; they were merciful enough to be renamed.

Tonight, that mercy rendered him entirely useless.

"The Butcher's Alley compound is frozen under official investigation," Caspel announced, his voice raspy. His registry lamp smoked between them, painting his hollow face with yellow edges. "No title transfers until the governor's office clears the hold."

Sable stood by the iron door, a shadow. Mara anchored herself near the file shelves, her hand resting on the folio that contained every legal argument available should the man concede. Twenty-three legal paths existed. None survived an official refusal entered before midnight.

An ink stain marked the clerk's second knuckle. A mild tremor shook his right hand, bearing a faint paper-knife scar across the thumb. A rapid pulse fluttered visibly at his throat—beating far too fast for the cold room. He was a man terrified of his visitors, not the legal request.

"Who ordered the hold?" Banki asked softly.

"The registry entry states provisional review."

"That was not my question."

Caspel swallowed hard, his throat dry. "Lord Prost's office."

A cold wire inside the machine hummed to life.

Bribery would fail; fear had already purchased the clerk's loyalty to the governor's office. Violent threats against his family would be vulgar and inefficient.

Banki required a signature.

The small room narrowed.

Banki drew upon a three-fold binding spell, waking the ancient magic without a single physical gesture. Ozone sharpened the stagnant air. The ambient pressure dropped just enough for Mara to touch her ear and for the lamp flame to bow toward Banki, gravity yielding to power. The spirit's refraction split the clerk's outline into three overlapping phantoms: the mortal body sitting at the desk, the terror struggling to flee it, and the absolute obedience Banki prepared to stitch between them.

Caspel gasped, his hands spastic against the blotter. The human mind always recognized violation before it understood the mechanism.

"Please," the clerk whispered.

Banki despised the word for how it sought to recruit witnesses to its own weakness.

Sable remained frozen by the door. Mara's jaw tightened.

Banki opened his inner sight to the crystalline architecture of the man's soul.

Caspel's mind arranged itself in rigid lines: ledger-loyalty, terror of Lord Prost, pride in the registry, love of legal procedure, and a deep, simmering resentment of nobles who utilized clerks as human shields. Beneath it all lay a buried wish—small, sour, and ancient—to sign one monumental document in his life because it was correct rather than because a superior had crushed his spine.

Creating obedience out of thin air demanded too much violent force. Instead, Banki located that buried wish and pulled.

The spell translated the clerk's conscience into tactile sensations against Banki's mind: dry vellum, brittle twine, damp wool, a brass key worn smooth by three generations of hands. Banki threaded mental compulsion through those textures with the surgical precision of an anatomist suturing a nerve, ignoring the sharp, copper ache building behind his own eyes.

*Sign because the hold is legally improper.*

Caspel whimpered, a tear escaping his eye.

*Sign because standard procedure demands a clean title when the investigation targets the former owner, not the physical property.*

His steel nib twitched toward his steel nib.

*Sign because Lord Prost has already corrupted the legal process, and you have spent thirty years hating corrupted process.*

The clerk wept with quiet, professional dignity, treating his tears as merely another document to be filed in duplicate.

Banki pushed one layer deeper.

Erasing Caspel Rooke entirely would be wasteful. Instead, Banki excised the man's ability to feel the wound. He altered the memory's angle, reweighted causality, softening the exact node where terror would have matured into resistance, hardening the buried resentment into moral courage. When the mental weaving finished, Caspel would remember being terrified, then becoming righteous, then doing his duty. He would believe the signature had been his first heroic act in thirty winters.

The steel nib scratched violently across the vellum, filling in a name, two dates, and pressing three red wax seals into the paper.

The Butcher's Alley Yard opened under Banki's hand.

When the final impression cooled in the wax, Caspel sat back with the hollow dignity of a man who had been robbed, silently thanking his robber for balancing the ledger.

"The transfer is legally sound," the clerk whispered.

"Yes," Banki agreed softly. "It is."

Outside, heavy rain hit the registry windows like fingernails scratching on a coffin lid.

Mara took the signed folio, her voice clipped. "We have legal title."

Sable remained motionless by the door, her hand resting near her blade. "That was a person."

Banki studied the signed document, the black ink still wet enough to glisten in the lamplight.

"Tonight," he said, "he was both."

Sable's expression remained frozen, but the air in the room grew colder than the storm outside.

By dawn, supply wagons would move. Locks would be replaced. Work crews would enter a compound that had belonged to a executed criminal yesterday and belonged to Banki Sovereign tonight—simply because ink, witness, and a violated clerk had taught the city what to call it.

## A Knife Wakes Sharp

Twelve days later, Banki woke sharp, his mind cutting through the morning fog like a drawn razor.

The Yard hummed in the pre-dawn dark. Heavy freight wagons loomed under wet tarps while perimeter lanterns burned low. The compound stank of damp wood, oiled leather, and the sour metallic tang of exotic beasts despising their cages.

He stood at the window of the temporary office—a converted loft above the primary stable block—and evaluated the shifting shadows below. Blackwater Quay required constant calibration. Ownership remained an unproven hypothesis tested daily by thieves, rivals, and the city watch, who were merely thieves possessing better tailoring.

"The perimeter is secure," Mara stated from the desk behind him, refusing to look up from her ledger. She never lifted her eyes when a number could be bullied into compliance instead. "Sable's people intercepted two spotters from the Tidegate gang last night. Both were discouraged."

"Lethally?"

"Structurally. One broke his ankle falling off a roof he had no business climbing. The other decided he'd rather join a whaling crew and is currently three leagues out into deep water."

Banki turned from the window. "Efficient."

"I am an accountant, Banki. Waste offends my soul." She closed the leather ledger with a soft thud. "We face an operational issue with the magistrate’s office. The noise complaints regarding our night-loading bays have escalated."

"Who?"

"Magistrate Vane. He is ambitious, heavily indebted, and signaling that he desires a monthly retainer."

"Place him on retainer."

"It establishes a dangerous precedent."

"Precedents are merely prices by another name," Banki countered smoothly. "Pay him. Ensure the silver arrives in a manner suggesting we could easily cut his throat if he becomes inconvenient."

Mara nodded, scratching a note onto her slate. "I will have Renn handle the delivery. He possesses a rare talent for making bribes feel like execution notices."

"And the new recruits?"

Sable entered the office, silent as smoke, carrying a sheaf of coarse parchment smelling of cheap ink and wet stone.

"Filtered," she announced, dropping the papers onto the oak desk. "Seventeen viable. Five useless. Three informants."

"For whom?" Banki asked.

"Two for the city watch. One for Lord Prost."

Banki’s eyes narrowed. The ambient temperature in the room dropped three degrees.

"Prost," he murmured. "He is watching the Yard."

"He is watching the ink dry on Caspel's signature," Sable corrected. "He knows the transfer was forced. He doesn't know the mechanism, but he knows."

"Then we let him watch," Banki said. "Give the informant something boring to report. Let them see a legitimate logistics firm operating with aggressive efficiency. We offer them no shadows to hunt, only ledgers they cannot balance."

Sable crossed her arms. "And if they don't want ledgers? If they demand blood?"

"Then we ensure it is their own."

Mara tapped her steel pen against the wood. "I have registered our operational structure with the Commerce Guild. As far as the city is concerned, we operate as a procurement and logistics firm specializing in exotic beasts and rare minerals. It offers a broad enough category to cover our assets, while remaining sufficiently vague to frustrate anyone attempting to tax us."

"And the physical expansion?" Banki asked.

"The adjacent parcels are ready for acquisition," Mara replied. "But we require additional leverage to force the price down."

"I will acquire it," Banki said.

"By violating another clerk?" Sable asked. Her voice was flat, devoid of judgment, but the question hung in the air like a blade suspended on a hair.

"By understanding what the current owners value more than property," Banki replied smoothly. "Sometimes that is coin. Sometimes physical safety. Sometimes the comforting illusion of choice."

He stepped back to the window, staring out at the Yard as the pale light of dawn bled through the fog.

"We are constructing a machine," he whispered, more to himself than to them. "Every soul in this city functions as a gear, a spring, or a lever. Most do not realize it. Our duty is not to break them. Our duty is to position them so the machine turns as we dictate."

"And if a gear refuses to fit?" Sable asked.

Banki kept his gaze on the yard below. "Then we file it down until it does."

Mara closed her second ledger with a sharp snap that echoed like a pistol shot. "I will draft the acquisition papers. We move on the lots tomorrow."

"Agreed," Banki said.

The machine turned. The gears slipped into place. Blackwater Quay had exposed its throat to him, and he intended to swallow it whole.

## The Assessment

The morning wore on, the yellow fog lifting to reveal the grim activity of the Yard. Handlers moved beasts in reinforced cages, clerks tallied manifests with frantic speed, and guards walked the perimeter with hands resting comfortably on their sword-hilts. It functioned as a symphony of controlled aggression, with Banki serving as its silent conductor.

He spent the next three hours in the subterranean holds, inspecting the latest acquisitions. A brace of shadow-stalkers from the deep woods prowled their enclosure, eyes glowing like burning embers in the gloom. A young wyvern hissed venom that sizzled and smoked against the flagstones, its leathery wings bound tightly to its ribs. A pair of basilisk eggs rested in lead-lined crates packed with enchanted ice.

These beasts represented raw value, lethal danger, and above all, strategic leverage.

He stood before the wyvern's cage, calculating the profit margin on its venom, when his telepathic awareness snagged on a mind that did not match the ambient hum of the Yard.

Sharp. Tight. Counting seconds.

*...third coupling pin... the scored one... swap it before she checks the harness...*

Not the idle drift of a bored handler. The thoughts carried the hooked edge of rehearsal—a sabotage being executed, not imagined.

Banki kept his head motionless. His focus locked onto the target like a compass needle finding north.

Near the rigging racks beside the shadow-stalker enclosure, a handler crouched over a heavy leather harness assembly. Unremarkable build. Steady hands. His surface thoughts spun in a frantic, tight loop:

*...grease the third coupling... scored pin goes in, good pin comes out... fingers sweating—wipe them on the tunic... don't rush... the stalker pulls hard during the trial run, coupling shears under tension, cage door swings wide... looks like standard wear... just mechanical failure...*

The man's pulse thudded through his psychic signature, fast and thin. His thoughts didn't flow—they stuttered, cycled back, tangling with the physical texture of the task. The rough bite of the iron pin against his thumb. The cold of the grease. A desperate awareness of every footstep in the corridor behind him.

*...how long has it been... someone's coming... no, just the clerk... keep moving...*

Banki's witch-sight, anchored by the marrow-runes carved into his very bones, caught the rest: a faint, sickly transmutation aura clinging to the man's tool roll. Cheap charm-work stamped out with bone-dust—the kind sold in any back-alley market—but deliberate. A scored iron pin enchanted to hold just long enough to pass visual inspection, then shear under load.

When the shadow-stalker hit the harness at speed, the coupling would snap. The cage door would swing open. A beast capable of gutting five men in pitch darkness would pour into the holds, and in the resulting bloodbath—

*...slip out the rear drainage stairs... they'll be busy dragging out the dead...*

Banki stopped fifteen paces away. He did not speak. He stood in the damp air and let his presence fill the hold like cold water rising in a sealed room—slowly, completely, drowning everything else.

The handler stiffened. The muscles in the back of his neck contracted before he even turned his head. His hands slowed, stopped, and retreated from the harness with the frantic care of a man who had just touched a sleeping pit-viper.

His eyes came up. Found Banki's grey-green gaze.

*...he's watching—don't think—DON'T THINK—act routine—my hands are clean—*

The mental equivalent of a man trying to hide by screaming his location.

Banki let the silence stretch. Five seconds. Ten. The handler's thoughts spiraled into frantic, weakening loops of denial, each thinner than the last. His composure stood like a salt pillar in a storm—technically intact, visibly dissolving.

"Show me the tools," Banki said softly. "The real ones."

The handler's hands trembled violently. He unrolled the leather tool wrap.

There, nestled beside the legitimate iron pins: three scored replacements, their transmutation aura flickering green in Banki's witch-sight. And beneath them, branded directly into the hide of the wrap—a dockmark. Two crossed fish-hooks over a tide line.

Tidegate.

The brand told him everything. The handler's fracturing mind confirmed it in parallel—jagged flashes of a rain-slick warehouse near the southern quays, a hooded figure whose face the handler had been careful never to look at, heavy silver pressed into his palm with the specific weight of someone else's war.

Banki reached out and tapped the man on his sternum—a gesture so casual it was utterly terrifying.

"You will proceed to the paymaster," Banki instructed, his voice dropping to a quiet rasp that echoed like thunder inside the handler's skull. "You will collect your earned wages. Then you will leave this Yard and never return to Blackwater Quay. If you show your face within these walls again, I will not kill you. I will simply excise your ability to process human speech."

The handler stumbled backward, nodding frantically, and fled up the stairs like a madman.

Banki tracked his retreat. The shadow-stalker growled behind him, a low, subterranean rumble vibrating through the flagstones. He met those glowing ember eyes, acknowledging the hungry intelligence behind them.

"We understand each other," he murmured.

He turned and ascended to the office. The machine remained fragile in its early growth, demanding constant, ruthless calibration.

## The Second Ring

The office sat quiet when he returned. Sable stood by the dormer window, gazing out over the Yard.

"You handled the saboteur," she stated without turning.

"I removed a defective gear," Banki replied, taking his seat at the oak desk and pulling a fresh sheet of vellum forward.

"You permitted him to live."

"Dead men tell no tales, and they certainly spread no terror," Banki noted, dipping his quill in black ink. "He will run straight to Tidegate. He will describe what occurred here. They will realize their clumsy disruptions are useless, forcing them to attempt something far more sophisticated. Or, preferably, they will capitulate and pay tribute."

Sable turned, her expression unreadable. "You play a terrifyingly long game."

"The only game worth playing," Banki replied. "Short-term violent gains belong to footpads and politicians. We are constructing an institution."

He began to write, his hand moving in precise, flawless calligraphy.

"What is that?" Sable asked, gesturing toward the vellum.

"A formal invitation," Banki said. "To Magistrate Vane. It is time we conducted a face-to-face conversation regarding his monthly retainer."

"In person? Here in the Yard?"

"No. On neutral, public ground. The Whispering Lamp, in the Silk District. Public enough to make him feel secure, expensive enough to flatter his vanity."

"I will arrange the security detail," Sable offered.

"Do," Banki agreed. "And Sable?"

She paused at the threshold, her hand on the latch.

"Ensure the security detail is visibly lethal. I want him to know we can reach his throat at any moment, but I also want him to know we choose restraint. Let him feel the leash. It proves infinitely more persuasive than a cold blade."

"Understood," she replied, vanishing into the corridor.

Mara stepped back into the office, carrying a finalized stack of property acquisition deeds. Banki finished his letter, sealed it with crimson wax, and set it aside to dry.

"And if I had preferred immediate leverage?" Banki asked, referencing their earlier discussion regarding the Guild registration.

"Then you would have gained one frightened clerk and lost an entire legal category," Mara said smoothly, organizing her ledgers. "I secured the category."

The shape of her strategy crystallized in his mind. She hadn't built a favor, a bribe, or a threat. She had constructed an official vocabulary the city now had to utilize when speaking of him: *Containment.*

Mara took the document back and filed it before he could decide whether to be pleased. By noon, Caspel's forced signature had become a legal fact clerks repeated carefully. No one said *coerced*, and no one said *clean*. They simply said *filed*—a word that in Blackwater Quay proved far more dangerous than the truth.

Sable returned to the doorway, her voice calm as a closing latch. "The new recruits on probation are responding. Some are eager. Some are lying. I can purge the rot early if you grant permission."

"Purge them," Banki said. "But leave the liars intact until we determine precisely who they are lying *for*."

An old laboratory memory stirred within Banki's mind as he noted the complex warding sequence drawn across Mara's ledger. He chose not to dissect it. Sable's guarded mind, present and deliberately shielded, offered enough complexity for one morning.
