Inside, alongside the mundane implements of a legitimate yard-hand—wrenches, files, pin-drivers, the ordinary hardware of wagon maintenance—was a set of replacement harness pins.

Vexa's trial run would have failed. Harness, animal, handler, reputation: one scored pin could have turned a morning test into blood and public embarrassment.

The swapped pins answered for him.

The saboteur talked.

Two hundred gold: the price of betrayal in this man's economy.

Banki looked at the scored pins. He looked at the saboteur. He consulted the Divine Calculus for a rapid cost-benefit analysis that took two seconds and reached a conclusion that surprised neither of them:

Vexa's trial ran clean that afternoon. The handlers noticed the replacement pins because Banki made them notice, and by evening three would-be imitators understood that sabotage in the Yard left fingerprints even when the hands wore gloves.

"You will continue working in the Yard," Banki said. "You will install the correct pins. You will report to Sable by end of day with every detail of who contacted you, when, where, and how payment was arranged. You will do this because the alternative is that I reach into your mind and take the information directly, which I am capable of doing, and which will leave you with significant cognitive impairment that will make your remaining years both brief and unpleasant."

"And then," he continued, "you will work for me. Because a man who can sabotage is a man who understands systems, and I need people who understand systems. The pay will be better than two hundred gold. The consequences for failure will be considerably worse."

He walked back towards the office through fog that parted before him like a curtain.

Behind him, the saboteur began installing the correct pins with hands that would not stop shaking for the rest of the day.

## Sable's Sorting

A business, if it means to survive first profit, eventually requires a body: walls, water, pens, storage, expansion routes, visibility enough to be real, concealment enough to remain difficult to count.

Mara found the options.

Tidegate Yard came to the top of the list: service-quay access, room for pens, adjacent parcels likely soft enough to acquire later, title clean enough not to disgust. Low Wharf tempted by cheaper frontage and danger thick as flies. Slipstone promised direct access and louder risk. Banki weighed them not as a sentimental builder but as a strategist deciding where best to root a predatory plant.

He wanted dock leverage. He wanted room to grow. He wanted Mara to run the legitimate face of the enterprise. He wanted Sable to remain free to manage the things legitimacy could not say aloud.

So the yard became a plan and then a project. Workshop requests followed. Two floors, basement, separate exits, redundancy. If something was worth building, it was worth building with the expectation of fire, betrayal, or siege.

The cellar beneath the Gull & Gasket smelled like salt, stone, and fear cured into masonry.

Sable had transformed it in six hours. The room no longer looked improvised. Intake by the stairs, where a body first learned whether it had been brought as recruit or risk. Assessment at the bolted chairs, close enough that breath became pressure. Holding in the salted corner where sound went to die. Nothing theatrical. Nothing wasted. Every line on the floor taught a lesson before Sable spoke a word.

Tonight was Rank Trials.

Banki watched from the shadows and did not interfere. This was Sable's room: her angles, her pressure, her test of whether a person could remain useful while frightened. He could read every mind she assessed, but the verdicts were hers. The room worked because the verdicts were hers.

Nell came first, dock-worker build and survivor's eyes, and answered the only question Sable cared about.

"Because I'm good at this," Nell said. "And because being good at things no one else wants to do is the only leverage someone like me has ever had."

Sable studied her for three heartbeats. "Survive the next test."

The others followed. Tams failed with a small lie about a surveillance exercise Sable had already verified, and the lie landed in the room with the wet little weight of a dead fish. Pike passed barely because his fear was honest and his answers remained accurate when Sable pushed. Orin passed cleanly: report-shaped thoughts, limitations named before they became excuses. S1 through S3 confirmed what they had already earned.

S6 answered a field scenario by explaining he would have prevented the scenario during planning. Patrols mapped. Third party identified. Return route dirtied before entry.

Sable's eyebrow rose a fraction.

She did not praise quickly. Praise made hungry people careless, and careless people became leverage for enemies who did not need help. But she let the room feel the shape of her approval. The ones who had passed straightened without meaning to. The ones who had failed understood that the failure had not been theatrical; it had been measured, recorded, and made useful.

In Sable's vocabulary, "acceptable" was a medal.

When the last Shadow had gone, she handed Banki the list: "Nell and Orin promote. Pike extends. Tams is out, relocated, memory-adjusted. S1 through S3 confirmed. S6 formalised under logistics."

"Handle it," Banki said.

At the bottom step she paused. "The organisation is getting stronger."

"That's the point."

"I know." She climbed the stairs. "That's why I stay."

The cellar was quiet after she left. The chalk marks held their places. Sable was still awake.

Work usually filled the empty hours cleanly. Sable's relationship with sleep was transactional: she paid the body's minimum tax and no more. Two hours here, two hours there, always scheduled, never surrendered to. Tonight, though, the work would not hold. Ledgers blurred. Reports flattened. The empty spaces between tasks kept widening until memory could get through.

Kargrim had done it. The stonemason with the daughter who wanted a Bridge Warden's apprenticeship, forty-seven gold short, standing helpless while Banki read the private little architecture of his hope.

Sable had watched his face change when he realised what was happening. Not the broad fear of a man threatened with pain. A smaller thing. More intimate. The discovery that the walls around his inner life were paper, and the person standing before him could read through paper as easily as through glass.

Sable knew what that felt like.

She poured Edric's harsh whisky into a chipped glass and let it burn. She had been twenty-three when her handler taught her that walls were made of paper. She kept no name for him anymore. The name had been deleted from the index of her mind: not destroyed, only made difficult to reach, because some memories were less dangerous when they could not be summoned on purpose.

What remained was sensory and therefore harder to bury. Copper stink. A ruined room. His voice using the language of necessity after necessity had become appetite. The moment she understood his ethics had never been ethics at all, only a mask with clean hands painted on it.

The tests had been subtle at first. Assignments that pressed against her limits without quite crossing them. Questions phrased as hypotheticals. Targets described with just enough missing context that obedience could pretend ignorance was discipline. She had passed because the work had still been defensible by her own code, and Sable's code, private and hard-edged, had survived worse than other people's approval.

Then the aura changed. Not the official mission. The air around it. The pressure of a thing asking to be called necessary because calling it what it was would have made refusal possible.

She had left through a window three floors up and run for two years after that. Not through proper channels. Proper channels were cages with paperwork. She had crossed cities, names, patrons, and safehouses until her life became a map of exits.

Then Banki found her. Or she found him. The distinction had never mattered.

Banki was not better. Different.

That difference mattered because Banki did not ask her to pretend the knife was a candle. He could be monstrous, but he did not dress monstrosity in virtue and demand gratitude for the costume. He wanted power. He said so. He wanted order. He said so. He would do unforgivable things if the Divine Calculus and the Asmodean Pact demanded them. He said that too.

Her handler had pretended to have ethics and possessed none. Banki named his rules, named his appetites, named the work evil when evil was accurate. He valued order, competence, power, and the strategic destruction of threats. He did not pretend those values were mercy.

That did not make him safe.

He had read minds in front of her. Broken people in front of her. Turned need into leverage with the calm of a man arranging books by height. But he did not call the breaking kindness, and he did not ask her to be grateful for being controlled.

It made him legible. In Sable's life, legible danger had always been easier to survive than righteous mercy with a locked door behind it.

She finished the whisky, washed the glass, and looked once into the dark cellar.

Kargrim would be home by now. Memory-adjusted, as promised. His daughter would still want the apprenticeship. The forty-seven-gold gap would still exist. Neither of them would know a pixie in a human skin had read the shape of their hope while a woman in a cellar remembered what it meant to have her own walls opened from the outside.





"I chose this," she said.

The darkness did not answer, and she trusted it for that.

At the top of the stairs, Banki was waiting.

Not blocking the way. That mattered. Blocking would have made him a problem to solve. He stood beside the door with one shoulder against the frame, human face calm, grey-green eyes too still, and Sable knew with the same certainty she knew blade weight and roof angles that he had felt the shape of her distress from below.

His attention moved towards her mind.

She felt it before it touched. Not as sensation. As weather changing.

Then it stopped.

The restraint was small. Almost invisible. To anyone else it would have looked like nothing at all: a man deciding not to ask a question. To Sable it landed with the force of a hand opening instead of closing.

"You are upset," he said.

"That is an observation, not permission."

"I know."

He did not read.

That frightened her more than the reading.

Power was simple when it took. Power that could take and chose not to was harder to file. It made room for wanting, and wanting was more dangerous than fear because fear kept exits clear. Wanting rearranged the exits while one was looking elsewhere.

Sable hated that she noticed his mouth before she noticed his hands. Hated that competence had begun to feel like heat. Hated, most of all, that the attraction had arrived before trust and had therefore given trust something to bargain with.

"You want to protect what you think is yours," she said.

"Yes."

"Then learn this early. Protection is not partnership. If I stay, I stand beside you. Not under you. Not behind your wards. Not inside a pretty locked room you call safe because you built it."

Banki's gaze sharpened, not with anger. Interest. The dangerous kind.

"And if I fail to remember that?"

"Then I leave."

The sentence cost her less than the truth behind it.

Banki looked at her as if the words had altered the architecture of the room. Then he inclined his head once.

"Then I will remember."

It was not an apology. Nothing had happened yet that required one. It was closer to a treaty under the Asmodean Pact, signed before the war it existed to prevent.

Sable went past him without touching him.

She wanted to.

She trusted that part least.

## The Dead-Drop Chapel

Blackwater Quay made piety look damp: fog, salt, canals, and old tunnels remembering worse trades.

Not a paperwork problem or a shortage problem. People were vanishing between streetlamps.

By the time Banki came in person, the facts already had edges. Two probationary shadows gone. One returned without memory. Shadow-mail opened and resealed. The local powers set in crooked relation: Thieves Guild, Harbour Magistracy, Drowned Choir, and behind them all the Ashen Ledger with its Coin-Eaters.

The Dead-Drop Chapel stood on a wedge of land between canals, no bells, no congregation, only damp stone and stubborn mould. Sable waited in the back. On the altar sat the reliquary box, resealed but not honestly. Divination residue—the unmistakable scent of burnt marrow and industrialized High Magic—clung to it. Something near the confessional felt wrong: not absent, but flattened, as though a mind had been hammered smooth.

The first enemy in Blackwater had left a mark.

Inside, the air was thick with the smell of damp stone and old wax. A short nave with warped pews. A cracked altar with candle stubs like old teeth. A confessional booth that leaned slightly to the left, as if it were eavesdropping on conversations it had no business hearing.

Sable was in the back, hood up, posture loose as meant every tendon was a lie. She didn't greet him. She tilted her chin towards the altar.

On the altar: the dead-drop. A small blackwood reliquary box bearing the ShadowNet's quiet-seal pattern. Except the seal had been opened and re-pressed.

Someone had been reading their mail.

His senses found two things at once: divination residue on the box and a hidden mind near the confessional, flattened so carefully that a lesser reader might have mistaken discipline for absence.

Sable spoke, voice low enough that the fog outside wouldn't overhear.

"Branch is compromised. Two probationers missing. One came back smiling and hollow, like a good little citizen."

She stepped to a support beam and scraped a lantern shutter open just enough to illuminate what was carved into the wood.

A coin split by an eye.

"We didn't carve it," Sable said. "We didn't see anyone carve it. But they wanted us to."

She looked at Banki. Steady. Direct. The look of a woman who had built something with her own hands and was watching it be threatened, and who needed to know, right now, whether the man she had built it for was going to fight for it or walk away.

"I can escalate," she said. "Start breaking knees until we find a throat that sings. Or we do this quiet. Your call."

Banki did not answer immediately. He was too busy peeling back the flattened mind near the confessional. Choir rose from it like something drowned returning to the surface.

Banki smiled. The smile had no kindness in it.

"Quiet," he said. "We do this quiet. And we start with the one hiding in the confessional."

Sable's hand went to her hip. Not a weapon—a signal. Her fingers formed a shape that meant "ready" in the ShadowNet's silent language, and her body shifted from at-rest to coiled-spring in the space between one breath and the next.

The mind in the confessional flickered with a sudden spike of alarm—it had heard something, or felt something, or simply realised, with the terrible intuition of a hunted thing, that it had been found.

Too late.

Banki was already moving.

The confessional was a wooden box roughly the size and shape of a coffin that someone had decided to stand upright, and the comparison was about to become uncomfortably apt for the person hiding inside it.

Banki moved first. Not towards the confessional—towards the door. The chapel's only exit. The sound was a single, heavy clunk that echoed through the chapel's nave and bounced off the salt-stained stone walls and arrived at the confessional a third of a second after Banki had already moved to his second position.

Sable moved simultaneously. Banki did not physically shield her or pull her back—he operated strictly as a peer, providing an invisible safety net by psionically anchoring her mind against the Choir's psychic static so she could fight unhindered. She did not seal anything or lock anything or use any force more exotic than the application of her own body to the space between the confessional and the chapel's altar. She positioned herself with the fluid, unhesitating economy of a woman who had been trained to control interior spaces by people who understood that the shape of a room was a weapon, and that the person who controlled the angles controlled the fight.

The mind in the confessional knew. Banki felt the knowledge spike through the suppressed consciousness like a needle through cloth: fear, calculation, a trapped woman's frantic count of bad choices.

The options were limited. The door was sealed. Sable blocked the altar approach. And the confessional itself—a two-compartment box with a latticed divider and a curtained entrance—was not a defensible position. It was a cage.

"Come out," Banki said. His voice was conversational. The kind of conversational that a cat might use with a mouse, if cats could speak: genial, patient, and coloured by the particular confidence of a predator that knows the chase is over and is offering the prey a dignified alternative to being dragged out by the throat.

Silence.

Banki pushed his Mind-Sense deeper into the suppressed mind, peeling back the trained emptiness with the firm, precise pressure of a man opening a locked box whose combination he already knew.

Choir. Definitely Choir. And not a low-level acolyte—this was an operative, a specialist, someone whose training and positioning suggested a role considerably more significant than "hide in a chapel and observe."

The deep read broke through the suppression like a pick through ice. The mind beneath opened involuntarily: not confession, but structural failure under pressure its owner had never been trained to survive.

The operative's name was Halvine.

She had been here for three days. She had been maintaining the trained emptiness for three days—a feat of psionic endurance that impressed Banki's professional respect even as his predatory attention marked her as a target to be neutralised.

And she was armed. A blade, concealed in her sleeve. A signalling focus—a mirrored token similar to the one that the dockside watcher had carried. And something else: a glass vial, nested against her inner wrist, containing a liquid that Banki's Aether-Sight identified as transmutation in nature and that his knowledge of alchemical compounds suggested was either a combat enhancement potion or a suicide agent.

The distinction mattered.

"I'm going to say this once," Banki said, still conversational. "The vial on your wrist. If it's a potion, drink it or don't—I don't care. If it's poison, don't. I need what's in your head, and I need it alive."

The curtain of the confessional moved. Not pulled aside—shifted. A fraction of an inch. Enough for an eye to appear in the gap: dark, wide, pupil-dilated with adrenaline, sharp with the intelligence of someone who was calculating odds in real-time and who had arrived at a number that was not zero but was close enough to zero that the difference was academic.

"You're the reader," Halvine said. Her voice was low, controlled, carrying the studied calm of someone who had been trained to maintain composure in interrogation scenarios and whose training was currently being tested against the reality of facing someone whose capabilities the training had not fully accounted for. "The one they warned us about."

"They warned you about me?"

"They said there was a telepath operating in the district. They said the telepath was powerful. They did not say—" a pause, a recalculation, a moment of honesty that slipped through the professional mask the way light slips through a crack in a shutter "—they did not say that the telepath was this powerful."

Banki felt the compliment land and filed it with the impersonal appreciation of a craftsman who has received a positive review from a competitor. "The vial," he repeated.

A beat. Two. The eye in the gap studied him with the intensity of someone making a decision that would determine whether they saw tomorrow.

The curtain pulled back.

Halvine emerged from the confessional with the deliberate, controlled movements of a woman who understood that fast movements in the presence of dangerous people were the kind of decisions that turned "standing" into "bleeding." She was compact. Lean. Built like a runner—all efficiency, no excess, the body of someone whose profession required the ability to be in a place, observe that place without being observed, and leave that place quickly if the observation went wrong.

The blade stayed in her sleeve. The mirrored token stayed in her pocket. The vial—she raised her wrist slowly, showing it to Banki with the careful deliberation of someone presenting evidence to a judge.

"Combat enhancement," she said. "Reflexes and speed. Thirty-second duration. Not poison."

"Leave it on the pew."

She placed the vial on the nearest pew with the precise, unhurried movements of compliance. The glass clinked against the warped wood—a small, final sound.

Banki read her mind. Not a Neural Savant theft; a hard scan, thorough enough to take the Listener network and leave Halvine standing.

When the extraction was complete, Banki nodded to Sable.

Sable moved with the economical violence that characterised all her physical actions: two steps forward, a controlled strike to the base of Halvine's skull—the same pressure-point technique that Banki had used on the Rinse Below guards, applied with the practised confidence of someone who had been rendering people unconscious since before most of the ShadowNet's current operatives had been born—and a catch that prevented the Listener's body from hitting the stone floor in a way that would produce either injury or noise.

Halvine was unconscious before she finished falling.

"Prisoner sack," Banki said.

"Already?" Sable asked, though the question was not a protest—it was a scheduling inquiry, the organisational equivalent of "do we process her now or later?"

"Later. We have work to do here first."

The work was the dead-drop itself. The blackwood reliquary box on the altar, with its tampered seal and its divination residue and its contents—whatever Sable's coded communications had contained before the Choir's Listener had intercepted and re-sealed them.

Banki checked the box. The seal had been opened and re-pressed, carefully but not perfectly. Inside, Sable's coded report remained unreadable to the Choir but exposed as a channel.

Banki read the report.

Then he turned to Sable.

"The chapel is compromised as a dead-drop location," he said. "We'll continue to use it—but as a honeypot. Every communication we place here from now on is curated. True enough to be believed, false enough to misdirect. We feed the Choir whatever we want them to know, through a channel they already trust."

Sable's expression was the expression of a woman who appreciated the elegance of turning an enemy's intelligence victory into an intelligence liability. "Curated. Meaning I draft the messages?"

"You draft them. I approve them. We build a narrative that points the Choir at targets we want them to target and away from assets we want them to ignore."

"That's—" Sable paused, searching for the word.

"Misdirection," Banki supplied.

"I was going to say 'beautiful,'" Sable said. "But misdirection works too."

They left the chapel at midnight. The bolt slid back. The door opened. The fog welcomed them like an old friend—wet, cold, impenetrable, and absolutely indifferent to the moral character of the people who walked through it.

Behind them, the confessional stood empty, its curtain still half-drawn, its shadows still and patient.

Behind them, the confessional stood empty, curtain half-drawn, while the candle on the third shelf kept burning.

## The Drowned Counting Room

The Drowned Counting Room was exactly the kind of place Banki had spent his life learning to love: ugly, useful, half-submerged, and arrogant enough to believe darkness was the same thing as safety.

Beneath the Gull & Gasket, the Choir had built three things into Blackwater Quay's bones: hymn stones, a tuning apparatus, and a secondary node in Canal Spur 7. Together they smoothed memories, nudged choices, and taught a district to sing without remembering consent.

Banki did not need a sermon. He needed the mechanism broken and the pieces in his hands.

Sable objected to the first draft of the plan because it looked too much like Banki: elegant, invisible, and certain it could solve every problem by being more intelligent than the opposition.

"If three nodes die in one hour, they know they are being hunted," she said.

"They will know eventually."

"Eventually is useful. Immediately is expensive."

So the plan changed. Sable staggered the failures, dirtied the timing, and built ordinary ugliness around the magical work. A drunk ferryman was bribed to complain loudly about a missing lantern. Two boys were paid to switch canal markers in the fog and then forget they had been paid. One mule cart broke a wheel exactly where a Choir runner would have preferred a clean path. None of it was brilliant, which was why it worked. Mud, rain, bad luck, and petty inconvenience were the native language of Blackwater Quay.

Sable took the northern sluice first. Cutoff, net, silence. No alarms. Three acolytes down. One dark-sash lead alive long enough to give Banki the map of the whole network and hate himself for the courtesy.

The Counting Room came next.

Banki moved through the water under Greater Shroud while Sable erased the canal-side watcher. The chamber below had once counted cargo. Now it counted minds. Three fist-sized hymn stones sat on a raised platform, humming against the inside of his skull with patient, invasive hunger.

A fingernail mark had been scratched into the platform beside the nearest stone. Not a sigil. Not code. Just one human edge dragged through dust before obedience finished arriving.

He neutralised the guards through the water, caught the splash before it became sound, and crossed the chamber with every sense open.

The stones tried to sing.

Banki took them apart instead.

He did not smash them. Smashing useful things was for heroes, vandals, and people who could not afford laboratories. He cut the mounts with bone-dust bolts, suppressed the warding using industrialized marrow runes, wrapped each crystal in a shielded pouch, and left the platform looking as if salt, neglect, and municipal incompetence had finally become one more local disaster.

The Chapel of Winds required less delicacy and more timing. Its bell tower had not held a bell in forty years. It did hold the Choir's tuning bracket: a forked apparatus mounted on a gimbal and aimed across the district like a priest's finger.

The conductor never saw him. Banki lifted the man off his feet, set him safely outside the tower rail, and let him shout at the fog while the bolts turned loose under telekinetic pressure. The apparatus floated down through the mist beside a three-inch pixie with wings like stained glass and the professional satisfaction of a thief carrying off an enemy's engine while the enemy still had one hand on the wheel.

Canal Spur 7 was the last arm.

The entrance hid behind three stones in a wall that pretended to be solid. Banki knew the sequence because the dark-sash had known it, and the dark-sash's mind had been much less private after Banki finished asking questions.

Two acolytes guarded the secondary stone. Both fell before the daily calibration pulse. The node's mounting resisted longer than it deserved. Behind it, a camouflaged compartment held the real prize: a ledger, a seventy-two digit sync code, and a subfloor anchor plate that told the network where this node belonged.

The stone was the lock. The plate was the key.

Banki took both.

The one part that nearly failed was not magical.

An acolyte woke too early, coughing river water and panic. He got one hand on a signal token before Sable's borrowed ferryman drifted under the bridge cursing at the top of his lungs about stolen boots, broken rope, and the moral character of every canal worker in Blackwater. The acolyte hesitated, because people in Blackwater hesitated for money, scandal, and the possibility of being noticed by the wrong witness.

That hesitation bought Sable four seconds.

She used three.

Banki felt the acolyte go down through the link and allowed himself the private irritation of being saved by something as inelegant as noise.

Sable's mental reply was dry as chalk. *You may thank the boots later.*

By six fifty-nine, eleven minutes before the Choir expected the network to answer itself, three nodes were dead, one tuning apparatus was on his worktable, and the Blackwater Quay resonance web had dropped to less than half strength.

The Choir would feel the wound before it understood the knife.

On his worktable, the stolen tuning apparatus clicked once and went still.

## Edric Mallon's Salvation

The man behind the bar had the look of someone who had been drowning slowly for years and had finally stopped struggling.

Edric Mallon was forty-three years old, though he looked sixty.

The Gull & Gasket was his tavern only by courtesy. Debt owned the sign, the shelves, and most mornings of his life.

There had been pride here once.

That interested Banki more than despair did. Despair was common. Pride that had learned to keep breathing under debt was useful.

Banki sat down across from Edric at the bar and ordered two drinks. He paid for both. He let the silence between them fill with the ambient noise of the tavern—the clatter of cheap crockery, the murmur of conversations too boring to eavesdrop on, the creak of floorboards that had been absorbing moisture and human misery in approximately equal proportions since before Edric's grandfather was born.

He did not begin with threat. Threats were for people who had not done the Divine Calculus. Edric had done it every morning for six years and woken each time to the same answer: not enough. A man like that did not need to be pushed. He needed only to be shown where the floor ended.

"I'm buying another drink," Banki said, sliding a purse across the bar. "And your salvation."

Edric stared at the money. Hope arrived first. Terror followed close enough to keep it honest.

"Why?" Edric asked. The word came out like something he'd been chewing on for too long.

"Opportunity," Banki said. He smiled. It was the kind of smile that looked warm from a distance and felt surgical up close. "I represent a new organisation. We need a presence in this district. A legitimate face. A tavern that doesn't ask questions and doesn't answer them."

"What organisation?"

"The ShadowNet."

The name landed between them like a stone in still water. Edric's thoughts rippled: he had never heard the name before, which was exactly the point. A name no one had heard was a name no one feared, and a name no one feared was a name that could grow in the shadows until it was too large to be ignored and too entrenched to be removed.

"What does the ShadowNet want with a tavern?" Edric asked.

It was the right question, which improved Banki's opinion of him. Some men, when offered rescue, asked only how much of their dignity must be paid in advance. Edric still wanted to know the shape of the hand reaching down. Worn down was not the same as stupid. Broken was not the same as empty.

"A front," Banki said simply. "A legitimate business that generates legitimate revenue and provides a legitimate reason for people to come and go at all hours without attracting the kind of attention that less legitimate establishments attract."

He leaned forward slightly. "Your name stays on the sign. You stay as Head Bartender. A clean share of net profit is yours, paid monthly, no questions asked. You run the tavern the way you've always run it—same menu, same hours, same customers. The only difference is that the cellar has new management, and the new management is not interested in your opinions about what happens in it."

Edric's thoughts cycled through the offer with the speed of a man who had been desperate long enough to recognise a lifeline when one was thrown, and suspicious enough to check for hooks before grabbing it.

"And the debt?" he asked.

There it was: the true altar of the room. Not the bar. Not the sign. Not the licence nailed behind the shelves. The debt. Invisible, omnipresent, more sacred to Blackwater than any altar because everyone believed in it and everyone bled for it.

"Paid. As of this conversation. The creditor will receive a visit from my associate within the hour, and the visit will conclude with the transfer of ownership documents from his name to a holding entity that you do not need to know the name of. Your debt disappears. Your tavern remains. Your life improves."

"And if I say no?"

Banki's smile didn't change. "Then I finish my drink, I leave, and you spend the rest of the week wondering whether the next person who walks through that door will make you a better offer or a worse one. I am making you the better one. I suggest you recognise it."

Edric looked at the purse. He looked at Banki. He looked at the purse again.

"The paperwork," he said. "Does it always hold up?"

Banki grinned. It was the first genuine expression of amusement he had permitted himself all day. "Sable and I make sure it always does."

Edric extended his hand. Banki shook it. The handshake was firm on both sides—Edric's with the desperate strength of a man gripping the edge of a lifeboat, Banki's with the measured pressure of a man who had just acquired a new piece of real estate and was already calculating its strategic value.

"Welcome to the ShadowNet," Banki said. "You'll find that we're excellent employers."
