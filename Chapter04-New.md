## Pimwick's Back Room

The stretch of the night market harboring Pimwick’s stall thrived in the choking, sulfurous shadows beneath the upper city’s massive brass-grated exhaust vents. Rainwater mixed with heavy industrial runoff, dripping continuously from the sagging, rot-blackened canvas awnings to pool in iridescent, oil-slicked puddles across the uneven cobblestones. The dense air tasted heavily of ozone, rusted iron, and the sharp, narcotic tang of raw dream-leaf burning in clay pipes. Here, in this subterranean artery of commerce, broken laws were not merely mended; they were recast in iron, polished with blood, and sold to the highest bidder with infinitely better hinges.

Banki moved through the throng of desperate merchants and cutthroats without making a sound, his heavy wool coat absorbing the damp chill. He ignored the hawkers peddling glowing, lead-lined boxes of volatile alchemical reagents. He bypassed the stalls displaying rusted clockwork limbs hanging from meat hooks. His focus remained absolute, a predator navigating a familiar jungle.

The crooked, splintering wooden sign hanging above the cramped, iron-barred front counter read *PIMWICK'S CURIOS & SUNDRIES*. Behind the scarred oak planks stood Pimwick Nacklegear. The merchant was a creature composed entirely of sharp, jutting wrists, a rat-like overbite, and a professional, gnawing appetite for desperate coin. He wore a heavy leather apron stained heavily with caustic alchemical burns and the fading, unmistakable brown smears of dried arterial blood. He exuded the scent of cheap, stale tobacco and overwhelmingly expensive, imported ledger parchment.

"A gentleman of discerning tastes," Pimwick began, his voice a practiced, oily rasp that fought a losing battle against the ambient, rhythmic hiss of a nearby steam-vent. He leaned forward, resting knobby, ink-stained elbows on the counter, his eyes darting across Banki's unremarkable attire. "I possess authentic, blood-consecrated Choir reliquaries. I possess Watchmen’s truncheons, only lightly dented with skull-fragments. I possess—"

"Both," Banki said. He did not raise his voice. He let the absolute, flat certainty of the single syllable slice through the merchant’s practiced opening patter like a surgical blade cutting through necrotic tissue. "I am currently evaluating specialized equipment for a private, dedicated military force. I require the Kestrel prototype armor sets. I also require the Brineglass & Bolt prototype sets."

Pimwick’s jaw snapped shut, the oily smile vanishing. His pale eyes darted frantically toward the deep shadows flanking Banki in the alleyway, desperately searching for the invisible, heavily armed army that would necessitate such a massive, specialized order.

"One standard grade, one elite grade, and two commander grades from each respective supplier," Banki continued, his tone carrying the precise, unyielding, mechanical cadence of a falling guillotine. He mapped out the tactical requirements in his mind. The Kestrel suits relied on kinetic-dispersion weaves, sacrificing heavy plating for mobility. The Brineglass sets utilized alchemically hardened ceramic plates that could withstand point-blank arcane detonations but restricted peripheral movement. Theory meant nothing. Only blood held truth. "You have exactly forty-eight hours to secure the inventory. I will field-test the sets against each other. I will put my men inside them. I will break them. I will observe exactly how and where the armor fails when subjected to extreme trauma. I will then award a highly lucrative, forty-unit contract to the victor."

Pimwick's sparse, pale eyebrows rose high toward his receding, sweat-beaded hairline. Surface thoughts bled immediately from the merchant's undisciplined, greedy mind, washing against the heavily fortified edges of Banki's consciousness. It was a frantic, ecstatic, undisciplined tide of rapid, desperate calculation.

*Forty units. Commander grades. By the Saint's bloody teeth, the margin on the Brineglass alone... I can retire to the upper tiers. Dangerous money. Dead-man's money. Who the hell is this? The posture is military, but the face is a ghost. It doesn't matter. Count the margin. Hide the supplier cut. Secure the down payment.*

Banki reached slowly into the deep inner pocket of his heavy coat. He withdrew a single, solid platinum trade bar. The ingot was flawless, heavy, and stamped with the seal of a dead banking house. He placed it onto the scarred wood of the counter and pushed it forward with a single finger. The heavy metal slid across the wood with a dull, heavy scrape that commanded the absolute, undivided attention of the merchant.

"Come to the back room," Pimwick breathed, his voice dropping to a harsh whisper. His hand darted out with the blinding speed of a striking viper. The platinum vanished into the deep pockets of his leather apron before the ambient, flickering gaslight could fully catch its reflection.

The front of the shop maintained the thin, necessary illusion of petty, street-level fence-work. The back room, hidden behind a reinforced iron door, abandoned the sterile, organized racks of a standard military proving ground for absolute, oppressive darkness and the whispering, malevolent presence of heavy chainmail forged with highly illegal soul-slag. The air in the rear chamber dropped ten degrees, thick with the heavy scent of gun oil, pulverized glass, and the sharp, ozone stench of lingering, high-level abjurations.

Banki stepped past a rack of serrated boarding pikes and ran a bare hand over a heavy leather chest harness mounted on a wooden testing dummy. The thick material radiated an unnatural warmth beneath his fingertips, humming faintly with the residual agony of the exotic beast flayed alive to produce it.

He closed his eyes, drawing the aether up from the cold, bottomless, pressurized well at his core. The raw magic surged through his veins like liquid nitrogen, freezing the blood in his capillaries. He channeled the freezing, violent current upward, driving it mercilessly into his optic nerves. When he opened his eyes, the mundane, visible spectrum of the dim room instantly vanished.

His vision snapped into a sharp, localized focus of violent ultraviolet and sickly, glowing greens. The cold, biting energy flooded his sight, painting the lingering afterimages of ambient magic like glowing, radioactive footprints smeared across the wooden crates, steel racks, and stone floor. It was a perfect, unyielding *residue forensics* sweep.

He examined the heavy iron latches of the crates containing the Kestrel samples. He scrutinized the intricate, interlocking woven mesh of the Brineglass elbow joints. The hidden weave of the world lay completely bare before his augmented sight. He found only the expected, jagged, chaotic residue of heavy industrial crafting enchantments. There were no tampered, subtle arcane signatures bleeding from the seams. There were no hidden, thread-thin tracking spells pulsing in the dark waiting to activate. The equipment was completely clean.

Pimwick hovered nervously near the doorway, his restless energy practically vibrating in the cold air. He finalized the delivery logistics, speaking in rapid, hushed, paranoid bursts. He arranged for the heavy crates to arrive at the side intake grates of the Gull & Gasket tavern in staggered, highly specific twenty-minute windows to avoid drawing the attention of the corrupted dockside Watch patrols.

Banki nodded once, committing the complex timetable to his flawless memory, and stepped back out into the sulfurous rain.

He returned to the subterranean cellar headquarters beneath the dilapidated tavern. The heavy, unmortared stone walls of the chamber blocked the city’s damp, biting chill, replacing it with the stagnant, suffocating cold of a forgotten tomb. The stale air tasted heavily of salt rot and ancient, crumbling earth. Banki sat cross-legged on the bare, damp flagstones in the exact center of the room. He let the raw aether pool around him, manifesting as an invisible, freezing ocean rising slowly to his chest.

The mind functioned precisely as a muscle; the arcane served as the relentless whetstone. Survival in the shifting, treacherous, bloody currents of the city required absolute, unquestionable mental supremacy.

He closed his eyes and drew the pooling energy inward, wrapping it tightly around the raw, exposed architecture of his own mind. The chaotic, roaring static of a thousand dockside thoughts—the desperate, gnawing hunger of a beggar in the alley above, the violent, animalistic lust of a sailor in the taproom, the calculating, cold malice of a cutpurse across the street—battered violently against his skull. It was an agonizing, deafening roar of raw humanity.

He applied immense mental pressure. He forged the raw aether into a dense, unyielding mental filter, folding the magical barrier over itself repeatedly like a master smith folding high-carbon steel. He visualized the screaming static and ruthlessly crushed it down. The psychic roar fractured, splintered, and compressed under his will, transforming from an overwhelming, mind-shattering avalanche into a dull, manageable, rhythmic background hum.

A perfect, impenetrable *noise gate*.

He tested the newly forged barrier. He relaxed his immediate focus and let the ambient noise of the city wash over him, processing the immense pressure of the thoughts without suffering the painful intrusion of their content. From this moment forward, locking onto a single, specific mind in a crowded, panicked room would require only a microscopic, deliberate shift in his focused attention, not a taxing, exhausting battle against the crushing psychic tide.

Mechanisms of the defensive mind firmly secured, he opened his eyes and poured his cold intent into the offensive, kinetic application of his telekinesis.

He leveled his right hand at a rusted, heavy iron padlock resting on the scarred surface of a wooden worktable ten feet away. He visualized the internal, hidden geometry of the lock. The heavy, grit-covered brass tumblers. The stiff, resistant, rusted springs. The exact point of leverage required to defeat the mechanism.

Invisible, aether-forged fingers reached across the dark expanse of the cellar. They bypassed the iron casing entirely and grasped the heavy internal tumblers directly.

Banki did not yank. He did not force the mechanism. The application of raw, blunt power was the hallmark of an amateur, and amateurs invariably died screaming in the gutters. He modulated the telekinetic force with agonizing precision, spinning the raw kinetic energy down to a microscopic, hyper-focused *micro-torque*. He applied the rotational pressure, testing the exact, gritty friction of the internal mechanism through his extended, invisible consciousness.

*Click.*

The heavy shackle sprang open. The physical movement occurred with such absolute, controlled smoothness that not a single mote of dust resting on the rusted iron casing disturbed the surrounding air. Perfect, silent, undeniable grip.

With the mechanisms of the mind honed to a lethal, razor edge, he rose from the freezing flagstones and turned his attention to the physical artifice required for the coming war.

Spools of conductive copper wire, heavy blocks of crimson sealing wax, sticks of alchemical chalk, and small glass vials of bone-soot lay meticulously arranged on the wooden worktable. They were mundane, ordinary materials, waiting in the damp dark for eldritch purpose.

He worked in absolute silence for two solid hours. The quiet of the cellar was broken only by the rhythmic scrape of metal files and the soft, sizzling hiss of melting wax. He blended absolute technical precision with a cold, simmering arcane spite.

He meticulously layered the bone-soot and crimson wax into Tamper-Slate seals. They were delicate, hairline traps, woven with microscopic threads of aether, designed to snap and shatter in a specific, instantly recognizable fractal pattern if a designated door, ledger, or strongbox suffered even the slightest unauthorized physical or magical breach.

He took a thin, highly flexible strip of spring steel and clamped it tightly into an iron vice. He worked a bastard file rapidly over the edge, shaving away microscopic ribbons of metal. He shaped the grip geometry to perfectly, flawlessly catch the precise angle of his telekinetic pinch. It formed a 'quiet key', a physical wedge designed to assist his mind in breaking stubborn, heavy-duty latches without leaving a trace of arcane forcing for the Choir's hounds to track.

A residue sample kit followed. He enchanted small, thick-walled glass vials and alchemically treated smear cards, attuning them to forcefully absorb and trap the magical footprints of rival spellcasters.

Lastly, he took a plain, unremarkable leather ledger. He carefully wove a thread of raw, localized telepathy directly into the parchment tabs marking the edges of the pages. He anchored the complex spell to his own central nervous system. The tabs would pulse with a sharp, static, electric sting against his fingertips whenever his wide-cast telepathic net caught a targeted, pre-defined hook-word in the ambient thoughts of those around him.

He stared at the array of tools spread across the rough wood. The instruments of total surveillance and silent, untraceable infiltration.

Preparation complete.

## The Night Market

The Splintered Keel hunched precariously over the edge of the dockside, its rotting, barnacle-encrusted timbers sagging heavily toward the black, churning water of the canal. The tavern looked less like a functional building and more like the decaying carcass of a leviathan washed ashore to rot. The taproom air hit Banki like a physical, suffocating blow the moment he pushed through the heavy, iron-banded wooden door.

Thick, pungent pipe smoke burned his lungs instantly. The atmosphere was a dense, toxic soup of spilled, sour ale, the heavy sweat of unwashed bodies, and the sharp, undeniable copper scent of desperate men trading stolen goods and fractured promises.

Banki stepped fully into the chaotic room and let the heavy door slam shut behind him, sealing out the rain. His newly forged noise gate immediately engaged with ruthless efficiency. It clamped down hard on the chaotic, localized psychic roar of fifty drunken, violent sailors, smugglers, and dockworkers. The overwhelming cacophony of their petty grievances, their simmering lusts, and their drunken rage compressed rapidly into a low, droning, manageable hum at the base of his skull. He navigated the crowded, dangerous room in total mental silence, a ghost drifting effortlessly through a violent tempest.

He moved with deliberate, unhurried purpose toward a scarred, splintering table near the back wall, situated directly beneath a soot-stained wooden sign that read *NO CREDIT*. He did not sit. He raised his right hand and tapped his silver ring twice against the sticky wood.

The sharp sound cut through the ambient noise with unnatural, piercing clarity. The barman, a massive, lumbering slab of meat with a flattened, heavily scarred nose and mismatched, dead eyes, paused in the act of wiping a filthy mug. He stared at Banki, grunted a low acknowledgment, and jerked a massive chin toward a heavy, reinforced oak door entirely blending into the shadows of the rear wall.

Banki crossed the distance and pushed the heavy door open.

Glassjaw Rellis waited in the cramped, low-ceilinged room beyond. The hidden chamber smelled overwhelmingly of cheap, cloying jasmine incense, burning furiously in a brass brazier in a desperate, failing attempt to mask the lingering metallic stench of old blood soaked deep into the floorboards. Abjuration wards hummed continuously in the baseboards. A low, grinding, uncomfortable vibration settled into Banki's jawbone, designed to swallow sound and nullify the intent of eavesdropping spells.

Banki’s altered, aether-infused vision picked up the anomaly before the door even clicked shut behind him.

A jagged, mirror-bright scar lingered on the invisible arcane fabric hovering near the iron lantern hood hanging from the low ceiling. It was the fresh, bleeding residue of a hurried, aggressively scrubbed scrying attempt. The magical footprint was sloppy, amateurish, reeking of bureaucratic desperation. The Choir possessed eyes everywhere, woven into the very infrastructure of the city, and they had been watching this exact room within the last hour.

Banki ignored the lingering magical scar and sat on the wooden chair opposite the fence. He reached into his coat and placed a single platinum trade bar flat on the center of the scarred table. The heavy, dense metal clacked against the wood with the finality of a judge's gavel.

"The shape of delay," Banki said, dropping any pretense of merchant warmth or underworld etiquette. His voice carried the absolute, freezing zero of the deep aether. It was a tone devoid of humanity, devoid of negotiation. "I require names. The paper-side officials. The quiet, ink-stained authority choking the docks and strangling my supply lines."

Glassjaw Rellis leaned back, attempting to project ease. His physical face instantly arranged itself into a sly, evasive, practiced rogue's smile. He spread his hands in a gesture of exaggerated, open innocence. "Friend, the docks are a highly complicated weave. Paperwork takes time. The harbor master has his moods, the registry has its thick ledgers, the tide—"

His physical voice was steady, practiced over years of lying to dangerous men. His unguarded thoughts, however, screamed in absolute, uncontrolled terror.

Banki did not wait for the fence to finish his pathetic lie. He drove his telepathic pressure forward, forging his consciousness into a solid, razor-sharp spike of pure will. He pierced the fence's mental defenses like driving a hot iron needle through the fragile wings of a pinned moth.

The psychic intrusion was violent, instantaneous, and entirely without mercy.

*Voss buys the delay,* Glassjaw’s inner voice sobbed into Banki's mind. The mental projection was frantic, incredibly loud, vibrating with raw panic. *Voss buys it. Wickline under the clerk umbrella. Sandpiper carries the paper tonight. Lantern Office is not an office. Don't say Mira Kett's name. For the love of the Saint, don't let him say Mira Kett.*

Banki withdrew the mental spike, leaving Glassjaw's psyche bleeding, exposed, and utterly violated.

"Marrin Voss," Banki said aloud, his physical voice echoing the stolen thoughts with perfect, terrifying clarity. He let the platinum bar catch the flickering, amber lamplight. "And the Lantern Office."

Glassjaw choked. The physical reaction was violent and immediate. The rhythm of his breathing shattered entirely, turning into rapid, shallow, desperate gasps. The sly smile melted off his face like wax held to an open flame. He stared at the platinum bar resting on the wood as if it were a coiled viper preparing to strike.

"Voss is port-facing," Glassjaw rasped, his vocal cords tightening painfully. He gripped the edges of the table with white knuckles to stop his hands from shaking. "But Wickline... that's Clerk-Senior Elira Pynn. The runner moving the authorizations tonight is Sandpiper. They meet at the Salt-Registry Annex. Precisely before the third bell."

An image flared with blinding, undeniable intensity in the fence's panicked, unraveling mind. A pristine white cotton glove. A stark, black ink-stained thumbnail. The image carried a crushing weight of institutional dread. *Sister Quill.*

"And Mira Kett?" Banki asked. He did not raise his voice, but he let the name drop onto the wooden table with the devastating, crushing weight of a collapsing building.

Glassjaw sagged heavily against the back of his chair. The last remnants of his roguish charm drained entirely from his features, leaving behind only the hollow, gray exhaustion of a thoroughly cornered, defeated man who knew he had just betrayed a god to a demon.

"People call her Sister Quill," Glassjaw whispered, his eyes darting frantically toward the shadows of the room as if expecting the woman to manifest directly from the dark. "It's an insult. But never to her face. If Voss buys the time, Mira is the one who ultimately decides which delay becomes law. She is the bottleneck."

Banki reached out and swept the platinum bar back into his palm. The metal vanished seamlessly into his coat. Glassjaw had preserved his own miserable life by surrendering the information. He had not earned the wealth.

"If you go after Voss, do it clean," Glassjaw whispered to the empty air, his gaze fixed on the table as Banki stood and turned toward the heavy oak door. "He panics downward. And downward reaches into very ugly, very dark rooms."

"I know," Banki said.

He opened the door and stepped back into the suffocating noise of the taproom, leaving the fence to drown in his terror.

## The Paper Saint

The Chapel Annex of Saint Brine clung parasitically to the sheer, black stone side of the main cathedral. It was a narrow, ascending spire of gothic architecture, looming menacingly over the labyrinth of the city's administrative district. Thick, freezing mist rolled off the surface of the black canal below, crawling up the side of the masonry and clinging heavily to Banki’s coat as he ascended the exterior, spiraling stone stairwell.

A single, thick enchanted candle burned in a wrought-iron sconce at the first landing, casting long, wavering, distorted shadows against the damp stone walls. The sacred and the profane met in this spiraling tower without forcing either side to officially admit the arrangement. It was a place where desperate prayers were quantified in heavy ledgers and salvation carried a strict, non-negotiable, exorbitant tariff.

The circular room at the top of the stairwell smelled of old, brittle paper, cold sealing wax, and the heavy, oppressive, undeniable silence of archived human pain.

Mira Kett sat perfectly motionless behind a massive desk carved from dark, petrified ironwood.

She wore severe, high-collared black clerical robes that swallowed her thin frame. Pristine, snow-white cotton gloves covered her hands, which rested flat and unmoving atop a massive, leather-bound ledger. A stark, jagged black ink stain marred the left thumbnail of the glove. Sister Quill. She possessed the rigid, bloodless uniformity of a woman who had survived eight decades in a cutthroat, violent city not by wielding a blade, but by dictating exactly who bled on paper. Her skin was the color of old parchment, pulled tight over sharp, avian bones.

"A blessing for a wagon that must leave Wednesday," Banki said, coming to a halt before the desk. Sable's procured access code hung in the quiet, dead air of the room, a password bought with blood and silver.

Sister Quill did not blink. Banki reached out with his mind, gently pressing against the perimeter of her consciousness. He found no frantic scrambling, no spike of animal fear like Glassjaw. Her thoughts remained entirely cold, meticulously cataloged, and hermetically sealed against emotion. She was a machine composed of rules and ledgers. She registered the access code. She registered his physical presence. She merely calculated his clearance to ask direct, dangerous questions.

"Wednesday is a highly specific day," her voice rustled. The sound was identical to dry parchment dragging across rough stone. "Specificity in this office is terribly expensive."

Banki did not barter. He did not negotiate. He reached into his coat. He placed fifty solid platinum trade bars onto the petrified wood of the desk, stacking them in five neat, flawless columns of ten. The pale, heavy metal caught the meager candlelight, throwing a hard, silver reflection across the woman's impassive face. It was a fortune large enough to buy a minor noble's estate outright. It answered any lingering questions regarding the absolute seriousness of his intent.

Sister Quill stared at the impossible wealth. Her heart rate did not elevate a single beat.

"Three things," Banki said, his voice mirroring the absolute chill of the room. "I need to know exactly who applies the bureaucratic pressure to my enchanter. I need to know exactly what moves through the Wickline checkpoint tonight. And I require the true name of the paperwork friend who has been sending anonymous, terrified warnings to my organization."

Sister Quill reached out. Her ink-stained thumb traced the sharp, milled edge of the nearest platinum bar. The physical contact seemed to solidify her decision.

"The enchanter’s pressure comes directly from a Choir intermediary," she said, her tone devoid of inflection, delivering intelligence that would cost lives. "Sub-Prefect Halvyr. He routes the disruption through the zoning board to maintain distance. Follow the chain of delays backward, and they end at a heavily fortified building the Choir refers to internally as the Quiet Loft."

She withdrew her hand and tapped a dry, bony finger against the cover of her ledger. "Wickline moves tonight because the transit stamp at Wickline Pier rotates at the changing of the guard. Two specific clerks. One authentic stamp. It provides completely unrestricted, uninspected southern canal access. The Choir uses that specific window to move heavy sanctification hardware. Weaponized relics. The handoff occurs precisely before the tolling of the third bell. The operational challenge phrase is *Brine blesses the bolt*."

She paused. Her pale, rheumy eyes lifted from the metal, locking onto Banki's face with the flat, dead stare of a reptile analyzing prey.

"The hander of your anonymous warnings is a mid-level registry clerk named Serris," she continued, delivering the woman's death warrant with absolute bureaucratic indifference. "She is married. She possesses no criminal history. She is currently terrified out of her mind. She folds those warnings into the weekly tithe envelopes because her fragile conscience has not yet been completely crushed by the reality of this city."

Banki processed the data rapidly. A critical vulnerability exposed. The true chokepoint did not lie in the physical system of stamps and armed guards. It lay in the terrified, breaking mind of the clerk manning it.

"What you choose to do with her," Sister Quill said, interlacing her white-gloved fingers, "determines the exact shape of your rising empire. Bodies create investigations. Fear creates efficiency. I deal in efficiency."

"I intend to build an empire that strictly rewards useful compliance," Banki replied. He reached down, sweeping the front of his coat back to settle the heavy fabric. He turned toward the descending stairwell. "Dead assets are a profound failure of imagination. Alive and terrified is an infinitely renewable resource."

## The Rank Trials

The isolation cell designated Add-on D sat buried beneath the Sablehook salvage front — a tomb of windowless stone. No ventilation. No light. A double-door iron airlock swallowed the city's ambient noise, leaving a silence so deep it generated physical pressure against the eardrums. The room existed for one purpose: to break a mind before the body registered the danger.

Banki stood in the far corner, wrapped in the absence of light.

The heavy inner door groaned open.

Sable entered first — not walking, stalking, her presence anchoring the room's chaotic energy. Shadow One followed, hauling a stained burlap sack over his broad shoulder.

The sack writhed. Muffled grunts and the thud of elbows against canvas echoed off bare stone. Seven prisoners. Choir logistics clerks, night-shift couriers, loft attendants — ripped from the city's hidden arteries over the past six hours. Seven minds, each holding a jagged piece of the Choir's operational puzzle.

A harsh oil lantern flared behind Sable. The sudden light threw her features into sharp relief and painted jagged silhouettes against the walls. The air thickened with the sour stench of adrenaline and unwashed fear.

"Dump them," Sable commanded.

Shadow One heaved the sack forward. Seven figures, bound with hemp and gagged with filthy rags, spilled onto the hard floor in a tangled, thrashing heap.

"Begin with the gossip broker," Sable said. "Surface-level route information. Ten minutes before we escalate."

Shadow One waded in. He grabbed a sweating, struggling man by the collar, hauled him upright, and ripped the gag away. The broker gasped, eyes blown wide in the lantern light.

Shadow One fired questions. Names. Supply routes. Shift rotations. His technique was blunt — heavy on intimidation, desperate for speed, and ultimately flawed. He pressed too hard, his body language betraying his own need for rapid answers.

The gossip broker was a hardened survivor. Despite his terror, his mind snapped into practiced defence — a dense verbal wall of irrelevant street rumours, frantic misdirection, a labyrinth of noise and calculated half-truths.

Seven minutes ground past. Shadow One stalled. From the void, Banki registered the sharp spike of frustration narrowing into the crude desire to use fists where leverage had failed.

Sable tilted her head a fraction toward the empty corner.

Banki dropped the shadow-cloak.

He flowed out of the darkness — a nightmare solidifying into flesh under the harsh amber light.

The broker's words died. His eyes locked onto Banki. The man's chaotic thoughts collapsed into a single, white-hot spike of paralytic horror.

Banki crouched in front of the bound man. The stench of stale sweat and voided bowels filled the claustrophobic space between them. No question. No threat. No negotiation.

He placed both hands against the sides of the man's skull.

The contact was gentle. That was the worst part.

He pushed inward — not through bone, but through the architecture of identity. The broker's surface defences, all those practised lies and evasions, peeled away like wet paper. Beneath them lay the real structure: a lifetime of accumulated self, built memory by memory, experience by experience, the way a reef builds itself one organism at a time over decades.

Banki began taking it apart.

The broker's mouth opened. No scream came out. The vocal cords still worked. The lungs still drew air. But the part of the man that understood what a scream *was for* had already come loose.

The first memory arrived like a gasp after drowning. The sharp geometry of street-corner diplomacy. The exact weight of a bribe in a palm — not the number, the *weight*, the physical sensation of coins pressed against sweating skin. The broker's mother's face, irrelevant, discarded. A childhood beating, irrelevant, discarded. The silver-tongued rhythms of persuasion — *kept*, slotting into Banki's mind like a key finding a lock it had been cut for. The precise layout of the black-market information networks bloomed behind his eyes, complete and navigable, a map drawn in another man's lifetime of desperate survival.

The broker's eyes stayed open. The pupils had gone fixed and wide. Breathing continued — shallow, mechanical, stripped of the irregular rhythm that distinguished a person from a bellows. Something still occupied the chair. It blinked. It breathed. It would never understand language again.

Banki released the skull. The body slumped sideways against the wet stone.

Shadow One staggered backward. A choked gasp escaped the recruit.

Banki stood. The flood of stolen memory settled into his own architecture — not painlessly. Each absorbed life carried the sensory residue of its original owner: the broker's chronic knee pain, the ghost of tobacco on a tongue that wasn't his, a flash of desperate love for a woman whose name now belonged to Banki and meant nothing to him.

He turned to the next prisoner.

The Choir courier went faster. He had the technique dialled now — bypass the identity, target the operational knowledge, strip the useful architecture and let the rest collapse. The courier's mind offered trained low-light acuity, counter-surveillance instincts, the exact timing of the mirror-glint sequence the Choir used to signal all-clear on the rooftops. The body went limp in thirty seconds. The eyes stayed open.

The loft attendant. The meeting cadence of the Choir lieutenants. The physical layout of the fortified loft. The confirmation of the challenge phrase: *Brine blesses the bolt.*

The fourth — supply routes. The fifth — faces and names of mid-level enforcers. The sixth — cipher keys. The seventh — safe house locations.

He moved down the line with the mechanical efficiency of a man emptying a filing cabinet, taking what was needed and leaving the drawers open and bare. Each extraction met less resistance than the last. By the fifth, the remaining prisoners had stopped struggling. They lay in the flickering lantern light, staring at nothing, already gone behind the eyes before his hands ever touched them.

Seven bodies lay across the floor. All of them still breathed. None of them were people anymore.

Banki stood among them. His mind surged with the massive influx of stolen architecture — the Choir's compartmentalised logistics, the timing of the third-bell window, the two-clerk bottleneck at the pier. He possessed it all, absorbed with the desperate thoroughness of a creature who'd once had everything torn from his own mind and had sworn never to need anything he didn't carry inside his own skull.

He turned to Shadow One.

The recruit was on his knees. His hands trembled against the wet stone. The terror radiating from the man wasn't worship — it was the cold, permanent understanding of what he served. The Rank Trials had never been designed to teach interrogation. They existed to teach the Shadows what lived behind the face of their commander.

"Dispose of the husks," Banki said.

Shadow One's head dipped. "Yes, sir."

Sable stood near the door, her mind already recovered, recalculating. The tactical applications of what she'd witnessed were writing themselves across her thoughts in cold, precise shorthand. She hadn't flinched. She'd catalogued.

Banki walked past her. The shadows parted.

The Choir's architecture lay open in his mind — every route, every name, every weakness illuminated by the stolen light of seven extinguished lives.
