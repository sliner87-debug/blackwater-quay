He finished his drink. It was, as expected, terrible.

But the cellar beneath it was perfect.

## Pimwick's Back Room

Pimwick's stall was in the part of the market where honest commerce went to die and was reborn with better hinges.

The sign said PIMWICK'S CURIOS & SUNDRIES, which was technically accurate in the way a lightning strike is technically weather. Pimwick Nacklegear waited beyond the front counter, built almost entirely out of wrists, opinions, and professional appetite.

"Both," Banki said, when Pimwick asked whether he was shopping for defence or curiosity. "I'm equipping a private military force. Forty standard kits, eight elite, two commander-grade. Stealth priority, then combat. Capture gear separate. And I don't cheat my suppliers, so don't cheat me."

Pimwick's eyebrows rose. His thoughts flickered with rapid commercial calculation: forty standard, eight elite, two commander, serious money, dangerous money, possibly the best Tuesday in six years.

"Commander-grade," he said carefully. "That's a prototype conversation, not an off-the-shelf conversation."

"I know. One standard, one elite, two commander prototypes. Forty-eight hours. I'll evaluate against Brineglass & Bolt and award the full contract to whoever impresses me. Payment on delivery. Prototype cost absorbed into the contract if you win."

Pimwick's expression went cold with competitive joy.

"Halven," he said, as if the name were something unpleasant caught in a gear. "That bastard."

"Good," Banki said. "I prefer suppliers with enemies. It keeps the work honest."

He set a sapphire on the counter. Pimwick examined it once, then pocketed it with the practised motion of a man accepting the first plank in a bridge he intended to charge tolls on.

"Come to the back room," he said. "I have things to show you that the front room would be embarrassed by."

The back room was an armoury.

Pimwick did not open the door like a showman. He opened it like a man revealing the room that had kept him alive. One hinge sighed. He heard it and winced, already calculating oil, privacy, risk, and whether Banki had noticed.

Pimwick paused at the threshold and looked at Banki with his hand on the latch. For half a second, the merchant dropped the bright patter and let the real calculation show. The back room was not for customers. It was for people one either trusted, feared, or intended to profit from too extravagantly to refuse.

Not the kind soldiers trained in, all racks and institutional confidence. This was a private armoury in the space between legal and worth-denying: whispering chain forged with soul-slag, bone-dust bolts, marrow-runed leather, and capture gear built for things that climbed, flew, burrowed, displaced, or objected to physics as a matter of temperament.

Banki examined each piece with focused attention. The difference between good equipment and great equipment was often the difference between a successful operation and everyone dying loudly. His Aether-Sight walked every seam. His fingers checked stitching, hinge play, buckle tolerance, and the little lies craftsmen told themselves when they hoped a buyer would not know better.

There were fewer lies than expected.

"I want all of it," Banki said. "The standard kits, the elite kits, the commander sets, and a complete capture-and-containment package scaled to handle everything from a pixie to a dire bear. And the force-edge daggers. Two for each commander kit."

Pimwick's thoughts sang with commercial ecstasy. His face remained professionally neutral, which Banki appreciated. Showing excitement during negotiation was the same thing as leaving money on the table with a note attached.

"Bulk discount above thirty units," Pimwick said. "Standard terms."

"Custom terms. Non-traceable procurement. Delivery to a location I specify. No records that include my face, my name, or the name of my organisation. Payment through a holding entity."

"That costs more."

"I know." Banki smiled. "I'm not buying equipment, Pimwick. I'm buying a relationship. Relationships are worth the premium."

Pimwick studied him for a long moment. Then he extended his hand.

"You're going to be my favourite customer," he said. "Or my most dangerous one."

"With any luck," Banki replied, shaking his hand, "both."

## The Night Market

The Splintered Keel was the kind of tavern that made the Gull & Gasket look like a cathedral.

It hunched on the dockside like a drunk who had been thrown out of every other establishment on the waterfront and had decided, in a fit of resentful self-sufficiency, to become an establishment himself.

Inside, the air was thick with pipe smoke, spilled ale, and the stink of people selling what they should not own.

The second door marked "NO CREDIT" was in the back. Banki tapped his ring twice on the table as he'd been instructed, and asked for "saltproof goods."

The barman—a man whose face looked like it had been assembled from spare parts by someone who had never seen a face before—gestured towards the door without making eye contact, which was either professional discretion or a complete lack of interest in anything that wasn't directly related to the pouring of drinks.

Glassjaw Rellis was waiting in the back room.

He was smaller than Banki had expected.

The back room was small, low-ceilinged, and warded. Banki's Aether-Sight caught the defences immediately: an abjuration privacy seal drawn in powdered bone on the walls (designed to prevent eavesdropping), a faint divination charm powered by a dried optic nerve on the lantern hood (a passive tell that alerted Glassjaw if certain magics were used in the room), and—more interestingly—a residual mirror-glint signature in one corner that told Banki someone had been scrying into this room recently and had made a hurried, imperfect attempt to mask the evidence.

Choir residue. Again.

Banki sat down and laid his opening bid on the table: two gold trade bars and a platinum.

"Buyer," he said. "Big purchases. Discretion paid."

Glassjaw's eyes flicked to the metal. His thoughts read: Not counterfeit. Not desperate. Dangerous combination.

"Private buyer for the Powerful, Rare, or Unique things," Banki continued, letting his voice shift from merchant-warm to predator-cold. "And names. Names are always an important purchase."

Forty-five minutes bought him defence items, mobility gear, the name of the pressure behind his enchanter, and the person who had steered two Choir-adjacent agents towards this meeting.

By the time he left the Splintered Keel, the fog had thickened to less than ten feet and Blackwater had shown him several more wires worth cutting.

The city showed him its moving parts. Banki remembered which ones squealed.

The fog swallowed him as he walked.

Blackwater Quay continued to decay, unconscious of the fact that its new owner had already begun drawing up renovation plans.

Darric Vane operated from a compound that smelled like a zoo that had been built inside a blacksmith's shop and then forgotten for several years.

The compound occupied a triangular lot at the intersection of Butcher's Alley and the Tanners' Walk—a location that was either strategically chosen for its proximity to the raw materials that animal husbandry required or was simply the only piece of real estate in Blackwater Quay whose neighbours were already producing smells potent enough that the addition of exotic animal musk went unnoticed. Banki suspected the latter.

The buildings were functional rather than aesthetic: low stone structures with heavy doors and small windows, their interiors divided into pens, cages, and workspaces that were arranged with the kind of organised chaos that characterised operations run by people who were very good at one thing and very bad at everything else.

Darric himself was a man in his late forties, built like a barrel that had been wrapped in leather and left out in the weather.

His mind, which Banki read as a matter of course upon entering the compound, was a fascinating place.

"You're the auction man," Darric said, not looking up from the feeding trough he was filling with something that smelled like fish and failure. "Sable's boss."

"I'm Banki. Yes."

"She said you want to build a capture-and-breeding operation. She said you have money. She said you're not stupid." He looked up. His eyes—brown, sharp, embedded in a face that had been hit by more hooves and claws than most faces survived—assessed Banki with the blunt, non-diplomatic evaluation of a man who judged people the way he judged animals: by their behaviour rather than their presentation. "She didn't say whether you were honest. Are you?"

"No," Banki said. "I am bound by the Asmodean Pact and the Divine Calculus. Honesty is a human inefficiency. I offer ironclad contracts and inevitable consequences."

Darric's eyebrows rose. Then he laughed—a short, barking sound that shared certain acoustic properties with the creatures in his care.

"Good. Honest people lie about being honest. Dishonest people who admit it save me time."

He set down the feeding bucket.

"What exactly are you proposing?" Darric asked.

Banki gave Darric partnership, not theatre: money, capture infrastructure, market access, handler authority, and the shape of a dream he had carried too long to call new.

Darric did not interrupt. He set one palm flat on the table, the way practical men do when a profitable idea has become heavy enough to require bracing.

Darric listened without smiling. The jokes went first; then the merchant; then the man who knew exactly how badly a beautiful animal could suffer from another person's ambition.

Darric's gaze flicked once towards the empty wagon yard behind the compound.

"And the draft stock?"

"Dire Rhinos," Banki said.

Darric waited for the correction. None came.

"True Dire Rhinos," Banki added. "Two per wagon. Six total for the first live-lot push. I have wanted this combination since before Sablehook had a name."

"Combination?"

"Dire Rhino mass," Banki said. "Displacer wrongness. Not for war. For roads. A Caravan Line."

Darric's expression lost its humour.

"You are talking about breeding something no sensible road has ever asked to meet."

"I am talking about a caravan beast that can pull through mud, refuse ambush, brace a broken wagon, frighten raiders, and keep children and medicine moving when the road decides to become a throat."

"No matter the cost?"

"No matter the cost."

It was not Direction Four yet. It was not the Military Line. In Banki's private architecture it had been an old, almost tender dream: a black-yoked road monarch with enough wrongness in its blood to make bandits look for easier weather. The dream was Caravan first. Protection first. Weight, road, rescue, opened cages, medicine delivered.

The war version would come later, when Blackwater taught him that roads were not enough.

"Joint approval for hybridisation," Darric said. "Non-negotiable. I don't care how much money you throw at it—if a breeding cross is biologically unsound, ethically indefensible, or likely to produce an offspring that's going to eat its way through a containment facility, I say no and no means no."

"Agreed," Banki said.

"Handler authority on welfare. If one of my handlers says a specimen needs rest, food, medical attention, or a larger enclosure, that override is immediate and non-appealable. Even by you."

"Agreed."

"Blue Halt veto. If I determine that a capture operation has become too dangerous—for the handlers, for the specimen, for the civilians in the area—I call Blue Halt and the operation stops. Immediately. Everyone withdraws. No arguments. No exceptions. No 'just one more try.'"

"Agreed."

Darric studied him.

"Sixty thousand gold upfront," Darric said. "For startup costs. Wagons, containment equipment, handler salaries, specimen acquisition for the breeding stock. Paid in full before we start, not in instalments."

Banki had brought the money. Not in gold—sixty thousand gold pieces weighed approximately twelve hundred pounds and would have required a wagon of its own to transport. He had brought it in platinum trade bars: sixty bars, each one worth a thousand gold pieces, each one small enough to hold in one hand and heavy enough to make that hand respect what it was holding.

He placed the bars on Darric's worktable. The stack caught the light from the compound's single, sooty window and turned it into something that looked less like illumination and more like intent.

Darric looked at the platinum. He looked at Banki. He extended his enormous, scarred, creature-handler's hand.

"Partner," he said.

Banki shook the hand. The grip was powerful enough to make the polymorph's knuckles creak.

"Partner," he agreed.

The Live Lot Division was born.

## Glassjaw, Sister Quill, and the Paper Saint

The city changed texture once Sablehook had a legal name. Before, Blackwater had treated Banki as rumour. After, it began to treat him as market weather. Men adjusted. Women recalculated. Clerks sharpened their quills against his existence. Competitors sniffed at the air and pretended not to notice a new predator establishing feeding rights under municipal paint.

Banki spent the next day teaching the city to pronounce Sablehook correctly.

He bought in public.

Rope by the coil.

Under the public buying ran the other current.

Mind-Sense skimmed the market in partitions now, two target locks at once when needed, hook words armed and waiting beneath the noise: ShadowNet.

By noon he had three names around Wickline.

Glassjaw first. Because fences hear where honest people cannot.

The man kept rooms above a lane so narrow even noon reached it already tired. The stair smelled of incense used to hide older crimes. Banki went alone and openly this time, no invisibility, no pixie grace, only his human face, the commander weave under a city coat, and the sort of calm that makes cowards overestimate a visitor's legal protection.

Glassjaw lounged behind a low table strewn with beauties that all had bad histories.

“You've moved up in the world,” Glassjaw said, glancing at Banki's coat. “I can smell paperwork on you.”

“An infection,” Banki said. “Temporary.”

He set down two gold trade bars first, then, after a beat, a platinum one. The metals made three different sounds against the table. Glassjaw's thoughts lit at once. Not poor. Not reckless. Buying names, then. Careful.

“I want the shape of delay,” Banki said. “Not gossip. Names. Paper-side. Quiet authority.”

Glassjaw's smile thinned. “Expensive shape.”

“Then earn the metal.”

He did not go straight to mental force.

Fast deep scan.

It was not a dramatic gesture. Banki did not widen his eyes or speak in someone else's voice. He simply looked. The thought-pressure went through the man like a pin through a moth. Surface resistance cracked at once because Glassjaw had trained himself to lie verbally, not inwardly.

Not the rack. The delay. Voss buys delay. Wickline under clerk umbrella. Sandpiper carries tonight. Lantern Office is not an office. Don't say that aloud. Don't say Mira's name. Don't say it.

Banki chose the pressure point with exquisite laziness.

“One platinum for both names,” he said. “Marrin Voss. And who shields Wickline.”

Glassjaw's breathing altered by half a stitch. There it was. He knew he had been caught but not how deeply. Men hate that more than pain.

“Marrin Voss,” he said finally, lifting the platinum bar and testing the weight against his finger pads. “Port-facing. Paper-facing. Quiet enough that most people only notice him when their cargo stops moving.”

“And Wickline?”

“Clerk-Senior Elira Pynn. But Wickline's runner tonight is called Sandpiper.”

Banki said nothing.

Glassjaw made the error common to the frightened. He tried to sweeten the revelation with more truth than he had sold.

“Wickline meets them at the Salt-Registry Annex before third bell. Whole pack will spook it. No pack, you miss it. Also...” He hesitated. In the mind beneath the hesitation a different image flared. White glove. Cold room. Simple iron latch. Sister Quill. Mira Kett whispers to the saint, not to Voss.

“Also?” Banki asked.

Glassjaw wet his lips. “There's another lane. Deeper. People call it Lantern Office when they don't want to sound afraid. It isn't an office. It's a... handling room. Paper for the paper that isn't supposed to exist.”

“Who works it?”

The fence's fear peaked hard enough to taste. Don't say Mira. Don't say Mira. He'll take it. He'll make it matter.

Banki leaned forward just enough to let the commander weave whisper faintly under his coat. “You are not protecting her,” he said. “You are merely preserving the order in which you disappoint me.”

Glassjaw shut his eyes.

When he opened them, resignation had replaced charm with something that almost looked adult. “Mira Kett,” he said. “People call her Sister Quill. That is not a church title. It's an insult that stuck because she writes like she's taking confession. If Voss buys time, Mira decides which delay becomes law.”

The room went still around that.

Banki took the gold bars back and left the platinum where it lay. “Good,” he said.

As he rose, Glassjaw spoke one last time without his old wit.

“If you go after Voss, do it clean. He's city enough to panic downward. Panic downward reaches ugly rooms.”

“I know,” Banki said.

Outside, he did not go straight to the Annex.

He set the trap first.

A tail-check in the lane found the watcher quickly, not because the man was incompetent, but because Banki had become unusually good at hearing the difference between ordinary caution and purchased attention. A labourer too idle. A boot too clean for that district. Mind carefully blank at the edges. Banki fed him one false glance, one deliberate route, then slipped away invisible at the corner and let Sable's team take him in the alley beyond. Not dead. Not yet. Alive questions were often worth more than dead warnings.

Sandpiper did not get caught that night. The lane shifted. The runner smelled something wrong. But Banki took something more durable instead.

He found Mira Kett's second thread through Irin Halvek.

The woman kept rooms in the old Cold File annex, a place of leather-bound ledgers, damp stone, and silence so thick it felt archived. She had a plain face that would not survive in songs and a mind so composed it might have been sewn from paper. White gloves. Ink under the nail of the left thumb. Steel in the posture. She let him in by appointment nobody had dared admit arranging.

“I was told you buy delay,” Irin Halvek said.

“I buy routes around it.”

Her mouth barely moved. “Better.”

No tavern charm here. No fence's crooked theatre. This was the paper saint's chapel. The desk held only three items: a ledger, an iron seal, and a bowl of plain water. Irin sat as though she had charged the chair with authority before arriving.

“What do you want?” she asked.

“Friction points. Permit choke lines. Which clerks are corrupt, which are frightened, and which are so devout they'll do damage for free.”

“Why?”

“Because I am moving living cargo and I dislike discovering morality in triplicate halfway through a route.”

That earned him the smallest ghost of approval.

She did not confirm Mira Kett.

By the time he left, Banki had more than a lead. He had a map of the city's bureaucratic pain.

All of it pointed back to one man more immediately than the others.

Marrin Voss.

If Glassjaw had been a crooked mirror, Voss was the actual blade behind the reflections. He bought time. Time delayed wagons. Delayed wagons meant stress at Darric's yard, pressure on enchanters, vulnerability in launches, and opportunities for anybody in the Choir or the Ledger who understood schedules better than combat.

That night, Voss stopped being paperwork.

He became prey.

The Chapel Annex of Saint Brine occupied the side of the main chapel, past smoke-black stone, salt-worn statuary, a door that was not hidden but was not meant for tourists, and a stair lit by one stubborn enchanted candle. Sacred and profane met there without making either side admit the arrangement.

Beyond the candle, a room.

The woman behind the desk was plainly dangerous. The desk was worse: it contained information.

She looked up when Banki entered. Old, bright-eyed, white-gloved, ink under one thumbnail. Her face would not have survived in songs, but it had survived eight decades of rooms where paper decided who bled. Her first glance inventoried him as quickly as his Mind-Sense inventoried her.

She didn't find a match. That interested her.

"A blessing for a wagon that must leave Wednesday," Banki said, repeating the phrase Sable had provided.

Sister Quill's expression didn't change, but her thoughts flickered with recognition—not of the phrase itself, which was a rotating access code that changed weekly, but of the door it represented. Someone from Sable's network. Someone with clearance to ask direct questions and the resources to pay for direct answers.

"Wednesday," she repeated, her voice dry and precise as rustling paper. "That's specific. Specificity is expensive."

Banki placed fifty platinum on the desk and sat without invitation. "Three things," he said. "Who is pressuring my enchanter. What moves through Wickline tonight and who moves it. And the true name of the paperwork friend sending anonymous warnings to my organisation."

Sister Quill looked at the pouch. She looked at Banki. She looked at the pouch again.

"Those three things," she said carefully, "are connected."

"I know," Banki said. "That's why I'm asking for all three at once."

"The enchanter's pressure comes from a Choir intermediary named Halvyr," she said finally. "Sub-Prefect rank. Message runners. Scheduling conflicts. Competing priorities. Follow the delays far enough and they end at a building the Choir calls the Quiet Loft."

"Wickline," Sister Quill said, "moves tonight because the transit stamp at Wickline Pier is rotating. Two clerks, one stamp, southern canal access without inspection. The Choir has used it for six weeks."

"The hander?"

"Serris. Married. No criminal history. Terrified. Her son works at the salt-press, and the Choir owns the salt-press. The receiver is unknown. Handoff before third bell. Challenge phrase: BRINE BLESSES THE BOLT."

Banki absorbed the shape: not clerk, stamp. Not person, chokepoint.

Sister Quill paused before the last piece. The candle made a small sound in its cup. "Serris has been sending warnings to anyone she thinks might stop them without getting her son killed. She folds them into tithe envelopes and dock tallies. Conscience makes people useful and vulnerable."

Sister Quill fixed Banki with a look that was equal parts warning and assessment.

"What you do with her determines what kind of man you are. And what kind of organisation you're building."

Banki considered Serris: frightened, compromised, useful because conscience had not yet been beaten out of her.

"I'm building an organisation that rewards useful people," he said. "Alive and grateful is often better than dead and silent."

"Then I suggest," she said, "that you handle tonight very carefully."

"I always do," Banki said.

He left the chapel with three names, a timeline, and the beginning of a plan that would, by morning, reshape the balance of power in the southern canal district.

The candle on the third shelf continued to burn. Patient. Steady. Eternal as only enchanted things can be.

Sable walked half a pace behind him, her boots making no more sound on the wet river stone than the mist itself. Under the shadow of the cooper's overhang, she stopped. Her hand went to the damp brick, fingers tracing the outline of a rusted iron hook where fish-skeins had once hung to dry.

“I didn’t build the cottage because I distrusted your numbers, Banki,” she said. Her voice was flat, dry, stripped of anything like an apology. “I built it because I’ve seen what happens to people who don't have a second key.”

Banki turned. His polymorph face was still, his mind-sense brushing against the tight, iron-bound perimeter of her thoughts. The structure of her mind was always a pleasure to inspect—no loose threads, no erratic surges of hope, just cold walls and clean hinges. “A professional precaution,” he said.

“A lesson,” Sable corrected. “One I paid for in the south, in Oakhaven, before the Greybridge road. Before I knew what a ledger smelled like.” She stared into the dark canal, where a bloated cabbage floated in the black water, rolling sluggishly in the wake of a distant coal barge. “I was sworn to House Vaelen. Not the main branch—the third son, a soft-handed boy named Jarek who thought the world could be managed with good manners and high walls. He was nineteen, and he had a habit of writing bad poetry about the sea. He was my charge. I took his coin, and in return, I kept his throat intact. I spent four years learning the names of every man who looked at him too long in the markets, every cousin who stood too close to his glass at feast-days.”

She let her breath out, a pale plume in the cold air. “I thought the contract was the foundation. I thought because his father had signed the parchment under the seal of the Council, the Council would keep the terms. I believed in the structure. I was young enough to think that because I had never broken an oath, the oath itself had some kind of physical weight that could stop a blade. But honor is a cheap paint, Banki. It washes off the moment the weather turns expensive.”

“Go on,” Banki said. He did not ping her mind telepathically; he preferred to let her shape the delivery.

“The Guild of Scribes squeezed the docks,” she said, her eyes narrowing in the grey dark. “A quiet blockade. No ships burned, just papers delayed, permits lost, cargo rotting in the holds while the lawyers argued. House Vaelen was drowning in interest. The father needed a trade concession—a three-year exemption on the salt tax—and the Guild wanted a leverage point to break the family's vote on the Council. They wanted Jarek. Not to kill him—not at first. To hold him, to keep the father’s hand steady when the votes were cast.”

She paused, her fingers tightening on the iron hook until the rust stained her skin like old blood. “The father didn’t fight. He didn’t even hire extra steel. He bargained. He signed a new warrant—a private transfer under a false shipping description. And the Guild used my own security routes, the ones I had mapped to keep the boy safe, to deliver him to the collectors. They told me it was a routine transfer to the summer estate. They used my signature on the itinerary to make him walk out of his chambers without his personal guard. He didn't suspect a thing. He looked at me, he smiled, he said he’d bring back some fresh lavender for my quarters. I stood on the landing and watched him walk down the stairs. I watched him hand his cloak to the collectors because he trusted the paper I had approved.”

The water clicked against the canal wall, a rhythmic, wet sound like a tongue against teeth.

“They found him three days later in the salt-pans,” Sable said. “The collectors had been careless with the sedative. Or perhaps they simply didn't care. His lungs were full of brine, his skin pickled white by the flats. The Council got their exemption. The father got his margins. And I learned that a contract is only as strong as the teeth of the person enforcing it. If you don't have a place where the Scribes cannot write, you don't own your life. You're just a line in someone else's inventory.”

Banki studied the line of her jaw, the dry efficiency of her breath. There was no grief in her mind—she had processed the loss years ago, turning the pain into cold, structural reinforcement. The memory was not a wound; it was a wall.

“A logical conclusion,” Banki said. “Redundancy is the first rule of containment. One must always have a reserve that does not appear on the main ledger.”

“Exactly,” Sable said, her hand leaving the brick. She wiped the rust from her fingers onto her dark leather brigandine. “Sablehook is your design, Banki. It’s a good system. I run it because it works. But the cottage is my exit. If the ledger ever burns, I don't intend to stay in the ashes.”

“A wise preference,” Banki said, turning back toward the lane. “Let us ensure the ledger does not burn. The Scribes of Blackwater Quay will find that our contracts have significantly more teeth than those of Oakhaven.”

## The Rank Trials

The Sablehook property's isolation cell had been purpose-built for occasions like this.

It was a room with no windows, no light sources, and no features that could provide comfort, orientation, or hope.

Banki stood in the corner opposite the door, invisible in the darkness that was his natural element. Greater Shroud wrapped him in absolute imperceptibility. Mind-Sense ran at full operational depth, three keyword hooks armed—Steal, Kill, Lie—waiting for the triggers that would flag a subject's thoughts for special attention.

The prisoners had been prepared.

Eight selected captives waited in the prisoner sack: four dwarves, four elves, eight different architectures of fear. It was enough for the Shadows to learn that interrogation was not one skill but a family of skills, each calibrated to a different kind of resistance.

Sable entered the cell first. She moved through the darkness with the unhurried confidence of a woman who owned it, and when she stopped in the centre of the room, the darkness seemed to arrange itself around her like a garment—not because of magic, but because Sable had the kind of presence that made the environment defer to her rather than the reverse.

"Bring the first subject," she said.

Shadow One escorted the first prisoner in: a dwarf, male, middle-aged, with the thick-fingered hands of a mason and the suspicious eyes of someone who had been drinking in a tavern one moment and regaining consciousness in a dark room the next. He was blindfolded. His wrists were bound behind his back. His gait was the shuffling, uncertain stumble of a man navigating unfamiliar terrain without the use of his hands or eyes, which was precisely the kind of disorientation that Sable's interrogation protocols were designed to exploit.

The blindfold came off.

The dwarf blinked. The single lantern that Sable had lit—positioned behind her, so that her face was shadow and her silhouette was authority—threw hard-edged shadows across the room that turned every surface into a potential threat and every corner into a potential hiding place for things that the dwarf's imagination was already populating with terrors considerably worse than reality.

Sable didn't speak immediately. She let the silence do its work: the oppressive, salt-deadened silence of a room where sound went to die, where the dwarf's own breathing was the loudest thing he could hear, where the absence of external stimulus forced his mind to turn inward and begin the slow, corrosive process of manufacturing its own fear.

Ten seconds. Twenty. The dwarf's jaw tightened. His thoughts—which Banki read from his invisible corner like a man reading a newspaper over someone's shoulder—cycled through the classic stages of captive psychology: denial (this isn't happening), anger (when I get free I'll kill every one of them), bargaining (if I tell them what they want maybe they'll let me go), and the beginning of depression (I'm never getting out of here).

Sable spoke.

"You were taken from the Silver Anchor tavern at approximately nine fifteen last evening. You were drinking alone. You had three ales and a whisky. You paid with a clipped sovereign that the barman noticed but chose not to mention because you tip well and you don't cause trouble."

The dwarf's thoughts went cold. Not with fear—dwarven fear was a slow, deep thing, like water freezing from the bottom of a lake—but with the sudden, sharp realisation that the person standing in front of him knew things she should not know, which meant that she had been watching him before he was taken, which meant that he was not a random victim but a selected target, and targets are selected for reasons, and reasons implied purpose, and purpose implied that whatever was about to happen had been planned.

Planning was worse than impulse. Impulse you could survive through luck. Planning you survived only if the planner allowed it.

"Shadow One," Sable said, without turning from the dwarf. "Begin your extraction. Surface information first: name, occupation, daily routine, known associates. You have ten minutes."

Shadow One stepped forward. He was good—Banki had identified his talent early, had recommended him for the extraction track—but he was raw, and rawness showed in the way he positioned himself (too close to the subject, within headbutt range) and the way he opened his questioning (too many questions too fast, giving the subject information about what the interrogator valued by the urgency with which he pursued specific topics).

Banki watched. Read the dwarf's thoughts. Catalogued the dwarf's resistance strategies (stonewalling, deliberate misdirection through truthful-but-irrelevant answers, the classic dwarven technique of answering questions with questions). And he waited for the moment when Shadow One would reach the limit of his current skill and would need to be shown what the next level looked like.

That moment came seven minutes in.

The dwarf had given his name—Kargrim—and his occupation—stonemason—and had then proceeded to wrap every subsequent answer in enough contextual padding that Shadow One was drowning in detail without gaining useful intelligence.

Sable caught Banki's eye—or rather, caught the corner of the room where she knew Banki was standing invisible—and tilted her head a fraction of a degree. The signal meant: your turn.

Banki dropped his invisibility.

He appeared in the corner of the room like a man stepping out of a shadow that shouldn't have been deep enough to hide a man. Kargrim's eyes found him, widened, and his thoughts spiked with a new and different kind of fear—the fear not of the unknown but of the suddenly-known, the terror that comes from realising that someone has been watching you from a place you didn't know was occupied.

Banki crossed the room in four steps and crouched in front of the dwarf, bringing their eyes level. The distance between their faces was eighteen inches. Close enough to smell the dwarf's ale-sour breath. Close enough to watch the capillaries in his eyes dilate with the autonomic response of a mammalian brain recognising a predator.
