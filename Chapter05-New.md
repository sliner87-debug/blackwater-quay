## The Dry Kiln

Rain hammered the cobblestones of the Lower Ward, a relentless, freezing deluge that washed the city’s filth into the overflowing gutters. The downpour offered a perfect shroud for a man who did not wish to be tracked. Banki moved through the black, slick streets with deliberate anonymity, his midnight-weft cloak absorbing both the ambient light of the gas lamps and the relentless chill of the storm. He did not hurry. Haste bred attention, and attention in this district invited a blade in the ribs. 

He paused before a sagging, soot-stained storefront. A rusted iron sign hung by a single chain, groaning rhythmically in the wind. *The Dry Kiln.* It masqueraded as a mundane smithy serving the merchant class—horseshoes, cart axles, heavy iron nails by the pound. A necessary lie for the tax collectors and the casual city watchmen. 

Stepping out of the rain and into the shop, the atmosphere shifted with brutal immediacy. Heat rolled up from the basement forge in staggering, suffocating waves, baking the moisture from the air before it could reach the lungs. It tasted of sulfur, rapidly cooled iron, and the sharp, metallic tang of desperate, clandestine enterprise. The front room was cramped, cluttered with barrels of low-grade iron scrap and racks of unremarkable tools. 

Banki ignored the mundane trappings. His attention locked onto the abjuration wipe-line—a faint, shimmering distortion in the air, humming with a low-frequency vibration that rattled the teeth. It hung just behind the heavy canvas curtain separating the front counter from the staff-only depths. The ward told the truth of this place. No one spent that kind of gold on anti-scrying defenses to protect horseshoes. 

He stepped to the scarred wooden clerk’s counter, his boots making no sound against the grit-covered floorboards. He stood motionless. Every line of his posture, every subtle angle of his shoulders, broadcast the exact, calculated weight of a man possessing dangerous money and absolutely zero patience for amateur hour. 

A clerk emerged from the shadows near the curtain. He was young, thin, and sweating profusely in the oppressive heat. His hands lacked the deep, blackened calluses of a true smith; they were ink-stained, twitchy. A front man. A disposable buffer between the real talent in the back and the street. 

"Custom commission," Banki said. His voice barely rose above the roar of the subterranean furnace, yet it carried a lethal, unyielding resonance that demanded absolute attention. "Heat-proof and ward-clean."

He dropped the necessary bait, crafting the lie with mathematical precision, layering the black-market terminology to establish immediate dominance. "It's for a regulated acquisition circuit out of Blackwater Quay. Private facility. Scheduled auctions. Strict escort rules. We build a public viewing day next week. I require work that doesn't light up every ward-sense in the city when a prospective buyer walks past the cages with an active monocle."

The clerk’s retail smile, a flimsy construction of customer service and underlying fear, slipped immediately. The muscles around his eyes tightened, calculating the sheer risk versus the astronomical profit of such a request. A surface thought rippled outward from the young man's skull, unshielded and loud, slamming into Banki’s waiting mental snare: *He knows the words. Ward-clean. Auction circuit. Not a kid. He’s syndicate. Or worse.*

"Ward-clean is... specialized," the clerk murmured, wiping a bead of sweat from his chin. He leaned forward, attempting to lower his voice to a conspiratorial whisper. "Heat-proof is easier. We can do alchemically treated slag-iron for heat. But ward-clean requires silver-dust quenching and a localized null-field during the hammer work. What exactly is the item?"

Banki bypassed the question entirely. The clerk was irrelevant. The watcher hiding behind the heavy canvas staff curtain required handling first. Banki had felt the secondary presence the moment he crossed the threshold—a dense, quiet mass of intent lurking in the blind spot. 

He feathered a telepathic probe forward. He did not hammer against the hidden mind; he slipped his awareness out like a silken thread, sliding past the clerk's noisy, panicking surface thoughts. The probe tapped the quiet mind lurking behind the curtain like a tuning fork striking obsidian. 

The watcher resisted with immediate, reflexive meditative discipline. The mental architecture snapped into a blank surface technique, a smooth, featureless wall of gray thought designed specifically to shed casual scrying and low-level mind-reading. Non-magical security. A physical enforcer, likely a former pit-fighter or a disgraced legionnaire, waiting in the gloom with a loaded hand-crossbow, ready to trigger an evacuation protocol or bury a bolt in Banki's neck if the conversation turned sour. 

A threat to amateurs. Entirely irrelevant to Banki. 

He pulled his awareness back, leaving the enforcer's mental shields unbreached but perfectly, intricately mapped. He knew the man’s height by the angle of his intent, his weapon by the cold, metallic visualization resting at the forefront of his motor-control center. 

Banki moved. He slipped past the clerk in a single, fluid motion that defied his size, parting the heavy canvas curtain with his hood drawn, stepping through the abjuration wipe-line. The ward washed over him, a static prickle against his skin that tried and failed to strip away his active enchantments. 

The back workshop was a cavern of clandestine industry. The smell of ink, clean soap, and hot oil permeated the gloom. It was a tight, hyper-efficient space. Every tool rested in a specific, recessed groove, positioned to be hidden fast in the event of a raid. A massive forge dominated the far wall, burning with a strange, blue-tinged chemical fire. 

A voice cut from the gloom near the far bench, raspy and hardened by years of breathing metal dust. "Sit. Don't touch anything. Speak like you paid."

Banki threw his mind forward like a weighted net, casting a wide, oppressive shadow over the room. The crafter’s mental architecture fortified itself instantly, throwing up barriers of jagged, pragmatic thought. But the edges yielded just enough to reveal the dominant instinct buried deep within the man's psyche: survival through structure. This workshop bled anxiety. They were desperate for a protected supply channel, drowning under the protection fees of the lesser street gangs. 

"Containment hardware," Banki commanded, letting the authority of the shadow network, the cold, blood-soaked reality of the underworld, bleed into his voice. He didn't sit. He loomed. "Collar fittings, heat-proof and ward-clean. Cage reinforcements. Transport lock collars. Field-tolerant. Signature-clean. The iron must not hold a single trace of the forge's magic. If a prospective buyer runs a divination sweep, I want these collars returning the exact resonance of a standard cobblestone."

The crafter stepped fully into the light of the blue forge. He was a slab of a man, his skin a patchwork of pale burn scars and intricate, black-inked runes. "Timeframe?"

"Seventy-two hours." 

The crafter let out a harsh, barking laugh. "For ward-clean iron? You're out of your mind. The quenching process alone takes—"

Banki laid down the contract terms like physical blades on the workbench, cutting off the protest. "Fifty percent upfront. In platinum trade bars." 

He reached beneath his cloak and withdrew a heavy, leather-wrapped bundle, dropping it onto the scored wood. The impact produced a dense, dull thud that silenced the room. 

"A twenty percent bonus for speed, delivered within the seventy-two hours," Banki continued, his voice an icy, unstoppable current. "Another twenty percent bonus for absolute, zero magical residue upon delivery. And, most importantly, a protected supply channel for rare reagents. I control the docks. You will no longer pay the Rot-Tooth syndicate for your charcoal and silver-dust. You will pay my people, at a fraction of the cost, and the shipments will arrive unmolested."

The crafter stared at the leather bundle. The math spun furiously behind his eyes. Banki watched the thoughts crystallize. 

"The cost," Banki said softly, leaning into the silence, "is absolute exclusivity. The Dry Kiln becomes a supervised node within my network. You take no outside jobs without my explicit, written approval. My shadows control your access points. If you freelance, if you forge a single dagger for a street rat without routing it through my ledger... the contract ends. And you are removed from the node. Permanently."

The crafter's mind crunched the variables. The allure of the platinum warred with the terror of the commitment. Slowly, the chaotic fear settled into a cold, unbreakable law: *Don't freelance. Don't die. Survive.* 

"Understood." The crafter finally turned away from the platinum, meeting Banki's eyes. The submission was complete. "Name the outpost lead and the payment method."

"Turnkey commands the perimeter. Whisperstamp handles the counter-intelligence. Outside jobs route through them. They don't watch your hands. They aren't interested in your hammering technique. They watch your door." Banki flicked the edge of the leather bundle, exposing the gleaming edge of a stamped platinum bar. The metal caught the blue light of the forge. "Deliver the hardware. We have teeth to cage, and I refuse to let them chew through subpar iron."

## The Eldritch Hour

Brine, bone-ash grout, and the heavy, ozone tang of captive violence hung suffocatingly thick in Sablehook’s subterranean void. 

The descent into the lower levels always felt like stepping into the throat of a dying god. The temperature plummeted, the dampness clinging to the skin like a second layer of clothing. The walls, constructed from massive, seamless blocks of magically-fused granite, wept slow, dirty tears of condensation. 

The main viewing lane stretched into the gloom, a cavernous corridor split by a twenty-foot standoff line painted in dark, coarse alchemical grit across the floor. On either side, massive iron enclosures, reinforced with spell-forged steel and intricate abjuration matrixes, turned unspeakable horrors into crisp, terrifying silhouettes. The backlighting within the cages rendered every spine, every claw, every twitching appendage in sharp relief, allowing visual inspection without requiring a fatal approach to the bars. 

Sable walked at the head of the trio, her boots striking the stone with militant precision. She stopped at the first heavy timber post, pressing two fingers against the soft wax seal on the log board, checking the perimeter integrity with paranoid thoroughness. The seal held. 

"Owner-only," she stated, her voice echoing flatly down the corridor. She did not look at the cages. She looked at the architecture holding them. "Twenty-foot standoff maintained at all times. No stress tests. No tapping the glass. Your rules, Banki. Enforced to the letter."

Darric fell into step beside Banki. The veteran soldier walked with his hands clasped firmly behind his back, a deliberate posture designed to prevent any reflexive reaching or defensive posturing. He didn't scan the pacing shadows within the iron. Instead, his eyes tracked the hinge plates, the structural integrity of the latch brackets, the depth of the anchoring bolts driven into the stone. He was evaluating the cage, not the monster. 

The first enclosure held the Reedsinger. Designated NMD-RS-01. 

A faint, sickly residue of failed enchantment clung to the iron bars of the cage—the desperate, rotting stain of prior handlers who had attempted to force compliance through brute-force mental domination. The creature itself radiated no magic. It possessed no glowing eyes, no aura of visible power. It sat crouched in the center of the cell, a gaunt, elongated horror wrapped in tatters of its own molted, translucent skin. 

Yet, its attention possessed a crushing, physical weight. 

It didn't pace the perimeter like a caged wolf. It didn't thrash against the bars. It remained utterly motionless, its obsidian eyes locked onto the trio. It tracked Banki's breath, measuring the expansion of his ribcage. It mapped the microscopic motor pulses of Darric's shifting weight as the soldier balanced on his heels. It listened, with terrifying acuity, to the precise cadence of Sable's heartbeat.

A raw, jagged transmission pushed against Banki's mind, a psychic bleed that felt like cold needles scraping against the inside of his skull: *pattern... cadence... opening... opening... opening.*

Banki tightened his mental shields, hardening his surface thoughts to impenetrable iron. "Learner-predator," he said, keeping his voice dead flat, devoid of any emotional fluctuation that the creature could analyze. "It isn't planning a mindless charge. It's mapping the mechanical lock of our attention. It is calculating our reaction times. Once it models a handler's tells—a flinch, a hesitation, a dominant hand preference—it becomes statistically impossible to contain. It will move perfectly in the blind spots of your reaction."

"Metronome noise," Darric replied immediately, his tone dropping to a featureless, tactical drone. He understood the assignment. "We install clockwork white-noise generators in the corridor. Break the acoustic rhythm mapping. We rotate handlers every shift, and we rotate approach angles randomly. We never let it learn a patrol routine. If it cannot predict the environment, it cannot formulate a strike."

Banki gave a single, rigid nod. The protocol was solid. He broke eye contact with the Reedsinger, feeling the psychic pressure immediately attempt to latch onto the back of his neck as he turned away. He ignored it. Next cage. 

The Reed-Mirror Serpent. Designated UNQ-RS-02. 

The enclosure appeared empty at first glance. A massive terrarium of thick, alchemically hardened glass and heavy stone. Banki drove a sharp, focused mental probe against the glass, seeking a life sign. 

The mind skated off. 

It was a frictionless awareness. There was no resistance, no shield, merely an absolute lack of purchase. Inside the cage, a localized illusion wash distorted the air, wrapping the creature in a second, constantly shifting skin. The ambient light bent and fractured around the serpent's scales. The reflections on the enclosure walls warped, showing the serpent in one corner for half a breath, before the image snapped back to reality, revealing the creature coiled directly against the front glass. 

Looking at it directly induced an immediate, stabbing headache behind the eyes. The visual cortex rebelled against the impossible geometry of the light. 

*Don't look straight.* The surface thought from the creature sat cold and glassy in the air, a predatory taunt. 

"Slick mind," Banki noted, rubbing his temple to dispel the creeping nausea. "It projects a constant, localized refraction field. It's waiting for the room to blink. Waiting for the eye to trust the illusion over the reality. If you open that cage based on where you think it is, it's already wrapped around your throat."

Next enclosure. The Shatterglass Lurker. 

The creature undeniably occupied the space behind the heavy bars, yet direct attention refused to anchor on it. Banki tried to focus on its silhouette, but his vision literally slid off the angles of its body, pulling his eye violently toward the empty corners of the cage. It was a walking blind spot, a creature that evolved to edit itself out of the prey's visual processing. 

"Perception trap," Darric said, his jaw tight. He was looking at the floor directly in front of the cage, refusing to engage with the visual anomaly. "This is a nightmare for a tactical unit. This breaks teams, not cages. Someone says 'it's on the left,' someone else says 'no it isn't, it's right in front of us,' and a third person steps closer to the bars to settle the argument. The confusion breeds frustration. The frustration breeds movement. Then they die."

"Keep it boring," Banki instructed, his eyes tracing the anchoring bolts instead of the monster. "Throw raw meat in through the automated chute. Do not engage. Not for sale by default. We only open negotiations for this asset if the offer is exorbitant, and only to verified buyers who demonstrate an understanding of cognitive hazards."

Down the lane, the Displacer Beast paced in short, hyper-efficient loops. Six legs padded silently against the stone. Its twin, spiked tentacles whipped the air with the crack of a slaver's scourge. It didn't watch them. It marched the perimeter of its cage with the absolute, simmering malice of a hunter denied its killing field. The light around it seemed to lag, creating a nauseating trailing effect with every step. 

"Displacers punish routine even worse than the Reedsinger," Darric said, his voice carrying the grim authority of a man who had seen squads shredded by the beasts in the jungle campaigns. "Rotate approach lanes and timings. Keep the inside of the cage completely clean. No dangling straps, no loose chains, no play targets. If you give it something to strike, it will practice its angles. If you want auction stability, you condition the staff to be flawless. You cannot condition the beast."

They moved into the quarantine zone, a separate, heavily reinforced wing separated by an airlock. Here, the air smelled of stale salt and raw fish. 

Kessa, the beast-master recruit, ran the Transition Tank. It was a massive, sunken pool where brackish water lapped against the dressed stone edges. She stood on an elevated gantry, holding a long, rigid pole. An inert bait bundle—a densely packed brined meat analogue heavily soaked in fish oils, entirely lacking any fresh blood splash that might trigger a mindless feeding frenzy—hung on a thin line just above the waterline. 

The dark, torpedo-like shape beneath the surface rose deliberately. It didn't thrash. It didn't surge. It glided upward, a shadow detaching from the depths. It brushed the bait bundle with the hardened edge of its snout, testing the texture, testing the contact. 

One controlled, explosive snap upward took the meat cleanly from the line. The jaws sheared through the thick sinew without a sound. The creature sank back into the black water without a thrash. Without a single ripple disturbing the surface. 

"Trainable," Banki murmured, watching the perfect, disciplined execution of the strike. "Give the hatchlings choices. Construct the enclosures with multiple biomes. Warm dry hide-boxes. Humid, mossy edges. Shallow waders. Let them choose their preferred environment, let them establish a territory, before we assign them a combat purpose. A comfortable asset is a predictable asset."

Later, before the shift change, Sable intercepted the staff muster line in the main preparation room. Thirty handlers, guards, and logistics runners stood in rigid formation. Sable stood at the front, positioned exactly where every set of eyes could see her empty hands. She projected total, unassailable authority. 

"Curiosity kills operations," she announced. Her voice wasn't loud, but it carried the brutal, inevitable weight of a falling executioner's axe. "If you speculate about the inventory in the lower levels, you become inventory for our enemies. You make yourselves targets for rival syndicates looking for a weak link. If you gossip in the taverns, you get one warning. Second time, you are reassigned away from the sensitive lanes permanently. You will clean the latrines until you rot."

She paced the line, her eyes locking onto the younger recruits. "If a prospective buyer fishes for information, you repeat the approved script. You know nothing beyond the public auction catalog. If a Shadow from Banki's network fishes for information... you shut it down just the same. My lane, my rules. Your loyalty is to the structural integrity of this compound. Am I understood?"

Silence reigned in the stone room. The policy transformed from mere words into a physical atmospheric pressure, pressing down on the shoulders of the staff. Banki watched from the doorway, his face a mask, deeply appreciative of the brutal efficiency. The clever ones in the ranks immediately stopped being clever. They became cogs in Sable's machine. Exactly as required.

## Crown Citizenship

The kingdom inaugurated itself not with the raising of a blood-soaked banner or the crowning of a monarch, but with a ledger.

Mara's iron-bound book sat open on the high salvage desk, its pages thick as hide, its spine reinforced with brass. The ink she'd mixed that morning stank of iron gall, bone-ash, and something older — a sharp, copper undertone that caught in the back of the throat. Banki had bled into the batch before dawn, three precise cuts across his left palm, the blood stirring into the pigment while the mixture was still warm. The magic threaded through the liquid the way dye threads through water: invisibly, completely, irreversibly.

When a citizen signed their name in that ink, the act completed a circuit. Their true name — written in their own hand, pressed into parchment mixed with blood and bone-ash — anchored a locator thread to their identity. The magic didn't command. It didn't bind a mind, edit a memory, or force an oath of fealty. That was the magic of tyrants and slavers.

Instead, Banki had woven a soul-address into the ink. A locator thread shaped to resemble the psychological warmth of recognition — the way a familiar hearth smells after months on the road. The magic settled into the signatory's identity, a quiet, thrumming beacon anchored to their true name, remaining active until extreme distance, profound fear, or death severed the connection. A modified Asmodean contract working, designed to find rather than to own.

Tovin Vale, a narrow-shouldered, sharp-eyed boy rescued from the Belowmarket fighting pits, sat on a wooden crate near the kitchen door. He was systematically testing the mechanical resistance of a new brass door latch with a child-sized, heavily worn screwdriver. Mara had written his name for him that morning, his fingers too small and too scarred to hold the pen steady. He'd watched the ink dry on the page with the focused suspicion of a creature evaluating a snare.

Tovin did not look up from his work as Banki approached. The boy possessed the hyper-vigilance of the hunted.

"If I get lost," Tovin asked, his voice entirely devoid of childhood innocence, sounding more like a veteran negotiating terms, "does my name in that book find me?"

Banki stopped, calibrating his response with extreme care. Children detected rhetorical traps and half-truths long before adults recognised them. Lie to the boy now, and the trust was gone forever.

"If the thread remains intact," Banki said, keeping his tone conversational, devoid of pity. "If your name remains your own, and your mind is whole. The magic helps me follow a thread through the city. A beacon in the dark. But it cannot weave a new thread after the world has violently cut the old one away. Heavy warding or deep water will fade the signal."

Tovin considered this, the screwdriver pausing in the brass mechanism. He tucked his narrow shoulders inward, a defensive posture. "So it is not a leash."

"Correct," Banki stated flatly. "The magic carries no command. It forces you to do nothing. It requires no debt to be paid. It finds the door. It is not a collar around your neck."

The sharp, uncompromising strike of Sable's boots against the stone floor announced her arrival before she spoke. She strode into the hall, the heat from the kitchens below doing nothing to thaw the glacial set of her jaw. She pointed a single, leather-gloved finger toward the open ledger, her voice low, vibrating with an intensity that silenced the room.

"You bled into the ink." Not a question. The copper smell had told her everything. "You wove a leash into the contract they sign with. Every person who puts their name in that book is wearing your thread without knowing what it costs."

Banki leaned back against the stone wall, his expression unreadable, though a spark of genuine irritation flared in his chest. "If I wished for blind, mindless obedience, Sable, I possess far more direct instruments than penmanship. I could rewrite their loyalties while they slept."

"That is not an answer," Sable snapped, stepping into his personal space, entirely unafraid of the shadows clinging to his cloak. "That is a threat disguised as restraint. You hid tracking magic in the ink they use to sign a *voluntary* oath. *Voluntary*, Banki. The word loses its meaning when the pen is rigged."

"It is an assurance of methodology," Banki countered, his voice dropping to a dangerous, silken whisper. "The world outside these walls views them as meat. I am ensuring the meat has a homing beacon."

Mara, sitting behind the ledger, pointedly ignored the escalating tension. Her silver-nibbed pen scratched against the heavy parchment with lethal, administrative precision.

"Crown Citizenship," Mara announced, her voice cutting through the argument like a scalpel. "Structured on the precedents of the Asmodean Pact, modified for mortal application. Voluntary oaths only. Named, codified rights. Named, absolute duties. The Crown guarantees food, fair wages, fortified shelter, medical care, and a strict, unbreakable prohibition against compelled mind alteration for any loyal citizen."

She looked up from the page, her pen hovering. Her gaze moved from Sable to Banki and back. "If the locator ink stays, it gets disclosed. Page one. Above the signature line. They know what the ink does before the pen touches the page." The pen tapped once against the brass lip of the inkwell. "Or I stop mixing the batches."

Sable turned from Banki, leaning over the desk, her eyes scanning the wet script. "Add a clause. Right there, under the medical provisions. Forbidding laboratory work on Crown Citizens. No experimental alchemical treatments. No testing new warding techniques on the staff. Even if they offer verbal consent while frightened or desperate. *Especially* then. Children under Crown protection are never leverage. We will not use them against heroes, we will not use them against our enemies, and we will not use them as proof of our own righteousness to the city."

Mara nodded, the pen flying across the page. She drafted the Crown Contract before the morning shift ended. The document offered no gentle pardons for past crimes, nor did it promise utopia. Treason carried brutal, fatal consequences. The oaths retained their absolute, magical weight. But the ink bound the Crown just as tightly as it bound the citizen: food preceded work, fair wages preceded luxury, rescue preceded punishment. No citizen of Sablehook would ever be spent as tactical fodder.

And on the first page, in Mara's precise hand, the disclosure:

*The ink of this ledger carries a locator thread bound to your true name. It allows the Crown to find you. It does not allow the Crown to command you. If you wish to sign with unbound ink, request it. Your citizenship will not be diminished.*

At the bottom of the contract, framed by Mara's flowing script, one single line anchored the entire architectural philosophy of Sablehook:

*The Crown claims service. It does not claim the soul.*

By dusk, the heavy ledger tested its own boundaries.

Hest, a rescued woman from the warehouse district who had eaten twice in the hall that day, slowly approached the dais. She stared at the open book — at the disclosure on the first page, at the dark, copper-smelling ink in the well — her hands trembling so violently she had to clasp them together to hide the shake. Her eyes darted toward the iron-banded doors.

She refused Crown Citizenship.

She requested her guaranteed three days' wage, a wrapped meal from the kitchen, and the immediate opening of the front door. She wanted to return to the streets.

The ancient, suffocating, predatory instinct flared within Banki. It rose from the absolute depths of his power, a roaring, monstrous urge to hoard the vulnerable. To protect by proximity. To cage the weak, lock the iron doors, enforce safety through absolute dominance so the grinding gears of the city could never devour them again. He could freeze her in place with a single word. He could alter her fear, make her see the walls as sanctuary instead of prison.

Sable stood completely still near the hearth, watching him. She didn't reach for a weapon. She simply watched the shadow war happening behind Banki's eyes.

Mara, expressionless, turned the heavy ledger past the Voluntary Oath page, preparing to log a departure.

Banki forced his fingers to uncurl. The physical effort required to restrain his magic made his joints ache. He exhaled slowly, a long, shuddering breath, violently shoving the predatory, hoarding instinct back into the dark cage of his own mind. He locked it down.

"Pay her," Banki rasped, his voice sounding like grinding stones.

Mara handed over the silver coins. The kitchen packed the food in waxed paper. Hest departed with the coin, the bread, and no thread of magic binding her name to the ledger on the desk. The heavy iron gate clanged shut behind her, the metallic boom echoing hollowly through the damp courtyard.

Banki stared at the sealed door, his jaw clenched so tight his teeth ground together. "She will be dead within a month. The Rot-Tooth syndicate controls her old district. They will find the silver."

"Then she dies free," Sable replied softly, the harshness finally leaving her voice. She walked over, standing beside him in the gloom. "A room you cannot leave is a cage, Banki. No matter how much food is in the kitchen. Especially when someone powerful looks down at you and calls it protection."

Banki turned away from the door, looking back into the depths of the compound, listening to the faint, distant sounds of the creatures pacing in the subterranean levels. The teeth of his new kingdom were growing sharper every day. Now, they just needed to learn exactly who they were allowed to bite.
