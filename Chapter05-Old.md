"Kargrim," Banki said, his voice barely above a whisper. "You've been very cooperative. You've answered every question truthfully. And you have told Shadow One absolutely nothing useful."

A pause. Banki let it fill with the sound of the dwarf's breathing, which had accelerated from twelve breaths per minute to twenty.

"That stops now. Not because I'm going to hurt you—though I can, and I will if the situation requires it. It stops because I can hear what you're thinking. Right now. In this moment. I can hear you trying to decide whether I'm bluffing about the telepathy, and I can hear the part of your mind that already knows I'm not."

Kargrim's thoughts erupted: gods, he can—no, it's a trick—but he knew about the Silver Anchor—he knew about the clipped sovereign—NOBODY knew about the clipped sovereign—"The clipped sovereign," Banki said, reading the thought as it formed. "You've been shaving coins for three years. Small amounts. Consistent. Not enough to attract the kind of attention that gets people killed, just enough to build a nest egg for—" He paused, reading deeper, pushing past the surface panic into the structured memory beneath. "For your daughter. Shale. She's eleven. She wants to apprentice with the Bridge Warden's office, and the apprentice fee is two hundred gold, and you've saved one hundred and forty-seven."

The silence that followed was absolute.

Kargrim stared at Banki with the expression of a man who has just been shown the inside of his own skull and has discovered that it contains nothing he can hide.

"I'm not interested in your coins," Banki said. "I'm not interested in your daughter's apprenticeship. I'm interested in demonstrating to my people—" he gestured at Shadow One, who was watching with the wide-eyed attention of a student witnessing a master class "—what a real extraction looks like. You are a teaching aid. You will cooperate fully and honestly, you will answer every question that is asked of you, and in exchange, you will leave this room alive, unharmed, and with your memory of the specifics adjusted so that you cannot identify anyone involved or describe the location."

He smiled. It was the smile that wolves would wear if wolves could make promises.

"Your daughter will get her apprenticeship. Shale will never know any of this happened. And you will have a story that you can never tell anyone, about the night you met a man who could read minds, and who chose to let you live because you were more useful alive than dead."

Kargrim swallowed. His thoughts settled from chaos into the cold, clear calculation of a man who recognises that he has been beaten by something he cannot fight and that his best option—his only option—is to cooperate completely and hope that the predator keeps its word.

"Ask your questions," the dwarf said.

Banki stood. He looked at Shadow One.

"Resume," he said. "And this time, listen to what he's not saying as carefully as you listen to what he is."

He stepped back into the shadows and vanished.

The extraction continued for another forty minutes. Shadow One's technique improved visibly under the pressure of knowing that his employer was watching from somewhere he couldn't see, reading the subject's thoughts, and evaluating every question, every pause, every tactical decision against a standard that Shadow One was only beginning to understand.

By the time the session ended, Shadow One had extracted: Kargrim's complete professional network (fourteen contacts in the masonry trade, three of whom had ties to the Harbour Magistracy's construction permits office), a detailed map of the district's structural vulnerabilities (information that only a career stonemason would possess, and that was worth its weight in platinum for anyone planning to move through or beneath the city's architecture), and the name of a Magistracy clerk who had been accepting bribes in exchange for falsified building inspection reports—information that would give Banki leverage over the Magistracy's infrastructure division without the Magistracy ever knowing where the leverage had originated.

Banki promoted Shadow One to Tier-2 that night. The dwarf was memory-adjusted and released. Sable filed the intelligence into the growing archive of the ShadowNet's operational database.

Sable delayed promotion for one candidate whose answers had been useful but whose hands shook only after the prisoner looked away. 'Skill without timing becomes cruelty,' she said, and no one in the room mistook the correction for mercy.

The remaining seven prisoners were processed through similar sessions over the next four hours, each one serving as a teaching exercise for a different Shadow, each one yielding a different species of intelligence, each one demonstrating a different principle of the interrogation doctrine that Banki had developed over decades and that Sable had formalised into a teachable system.

By the time the last prisoner was returned to the sack, it was past midnight. The Shadows were exhausted, elated, and operating at a level of professional capability that they had not possessed twelve hours ago. Sable was satisfied, which she expressed by not criticising anyone—her highest form of praise. And Banki was standing in the cold yard outside Sablehook, looking at the fog, thinking about stamps and clerks and the woman named Serris who was, even now, preparing to hand the instrument of the Choir's logistical authority to someone who would use it to erase memories and steal lives.

Not tonight. Tonight, the stamp would find a different owner.

Banki checked his weapons. Adjusted his cloak. Let the Greater Shroud settle over him like a second skin.

And walked into the fog.

## The Wickline Stamp

Wickline Pier at night was a study in controlled darkness.

The pier extended into the southern canal like a skeletal finger pointing at something it didn't want to touch.

Banki moved along the pier's underside.

Not on it—beneath it. He clung to the support struts in his true form: a pixie, three inches of iridescent fury with wings folded flat against his back, fingers gripping salt-roughened timber with the adhesive strength of a creature that had been climbing things larger than itself since before humans had discovered fire.

Above him, through the gaps between planks, he could see the pier's surface in narrow slices of lamplight and shadow. Two figures waited near the end of the pier, separated by twenty feet and the careful body language of people who were going to pretend they had met by accident.

One was Serris.

The stamp was in the satchel.

The other figure was new. Male. Young—late twenties, with the lean build and the controlled movement patterns of someone who had received physical training that went beyond the standard "stand behind a desk and push papers" curriculum of the average bureaucratic functionary. His thoughts were structured, disciplined, and laced with the same mirror-glint Choir resonance that Banki had been cataloguing across multiple encounters. This was not a clerk. This was an operative—a Choir agent operating under clerical cover, sent to receive the stamp and carry it into the next phase of whatever the Choir was building in the southern canal district.

Between them, the pier was empty. The fog pressed in from all sides, reducing the world to a corridor of damp air and uncertain footing.

Banki waited. Patience was a weapon, and he wielded it the way he wielded all weapons: with precision, with discipline, and with the absolute certainty that the moment to strike would present itself to anyone willing to wait for it.

The exchange began.

Serris approached the operative with the reluctant, shuffling gait of a woman walking towards something she would rather run from. Her thoughts were a litany of self-recrimination and fear: ...just hand it over, just hand it over, don't look at his face, don't think about what they'll use it for, just hand it over and go home and hold your son and try to forget that you're part of this...

The operative reached for the satchel.

Banki struck.

Not with a blade—but with raw, industrialized High Magic. He unleashed his Eldritch Blast in its Screaming Skull form. The spectral, shrieking projectile of bone-dust and marrow-runes slammed into the operative's psychic shielding, shattering it.

The operative's eyes rolled back. His reaching hand went limp. His knees buckled. He fell to the planking with a sound like a sack of wet grain hitting a barn floor, and his consciousness winked out of Banki's telepathic field like a candle in a hurricane.

Serris screamed.

The sound was short and sharp and bitten off almost immediately—not because she'd regained control of herself but because Banki had already projected a telepathic message into her mind, overriding the panic with a command delivered in the voice of absolute authority:

*Stop. You are safe. The man who was sent to take the stamp is unconscious. I am the one your anonymous notes have been reaching. Do not scream again.*

Serris stopped screaming.

Banki reverted to his human form on the pier's surface, stepping into existence from the fog with the quiet theatricality that had become his operational signature: not invisible one moment and visible the next, but emerging from the mist as if the mist had always contained him and had simply chosen this moment to release him.

Serris stared. Her thoughts were a storm of conflicting impulses—run, stay, trust, don't trust, give him the satchel, hold onto the satchel, scream again, don't scream again—"Serris," Banki said. His voice was gentle. Not soft—Banki's voice was never soft—but calibrated with the precision of a musician tuning an instrument. This was a frightened woman, not an enemy combatant, and the tools appropriate to the situation were reassurance and control, not intimidation and force. "I received your notes. All of them. The one about Wickline. The one about the stamp rotation. The one about the third bell timeline."

"You're—" She swallowed. "You're from the—the shadow—"

"The ShadowNet. Yes." He stepped closer. Not threatening. Approaching the way you approach a startled horse: slowly, predictably, with your hands visible and your body language broadcasting non-aggression with every muscle. "My name is Banki. And I'm here to make you an offer that is considerably better than anything the Choir has ever given you."

Her thoughts cycled through the offer before he made it, because frightened minds are prophetic minds—they anticipate everything, imagine everything, prepare for every possible outcome simultaneously, and in doing so they broadcast the entire decision tree of their internal calculus to anyone capable of reading it.

Banki saw what she wanted. What she needed. What she feared.

"Your son's job at the salt-press is safe," he said, addressing her fears in the order she'd thought them. "The Choir's leverage over you through his employment ends tonight. I have people who will ensure that the salt-press operates under new management within the week—management that has no connection to the Choir and no interest in using employees as hostages."





Her eyes widened. Hope, raw and painful as an open wound.

"The stamp," he continued. "Give it to me. Not to the Choir, not to their operative, not to anyone. Give it to me, and I will use it to take apart the network that has been using you as a tool. When it's done—when the Choir's presence in the southern canal is dismantled—you'll receive a permanent position in my organisation's legitimate business front. Better pay. Better protection. And a guarantee that no one will ever use your son against you again."

Serris looked at the unconscious operative. She looked at Banki. She looked at the satchel in her arms.

She held it out.

Banki took it with both hands. The leather was warm from her body heat. Inside, he could feel the weight of the stamp—a small, dense object with the particular heft of bureaucratic authority made physical.

"Thank you, Serris," he said. And he meant it—not sentimentally, not emotionally, but with the genuine appreciation of a strategist practicing the Divine Calculus, who has just acquired a critical piece of infrastructure and intends to deploy it with devastating efficiency.

He touched the operative's temple. Mind-Sense drove deep, past the unconsciousness, into the structured memory that remained accessible even in a mind that had been temporarily switched off. He read everything: handlers, routes, code phrases, safe houses, the location of the Quiet Loft, the identity of Sub-Prefect Halvyr, the timetable for what the Choir called "the Second Arrival"—a reinforcement of psionic operatives scheduled to arrive in Blackwater Quay within seventy-two hours.

Then he adjusted the operative's memory. Not erased—adjusted. The operative would wake with a clear recollection of arriving at the pier, of waiting for Serris, and of being attacked by "three large men in dark hoods" who had beaten him unconscious and stolen the satchel. He would not remember Banki. He would not remember telepathy. He would not remember anything that could lead the Choir back to the ShadowNet.

Serris would remember everything, because Banki needed her to remember. Memory, in this case, was the chain that bound her loyalty—not through fear, but through gratitude. Through the understanding that someone had seen her suffering and had chosen to end it, and that the price of that liberation was a relationship that would last as long as it remained mutually beneficial.

Which, if Banki's plans unfolded as intended, would be a very long time.

He walked Serris off the pier and into the fog. Sable met them at the canal-side, a shadow among shadows, and took Serris to a safe house that the ShadowNet maintained for exactly this kind of extraction: a warm room, a locked door, a bed, and the assurance that the morning would be better than the night.

A sliver of Wickline sealing wax remained under one plank. Banki almost removed it, then left it where a very good tracker could find it and become certain of the wrong thing.

Banki stood alone on the pier and examined the stamp by the light of a dying gaslamp.

It was small. Iron. Heavy. Engraved with the three-spoke wheel of the Harbour Magistracy's transit authority, the official seal that validated cargo movement through Blackwater Quay's southern waterways. In the right hands—in his hands—it was a key that opened every locked canal gate, every restricted waterway, every controlled shipping lane in the district.

He turned it over. The metal was cold against his palm.

The Choir had owned this stamp for six weeks.

Now it was his.

Banki pocketed the stamp and walked into the fog.

Behind him, the gaslamp flickered and died, and the darkness that replaced it was absolute and patient and, for the first time in six weeks, owned by someone new.

## The Naming

By the time darkness properly took the city, the Gull & Gasket no longer sounded like a tavern below stairs. It sounded like the first draft of an institution.

Chains on wood. Bone-dust on stone. Boots crossing divided ground. The wet slap of packed cloth gags laid into a tray. Sable's voice separating men into tasks and temperaments the way a stern magistrate might separate the useful from the decorative. The cellar had been marked into Intake, Secure Storage, and Temporary Holding with lines thick enough to see in poor light and obey in panic. A table meant for butchered hogs served now as search and evidence station. Two old ale cradles had become restraint frames. A crate stack hid the prisoner sacks. Another held the artifacts taken from the Counting Room and the North Canal, wrapped and tagged not because Banki loved order for its own sake, but because unlabeled danger eventually matures into surprise.

He liked the room better for the ugliness.

Edric sent down broth and black bread for the shadows. No questions. That, too, was a promising sound.

The dark-sash prisoner from the canal lay bound in the holding corner with a cloth over his face and a bucket beside him for the practical consequences of terror. One of the half-stripped hymn stones rested on a bed of salt and scrap lead in the secure zone, its wrongness reduced but not gone. Above them all the tavern muttered in its ordinary voice, tankards, curse-laughter, the scrape of stools, a fiddler doing bad things to a melody. Headquarters, Banki thought, were defined less by flags and titles than by the fact that violence could proceed underneath domestic noise without either one interrupting the other.

He was still thinking that when resonance began.

It came first as a thin ache in the molars, then as a pressure in the air that made the candle flames lean though no door had opened. The isolated harmonic stone in storage answered with a faint whine. Somewhere out in the district a bell did not ring so much as begin to vibrate against the concept of ringing. The dusk check.

The cellar smelled of blood and ceremony and the particular chemical signature of loyalty being manufactured under pressure.

Banki had not invented the naming ritual. The shadows had taught it to themselves across generations: anonymity protects, but a chosen name makes a person easier to hold accountable and harder to erase.

The four Shadows stood in a line in the cellar's assessment zone—the same zone where Sable had evaluated them during the Rank Trials, the same chalk marks on the stone floor, the same single lantern throwing hard-edged shadows that turned the room into a theatre of darkness and light. They were different now than they had been during the trials. The trials had been about proving capability. The naming was about receiving consequence.

Banki stood before them. Not in his business clothes, not in the casual attire of the tavern manager that the surface world believed him to be. He wore the shadow-stitched cloak. The Mithral Chain Shirt gleamed at his collar. The Custom Eldritch Focus Rod was holstered at his forearm, visible, a reminder that the man conducting the ceremony was not merely an administrator but a weapon.

Sable stood behind the Shadows. Witness. Judge. The field commander whose approval opened rank and whose disapproval could end more than employment—a word that, in Sable's vocabulary, carried both of its possible meanings with equal weight.

"You have earned your names," Banki said. His voice was quiet. The cellar's salt-treated walls absorbed the sound and returned it as something denser, more intimate, a voice that existed only in the space between speaker and listener and that the world beyond the cellar's boundaries would never hear. "From this moment, the designations you carried during your probation are retired. You are no longer numbers. You are names. And names carry weight."

He looked at S1. The adaptive extractor. The man who had demonstrated, during the Rank Trials and in the field, the ability to read people the way Banki read minds—not through magic but through the trained observation of behaviour, through the cataloguing of tells and patterns, through the kind of social intelligence that made someone capable of extracting truth from silence.

"Shadow One. Your name is Wickshade."

The word landed in the cellar's stillness like a stone in deep water. Wickshade. A name that evoked shadow and flame simultaneously—the wick that cast the light and the shade that the light created. The duality was deliberate. Wickshade was the Shadow who illuminated by observing, who revealed by remaining hidden, whose greatest weapon was not what he did but what he noticed.

Wickshade straightened. The fractional adjustment in his posture—shoulders back, spine aligned, chin raised by a degree that was barely visible but that communicated, in the body language that Banki read as fluently as text, the acceptance of something that was both honour and obligation.

"Shadow Two. Your name is Greyslip."

Greyslip. The scout. The one who moved through environments the way water moved through sand—finding every gap, exploiting every opening, present everywhere and noticed nowhere. The name was a description and a prescription: grey for the colour of invisibility, slip for the quality of passage that left no trace.

"Shadow Three. Your name is Latchwarden."

Latchwarden was the analyst: precise, structured, and capable of holding several complicated states in mind without dropping the key.

"Shadow Six. Your name is Ironmote."

Ironmote.

Four names. Four identities. Four commitments spoken aloud beneath the Gull & Gasket, with Banki and Sable as witnesses.

Banki presented each Shadow with a recognition gift: enchanted tools chosen for the work they had already proven they could do. Wickshade received the merciful sap. Greyslip, the seeking hand crossbow. Latchwarden, the keen dagger inscribed To exploit the cracks. Ironmote, the thieves' tools that would make locks and traps confess faster.

Each gift carried the ShadowNet's mark. Each platinum bar made the lesson vulgar enough to be understood.

"These gifts are earned," Banki said. "They are tools of your trade. Use them well."

He looked at each of them. Read their surface thoughts—not because he distrusted them but because the naming ceremony was a moment of maximum emotional honesty, and honest emotions were data, and data was the currency of Banki's existence.

Wickshade gave him measured pride. Greyslip, relief. Latchwarden, focus already sharpening around the new title. Ironmote gave no words at all, only gratitude buried deep enough that it would show later in the work.

"Dismissed," Banki said. "Report to Sable for your updated field assignments."

Four Shadows filed out of the cellar, carrying their gifts and their names and the weight of commitment that both represented.

Sable remained.

"They'll do," she said.

"Yes. They will."

A pause. Sable's expression shifted by the fraction of a degree that constituted, in her emotional vocabulary, the transition from professional assessment to personal observation.

"You chose well," she said. "The names. They fit."

"Names always fit when you've read the mind they're meant for."

"That's cheating."

"That's efficiency."

The ghost of a smile. Sable's rarest expression—visible for perhaps half a second before the work mask reasserted itself with the automatic, trained reflex of a woman who had decided, long ago, that smiles were currency and that currency was not spent without purpose.

"Cheating," she repeated. And left.

Banki stood alone in the cellar and listened. On Mara's desk, the new names dried in black ink. Below stairs, the cellar held them like nails driven into old wood.

## Sablehook, With Its Teeth Still Growing

Branna insisted the first basket look ordinary. No gold light. No ceremony. Biscuits that looked like biscuits, because frightened people trusted flour faster than doctrine.

Sablehook had been a cold shed before it became an intention.

The compound squatted at Wharfside Row and the Fifth Canal's industrial spur: main hall, cold-storage annex, dock warehouse, courtyard, and two documented sub-levels that were already useful. Beneath those, Banki's Aether-Sight found the better truth: a dry void the old builders had either missed or buried.

"Large enough?" Sable asked.

"Large enough for everything."

He did not say laboratory first. Respectable men called rooms laboratories after deciding the work inside deserved respect. Banki cared more for function.

The front rooms got the lie: salvage, auctions, viewing rails, painted sign, clerk's counter. The rear got the truth: chain-points, reinforced stalls, blood-iron ribs, bone-ash grout, handler lanes, isolation rooms, and doors that locked for the right person from the right side.

Sable walked the walls with a builder's eye and a prisoner's suspicion. "Direction Four gets staff exits that open from inside. Handler bells on every lane. No pen without a killing angle and a rescue angle. And no creature moved in until someone who understands animals better than you has told you how it will try to kill us."

"You object to the premise?"

"I object to preventable funerals."

So the void became four Directions.

Direction One would teach routes to lie less convincingly: maps, false ways, travel arrays, emergency bridges. Direction Two would hold relics, residue, dead things not finished being problems. Direction Three would make wearable doctrine: bracers, harnesses, collar-plates, and command tools for ordinary hands near extraordinary danger. Direction Four would be living force.

"The room where mistakes become bodies," Sable said.

"Yes."

"Then the teeth learn who they are allowed to bite."

The first hybridisation board did not list weapons.

It listed jobs.

Compulsion defence for sleeping rooms. Recovery beasts whose purpose was care. Canal denial. Undercity hauling. Tunnel rescue. Road guardians. Caravan engines.

Banki wrote one phrase larger than the others.

CARAVAN LINE.

Sable looked from the words to his face. "That one is older than this building."

"Yes."

"Then it gets rules before it gets teeth."

Mara added: Every line receives a lawful purpose before it receives a body.

Darric added: Every body receives a handler before it receives a purpose.

Hesta Bramblewake, not yet officially hired and already structurally necessary, wrote: EVERY HANDLER GETS A WAY OUT.

Below that, in smaller script, Mara wrote a provisional name for the whole dangerous family of problems:

PROJECT SECOND BITE.

"Too theatrical," Sable said.

"It is an internal label," Mara replied. "Internal labels exist so that when someone asks why the floor is bleeding, we know which file to curse."

The empty corner where WAR-LINE was not yet written mattered later. At the beginning, even Banki did not name war as the dream.

The early notes kept older words because old words show where mistakes began. RhinoWhip. Dire RhinoWhip. DDR. Sablehook Beast. Direction 4 line. Banki let them remain in the archive, then wrote the name he intended the future to use.

Dire Displacer Rhino.

Plural: Dire Displacer Rhinos.

Sable looked at the board for a long time. "Then the teeth learn who they are allowed to bite."

The Direction Labs began not with a monster, but with jobs power might someday perform and several irritating people determined that power would not mistake itself for permission.

The property became Sablehook that afternoon.

Banki named it in front of her. Sable paused at the word, not softening, not thanking him, but recognising that for once an organisation had treated her as a name instead of a tool.

She did not let the recognition become softness in public. Instead she turned to the foremen and changed three door swings, two sightlines, and the entire watch rotation before Banki had finished enjoying the moment. By sunset, every worker knew two things: the new owner paid on time, and the woman whose name was on the building would notice if a hinge lied.

Mara arrived with the deed packet and a headache.

"The front can be a salvage concern," she said. "The auction business can be licensed. The containment rooms can be licensed if we pretend the city understands the word containment. The lower levels do not exist."

"An elegant legal architecture."

"A dangerous one. Legitimacy is not a blanket you throw over crime until it stops looking cold. It is a house. If you want the house to stand, every load-bearing lie needs a real beam behind it."

"And you can build that?"

Mara looked at Sablehook's courtyard, the chain crews, the hidden doors, the workers pretending not to listen, and the first rescued people eating from bowls near the kitchen wall because no one had told them yet where safety ended.

"I can build something people can point to without flinching," she said. "Whether that becomes refuge or prettier prison depends on whether you let the paper bind you too."

Three mornings later, Mara began the kingdom with breakfast.

She called it efficiency: predictable meal, predictable hour, one hall where orders and petitions could happen before work scattered everyone into danger. Then she put a high-backed salvage chair on a dais and dared everyone to pretend it was not a throne.

The kitchen sent biscuits first.

At first they were only flour from Crown stores, household salt, kitchen fat, Branna's hearth, and the stubborn love of staff who treated feeding damaged people as a form of defence. They were good: the first miracle and the only one anyone trusted.

Then people began noticing small things.

A bitten hand closed without the angry red heat that usually followed a cage-room wound. A night porter who had slept in jerks since the Choir cellars woke with his knife still under his pillow but no scream in his throat. Tovin Vale, a narrow-shouldered Belowmarket boy who sorted bent spoons for Branna because small useful work made him stand straighter, scraped both knees on the courtyard stone, ate breakfast while pretending not to cry, and by noon had forgotten which knee was supposed to hurt.

Tally began making columns.

Mara saw the columns and shut the ledger with two fingers.

"No rates in the public book," she said. "No miracle accounting anyone can use to price a citizen."

"It is data," Tally objected.

"It is liability with butter on it."

Branna, from the kitchen line, said, "If anyone calls my biscuits liability, they can eat ink."

Banki saw the deeper thing before anyone named it. The biscuits did not command or bind a mind. They marked belonging with a soul-address faint enough to look like comfort until blood, distance, fear, or death tried to make His unfindable.

Children received the blessing first and most gently. No oath pulled at them. No clause waited behind their teeth. They slept warmer, healed cleaner, and woke with fear made smaller without being made false.

Tovin Vale raised his hand from the kitchen-end bench as if breakfast had become a class and he had found the dangerous question first. "If I get lost," he asked, "can the biscuit find me?"

Banki answered carefully, because children heard traps before adults named them. "If the mark remains. If your name remains. It can help me follow a thread. It cannot make a thread after the world has cut it away."

Tovin considered that, narrow shoulders tucked inward. "So it is not a leash."

"No."

Sable's gaze sharpened. "Say the rest."

"It is not an oath for children," Banki said. "No command. No debt. A door-knocker, not a collar."

Tovin nodded, satisfied by a danger put where he could see it, and went back to sorting spoons by whether Branna could still use them.

Branna set a second biscuit beside him. "Eat before sorting my ruined silverware into moral categories."

"It is not ruined if it still stirs," Tovin said.

Banki, who had intended to return his attention to the ledger, looked at the boy's screwdriver-large grip on a bent spoon and ordered Tally to procure three smaller tools before dusk. No ceremony. No praise. Just the correct instrument placed where a useful child could reach it.

"Interesting," Banki said.

Sable came to the side of the chair. "No."

The Hall went quiet.

"No mind-editing loyal people," she said. "No oath hidden in food. No using breakfast to make obedience feel warm."

"If I wished obedience, Sable, I have more direct instruments than biscuits."

"That is not an answer."

"It is an assurance."

"From a telepath on a chair that is not a throne."

Mara's pen paused.

Sable looked at the workers, then back at Banki. "If they are Yours, they need rights. His cannot mean owned. It has to mean answerable to. You owe them choices, wages on time, beds that lock from the inside, contracts they can read, and the right to leave unless leaving would kill someone else."

The word Kingdom moved through the room before anyone spoke it.

Mara opened a clean ledger. "Crown Citizenship, structured on the Asmodean Pact. Voluntary oath. Named rights. Named duties. Food, wages, shelter, medical care, resurrection priority if that absurdity becomes relevant, and prohibition against compelled mind alteration for loyal citizens except under immediate life-threatening necessity."

"Add no laboratory work on Crown Citizens," Sable said. "Not even if they consent while frightened. Especially then."

Mara wrote.

"Add children under Crown protection are not leverage. Not against heroes. Not against enemies. Not even as proof that you were right."

Banki looked offended. "Children are sacred."

"Then let the law know what your instincts already know."

Mara wrote that too.

The first Crown Contract existed before the biscuits cooled. It was not gentle. Treason had consequences. Oaths meant something. But the contract also bound the Crown: food before work, wages before luxury, rescue before punishment, no citizen spent as fodder, no loyal mind rewritten for convenience.

Banki read the treason clause twice and strengthened it. Mara let him. Then she strengthened the wage clause, the medical clause, the room-lock clause, and the clause requiring witnessed explanation before any oath could be sworn. Banki noticed the trap only after the ink dried.

"You made the Crown legible," he said.

"I made the Crown suable."

The word pleased her far more than it pleased him.

At the bottom, in Mara's wording and under Sable's stare, one line sat like a nail through the page:

The Crown claims service. It does not claim the soul.

By dusk, Mara made that line real.

Before Hest came to the ledger, Sable found Tovin behind the kitchen door testing the new latch with the solemnity of an engineer inspecting a bridge. He had a screwdriver too large for his hand and a biscuit crumb on his sleeve.

"It opens from inside," he said before she could ask.

"Every safe room does," Sable said. She crouched, tightened the loose plate herself, and handed him the screwdriver back handle-first. "A room you cannot leave is a cage. Remember that. Especially when someone powerful calls it protection."

Tovin repeated it under his breath until it sounded less like a lesson and more like a charm.

Hest, a rescued woman who had eaten twice in the Breakfast Hall, refused Crown Citizenship. She wanted three days' wage, a wrapped meal, and the door.

Banki felt the old instinct rise: keep the vulnerable close, protect by proximity, prevent the city from eating what he had pulled from its teeth.

Sable said nothing. She only looked at him.

Mara turned the ledger to VOLUNTARY OATH.

"Pay her," Banki said.

Branna packed biscuits. Sable assigned no escort until Hest asked for one. Hest left with money, bread, and no oath in her mouth.

Only after the gate shut did Banki speak. "She will be dead inside a month."
