# Chapter 5

## The Dry Kiln

Rain hammered the cobbles of the Lower Ward—a relentless, freezing deluge that flushed grease, horse offal, and the sour reek of unburied gutter-shrouds into the subterranean culverts. The storm offered a coarse, merciful veil for a man who moved by stealth. Banki slipped through the slick, obsidian streets with practiced anonymity, his midnight-weft cloak drinking both the jaundiced glow of the whale-oil lanterns and the biting damp of the gale. He did not hurry. Haste drew eyes, and in the Lower Ward, a stranger’s glance was usually followed by four inches of cold iron between the ribs.

He paused before a sagging, soot-blackened storefront. Overhead, a rusted iron sign groaned in rhythmic agony against the wind: *The Dry Kiln*. To the tax assessors and the lazy night watchmen, it was a mundane smithy hammering out horseshoes, cart axles, and crude iron spikes for the merchant docks. A drab, necessary lie.

Stepping out of the storm, the atmosphere hit him like a physical blow. Heat rolled up from the subterranean forge in dry, suffocating waves, scalding the moisture from his nostrils before it could touch his lungs. The air tasted of sulfur, flash-quenched pig iron, and the sharp, copper tang of clandestine violence. The front room was cramped and foul, crammed with barrels of pitted iron scrap and racks of cheap, mass-produced tools.

Banki ignored the dummy stock. His focus anchored on the abjuration wipe-line—a faint, iridescent distortion shivering in the gloom, thrumming with a low-frequency hum that set his molars vibrating in their sockets. It hung like a ghost-shroud just behind the oil-stained canvas curtain that divided the counter from the shop's true heart. The ward gave the game away. No smith spent forty gold sovereigns on high-grade anti-scrying matrices to protect horse tack.

He glided to the scarred wooden counter, his mud-slicked boots making no sound on the grit-dusted floorboards. He stood motionless. Every line of his frame, the subtle set of his heavy shoulders, projected the deadly, deliberate posture of a man carrying black-market capital and an absolute lack of tolerance for amateurs.

A clerk slid out from the shadows near the curtain. Young, gaunt, and dripping sweat into his collar despite the draft, his skin possessed the unhealthy, tallow-yellow hue of a subterranean runner. His hands bore no hammer calluses; they were stained with ink and twitched with nervous, erratic energy. A disposable frontman—a thin buffer between the ironmongers in the deep forge and the street.

"Custom commission," Banki said. His voice was low, barely cutting above the muffled roar of the blast furnace below, yet it carried an icy, razor-edged resonance that froze the boy in his tracks. "Heat-proof. Ward-clean."

He tossed out the bait with mathematical precision, weaving black-market jargon to force immediate compliance. "It’s for an acquisition circuit operating out of Blackwater Quay. A private hold. Scheduled buyer auctions with heavy escort details. Public viewing runs next week. I require hardware that doesn't light up every scrying lens in the district when a high-tier buyer passes the cages with an active monocle."

The clerk’s professional smile—a flimsy assembly of cheap greed and terror—shattered instantly. The fine muscles around his eyes twitched as he tallied the risk against the ruinous penalties of Crown law. A raw, unshielded thought burst from the young man's mind, loud and panicked, slamming straight into Banki’s waiting mental net: *He knows the cant. Ward-clean. Auction circuit. Not a street rat. He’s syndicate. Or a guild executioner.*

"Ward-clean is... specialized," the clerk stammered, wiping a bead of greasy sweat from his lip. He leaned over the counter, attempting a conspiratorial murmur. "Heat-proof is simple enough. We run alchemically treated slag-iron for heat. But ward-clean requires cold-silver quenching and a localized null-field while the hammer hits the anvil. What’s the subject?"

Banki didn't bother answering. The boy was irrelevant. The shadow crouched behind the canvas curtain required handling first. Banki had felt the secondary presence the instant his boot crossed the threshold—a dense, cold knot of violence waiting in the blind spot.

He feathered a telepathic probe forward. He did not hit the mind like a hammer; he unspooled his awareness like a strand of wet silk, sliding effortlessly past the clerk's screaming surface thoughts. The probe tapped the quiet mind behind the canvas like a fingernail striking basalt.

The watcher countered instantly with disciplined, military reflex. His mental architecture snapped into a blank-wall technique—a smooth, featureless surface of gray obsidian designed to shed casual scrying and mind-thieves. Non-magical discipline. A pit-fighter or a disgraced legionnaire waiting in the dark with a tension-cranked hand-crossbow, ready to put three inches of steel through Banki's throat if the negotiation turned foul.

A danger to common thieves. An absolute non-entity to Banki.

He retracted his probe, leaving the enforcer's psychic wall intact but meticulously mapped. He knew the man’s height by the downward vector of his intent, his weapon by the cold, metallic visualization resting at the forefront of his motor-cortex.

Banki moved. He swept past the clerk in a single, silent stride, his hood casting his face in shadow as he parted the heavy canvas curtain and passed through the abjuration line. The ward washed over him—a sharp, static prickle that tried and failed to strip the dormant weaves clinging to his skin.

The rear workshop was a cavern of illegal craft. The thick reek of bone-ash, lard, and red-hot iron hit his throat. Every heavy chisel, wire-cutter, and crucible sat in recessed wall-slots, positioned for quick disposal if the Watch breached the outer doors. A heavy, bricked furnace dominated the rear, glowing with an unnatural, blue-tinted chemical flame that cast long, grotesque shadows across the flagstones.

A raspy voice, pitted by years of breathing hot metal dust, cut from the gloom by the anvil. "Sit. Keep your hands off the stock. Speak like your coin is real."

Banki released his mental shadow, letting his aura spill into the room like spilled ink. The crafter’s mind walled up instantly, throwing up defensive barriers of sharp, cynical thought. But the edges frayed enough to expose the core instinct underneath: desperate, grinding survival. This shop reeked of fear. They were drowning under protection quotas from the local street-syndicates, starving for a patron who could keep the thugs off their threshold.

"Containment iron," Banki commanded. He didn't sit; he loomed, letting the shadow of Sablehook bleed through his words. "Collar assemblies—heat-proof and ward-clean. High-stress cage reinforcements. Transport neck-shackles. Field-tolerant, signature-void. The iron must hold no memory of the forge’s magic. If a buyer sweeps these collars with a divination rod, I want them reading as inert cobblestones."

The master smith stepped fully into the pale blue firelight. He was a mountain of scar-tissue and muscle, his skin a patchwork of pale burn-cracks and dark, tattooed marrow-runes. "Timeframe?"

"Seventy-two hours."

The smith spat into the cooling trough, the spit hissing violently against the fouled water. "For cold-quenched, silver-dusted iron? You’re out of your mind. The annealing process alone takes—"

Banki cut him off, dropping the contract parameters onto the worktable like physical blades. "Fifty percent upfront. In stamped platinum trade bars."

He reached beneath his heavy cloak and produced a leather-wrapped bundle, slamming it onto the scored oak. The impact was a dead, heavy thud that rattled the scrap-bins.

"A twenty percent bonus for early delivery within sixty hours," Banki continued, his cadence an unstoppable, frozen tide. "Another twenty percent for zero magical residue upon field inspection. And most importantly: an exclusive, protected supply line for rare reagents. I hold the docks. You will no longer pay the Rot-Tooth syndicate triple value for your furnace charcoal and silver-dust. My people will deliver your raw stock at cost, unmolested."

The smith stared at the leather package. The math whirred violently behind his eyes. Banki watched the thoughts crystallize, greed warring with terror.

"The price," Banki murmured, leaning down into the blue light, "is absolute exclusivity. The Dry Kiln becomes an iron-node under my umbrella. You touch no outside jobs without my written seal. My shadows will mind your access routes. If you freelance—if you forge a single latch for a alley-thief without routing it through my ledger... the contract is voided. And you are removed from the node. Permanently."

The smith’s mind crunched the variables. The glitter of platinum fought against the terror of an absolute master. Slowly, the panic hardened into cold, unyielding survival logic: *Don't touch outside work. Don't bleed. Live.*

"Understood," the smith growled, looking away from the coin to meet Banki’s eyes. The submission was absolute. "Name your lead and your handoff."

"Turnkey commands the perimeter. Whisperstamp holds the counter-intelligence. Outside work routes through them. They won't judge your hammer-work; they’ll guard your door." Banki flicked the leather wrapping aside, exposing the cold, polished edge of a trade bar. The platinum caught the furnace flame in a sharp, icy glint. "Deliver the iron. We have beasts to cage, and I will not lose stock to cheap latches."

## The Eldritch Hour

Brine, bone-ash grout, and the heavy, ozone stench of captive violence rendered the subterranean air of Sablehook almost unbreathable.

The descent into the lower containment levels always felt like dropping into the throat of a decaying leviathan. The temperature plummeted with every flagstone, the damp cold soaking through leather and linen to bite at the joints. The walls—hewn from massive, seamless blocks of spell-fused granite—swept continuous, dirty tracks of condensation down into the drainage channels.

The primary viewing corridor stretched into the gloom, bisected by a thick, twenty-foot standoff line painted in coarse, alchemical grit across the floor. On either side, massive iron cages—sheathed in spell-forged plate and braided abjuration matrices—turned unspeakable biological nightmares into sharp, shivering silhouettes. Backlighting inside the cells rendered every spine, every claw, and every twitching tendon in stark detail, allowing visual checks without bringing a throat within reaching distance of the bars.

Sable led the trio, her hobnailed boots striking the wet stone with military rhythm. She paused at the first heavy timber post, pressing two fingers into the soft lead seal on the log-board to verify the perimeter integrity. The seal was unbroken.

"Owner-access only," she reported, her voice flat and hollow in the narrow vault. She didn't look at the shadows inside the iron; her eyes were on the anchor-bolts holding the frames to the granite. "Twenty-foot standoff maintained around the clock. No stress testing. No touching the iron. Your doctrine, Banki. Executed without deviation."

Darric walked at Banki's shoulder, his hands folded behind his spine in a parade-rest posture designed to eliminate sudden, defensive movements. He didn't waste time scanning the monsters. His focus tracked the hinge-plates, the load-bearing welds on the latch-brackets, and the depth of the expansion pins driven into the stone floor. He was auditing the cage, not the inhabitant.

The first enclosure held the Reedsinger. Designated NMD-RS-01.

A foul, grey residue of burnt enchantment clung to the iron bars—the greasy stain of previous handlers who had tried and failed to force compliance through blunt mental domination. The beast itself radiated no visible magic. It possessed no glowing eyes, no aura of sulfur or frost. It sat huddled in the center of the cell, a gaunt, elongated horror wrapped in tatters of its own sloughed, translucent skin.

Yet its attention carried the physical pressure of an iron plate resting on the chest.

It did not pace the cell like a starved wolf. It did not hurl its bulk against the bars. It remained frozen, its twin obsidian eyes locked onto the three visitors. It tracked Banki's breath, measuring the expansion of his ribs. It mapped the microscopic muscle twitches in Darric's calves as the soldier shifted his stance. It listened, with terrifying precision, to the exact cadence of Sable's pulse pounding against her throat.

A raw, jagged transmission scraped against the inside of Banki's skull—a psychic bleed that felt like a rusted nail dragging across bone: *pattern... rhythm... gap... gap... gap...*

Banki hardened his mental shields, turning his surface thoughts to cold steel. "Learner-predator," he said, keeping his voice dead, stripped of all emotional cadence that the beast could analyze. "It isn't plotting a violent break. It’s mapping the mechanical lock of our attention. It’s calculating our nervous system's delay. Once it models a handler's tells—a blink, a hitch in breath, a preferred lead foot—it becomes statistically impossible to hold. It will strike straight through the lag in your reactions."

"White-noise metronomes," Darric cut in, his tone dropping to a dry, tactical drone. He knew the threat. "We mount clockwork noise-makers along the corridor. Shatter the acoustic mapping. We rotate handlers every shift and randomize approach vectors. Never let it learn the rhythm of a guard mount. If it can't predict the room, it can't launch."

Banki gave a tight, single nod. The protocol was sound. He broke eye contact with the Reedsinger, feeling the psychic pressure instantly snap toward the back of his neck like a hooked line. He ignored it. Next cage.

The Reed-Mirror Serpent. Designated UNQ-RS-02.

The cell appeared vacant at first glance—a massive terrarium of thick, alchemically tempered glass and heavy masonry. Banki launched a sharp mental probe through the pane, hunting for a life-signature.

The probe skated off.

The consciousness was frictionless. No resistance, no mental shield—merely an absolute lack of grip. Inside the enclosure, a localized refraction wash bent the air, wrapping the beast in a shifting skin of false light. Ambient illumination fractured across its scales. The reflections on the glass warped wildly, showing the serpent coiled in the far left corner for half a breath before the visual image snapped, revealing the monster pressed flat against the front pane, inches from Banki's face.

Looking at it directly triggered a sharp, nauseating pain behind the eye-sockets. The visual cortex rebelled against the impossible optical geometry.

*Don't look straight,* a cold, glassy thought drifted into the air, a predatory sneer.

"Slick mind," Banki noted, rubbing his temple to clear the spike of vertigo. "It projects a constant, localized refraction field. It’s waiting for the eye to blink—waiting for the handler to trust the illusion over the mass. If you open that door based on where you think it sits, it’s already coiled around your windpipe."

Next cell. The Shatterglass Lurker.

The entity undeniably filled the space behind the heavy iron bars, yet direct vision refused to lock onto its silhouette. Banki attempted to focus on its limbs, but his gaze slid off the sharp angles of its frame, violently pulling his eyes toward the empty corners of the cell. A walking blind spot—a predator evolved to erase itself from a prey animal's visual processing center.

"Perception trap," Darric spat, his jaw tight as he stared doggedly at the stone floor. "A nightmare for close-quarters squads. This breaks units, not doors. One guard screams it’s on the left, another swears it’s on the right, and the corporal steps closer to the bars to see who's lying. The confusion breeds panic. Panic breeds motion. Then the blood hits the floor."

"Keep it boring," Banki ordered, tracing the structural anchor-bolts instead of the shadow. "Automated chutes for raw meat. Zero direct contact. Excluded from standard sales. We only entertain offers for this asset if the coin is absurd, and only to vetted buyers who prove they understand cognitive hazards."

Further down the lane, the Displacer Beast paced in tight, hyper-efficient loops. Six heavily muscled legs padded silently over the stone. Its twin, spiked tentacles whipped the air with the crack of a slaver's lash. It paid the men no mind, marching its perimeter with the cold, calculating malice of an apex hunter denied a slaughter-field. The light around its flank lagged behind its motion, producing a stomach-churning trailing effect with every turn.

"Displacers punish routine harder than the Reedsinger," Darric warned, his voice grim with memories of jungle campaigns where squads were turned to mincemeat in their tents. "Randomize feeding times and entry lanes. Keep the inside of that cage sterile. No loose chains, no leather scraps, no targets. If you give it something to strike, it will map its reach. If you want auction stability, you train the staff to be flawless. You can't train the beast."

They passed through the quarantine airlock into the wet wing. Here, the air was thick with salt-damp, rotting kelp, and raw offal.

Kessa, the beastmaster recruit, stood on the elevated gantry above the Transition Tank—a massive, sunken pool of brackish water carved directly into the bedrock. She held a long, steel-reinforced pole. An inert bait bundle—brined meat soaked in fish oil, devoid of any fresh blood splash that might trigger a frenzy—hung from a cable inches above the dark water.

A dark, torpedo-like silhouette rose from the depths without a wave. It did not thrash. It did not surge. It glided upward—a shadow separating itself from the black water. It brushed the bait with the hardened edge of its snout, testing the density, verifying the contact.

A single, explosive snap of jaws sheared the meat clean from the line. The teeth cut through dense sinew without a sound. The beast sank back into the murky depths without a single splash disturbing the surface.

"Trainable," Banki murmured, watching the clean, disciplined execution. "Offer the hatchlings choices. Build the holding tanks with varied micro-climates—warm dry hides, wet moss banks, shallow waders. Let them choose their territory before we assign them a tactical role. A comfortable asset is a predictable asset."

Before the shift-change, Sable assembled the staff in the preparation hall. Thirty guards, handlers, and runners stood in rigid ranks. Sable stood on the stone mounting block, positioned so every eyes could see her empty, gloved hands. She projected absolute, ruthless authority.

"Curiosity kills operations," she announced, her voice flat, carrying the cold finality of an iron door slamming shut. "If you gossip about the inventory in the low vaults, you become inventory for our rivals. You make yourselves targets for syndicate snatch-squads looking for a weak mouth. If you talk in the taverns, you get one warning. The second time, you are reassigned to the latrine drains until your skin rots off."

She paced the line, her cold gaze locking onto the younger recruits. "If a buyer tries to pump you for details, you recite the printed manifest. You know nothing beyond the lot numbers. If one of Banki's shadows tries to pump you for details... you shut them down the same. My floor. My rules. Your loyalty is to the iron of this facility. Do we understand each other?"

Silence held the hall. The policy was no longer words on paper; it had become an iron weight resting on every neck. Banki watched from the doorway, his face expressionless, thoroughly satisfied with the cold efficiency. The clever ones stopped trying to be clever. They became cogs in Sable's machine. Exactly as required.

## Crown Citizenship

The kingdom inaugurated itself not with the raising of a blood-soaked banner or the crowning of a monarch, but with a ledger.

Mara's iron-bound book lay open on the high salvage desk, its vellum pages thick as cured hide, its spine reinforced with heavy brass straps. The ink she had brewed that morning stank of iron gall, bone-ash, and something far older—a sharp, copper stench that hung in the back of the throat. Banki had bled into the crucible before dawn, three precise cuts across his left palm, letting his blood pulse into the pigment while the batch was still boiling. The magic threaded through the ink as dye threads through water: invisible, absolute, and permanent.

When a citizen signed their name in that ink, the act closed an metaphysical circuit. Their true name—written in their own hand, pressed into parchment mixed with blood and bone-ash—anchored a locator thread to their soul. The magic did not compel. It did not enslave the mind, erase a memory, or force a slavish oath of fealty. That was the crude work of street-tyrants and slave-lords.

Instead, Banki had woven a soul-address into the ink—a locator thread engineered to mimic the subtle psychic warmth of home, like the smell of a hearth fire after months on a frozen road. The magic settled into the signatory's identity—a quiet, thrumming beacon bound to their true name, remaining active until extreme distance, absolute terror, or death severed the line. A modified Asmodean contract working, designed to track rather than to possess.

Tovin Vale—a sharp-eyed, narrow-shouldered boy pulled from the Belowmarket fighting pits—sat on an oak crate near the kitchen hearth. He was systematically testing the mechanical resistance of a brass latch with a worn, child-sized screwdriver. Mara had written his name for him that morning, his fingers too scarred and stiff to hold the quill. He had watched the ink dry on the vellum with the hyper-vigilant suspicion of a rat watching a trap.

Tovin did not look up as Banki approached.

"If I get lost," Tovin asked, his voice scraped raw of childhood innocence, sounding like a veteran scout checking his kit, "does my name in that book find me?"

Banki paused, calibrating his words. Children detected lies long before adults parsed the subtext. Lie to the boy now, and the anchor was worthless.

"If the thread remains whole," Banki said, keeping his tone flat, stripped of pity. "If your name stays your own, and your mind remains intact. The magic allows me to follow a thread through the city’s mud. A light in the dark. But it cannot spin a new line after the world has severed the old one. Heavy lead-warding or deep salt-water will drown the signal."

Tovin digested this, his screwdriver pausing on the brass screw. He pulled his narrow shoulders inward—a street-rat's guard posture. "So it isn't a collar."

"No," Banki stated. "The magic carries no command. It forces no duty. It demands no blood-debt. It finds the door. It is not an iron ring around your neck."

The sharp, heavy strike of Sable's boots against the flagstones presaged her arrival. She strode into the hall, the heat from the subterranean kitchens doing nothing to thaw the ice in her eyes. She pointed a gloved finger at the open ledger, her voice low, vibrating with an anger that silenced the room.

"You bled into the ink." Not a question; the copper stench had betrayed him. "You wove a leash into the parchment they sign. Every desperate soul who puts their name in that book carries your thread without knowing the price."

Banki leaned back against the granite wall, his face a mask, though a flicker of irritation stirred his chest. "If I wanted mindless thralls, Sable, I have far more violent tools than an inkwell. I could rewrite their loyalties while they sleep in their cots."

"That isn't an answer!" Sable snapped, stepping into his space, completely unbothered by the shadows swirling around his cloak. "That's a threat wrapped in restraint. You hid tracking magic in a *voluntary* oath! *Voluntary*, Banki! The word is a lie when the ink is rigged!"

"It is tactical doctrine," Banki countered, his voice dropping to a dangerous whisper. "The world outside these gates views them as meat for the butcher. I am ensuring the meat carries a beacon."

Mara, sitting behind the ledger, paid the escalating argument no mind. Her silver nib scratched across the heavy vellum with cold, administrative precision.

"Crown Citizenship," Mara declared, her voice cutting through the tension like an iron wedge. "Structured on the precedents of the Asmodean Pact, adapted for mortal survival. Voluntary oaths only. Named, codified rights. Named, absolute duties. The Crown guarantees rations, fair wages, fortified shelter, medical treatment, and an absolute, unbreakable prohibition against forced mind-alteration for every citizen."

She looked up from the book, her quill poised. Her gaze shifted between Sable and Banki. "If the locator ink stays, it is disclosed. Page one. Directly above the signature line. They are told what the ink does before the nib touches the paper." The quill tapped once against the brass lip of the inkwell. "Or I burn the batch."

Sable turned from Banki, leaning over the desk to scan the wet ink. "Add a clause. Right there, beneath the medical provisions. Forbidding experimental laboratory work on Crown Citizens. No alchemical trials. No testing new ward-matrices on the staff. Even if they offer verbal consent out of fear or hunger. *Especially* then. Children under Crown protection are never leverage. We do not use them against heroes, we do not use them against our enemies, and we do not use them to prove our own righteousness to the city."

Mara nodded, her nib flying across the vellum. She completed the Crown Contract before the morning bell rang. The text offered no easy pardons for past crimes, nor did it paint a delusional utopia. Treason carried swift, brutal execution. The oaths retained their absolute, magical weight. But the ink bound the Crown as fiercely as it bound the citizen: rations preceded labor, fair coin preceded luxury, rescue preceded punishment. No citizen of Sablehook would ever be spent as tactical fodder.

And on the first page, in Mara's sharp script, the mandatory disclosure:

*The ink of this ledger holds a locator thread bound to your true name. It enables the Crown to track your signal. It does not enable the Crown to command your will. If you demand unbound ink, request it. Your citizenship will not be denied.*

At the foot of the document, framed by Mara's precise flourishes, a single line anchored the philosophy of Sablehook:

*The Crown demands service. It does not own the soul.*

By dusk, the heavy ledger faced its first true test.

Hest—a woman pulled from the warehouse ruins who had eaten two full meals in the hall that day—approached the desk. She stared at the open vellum, at the cold disclosure on page one, at the dark, copper-smelling ink in the well. Her hands shook so violently she had to interlock her fingers to hide the tremor. Her eyes kept darting toward the iron gates.

She refused Crown Citizenship.

She demanded her three days' guaranteed exit-wage, a wrapped ration from the kitchen, and the immediate opening of the gate. She wanted back out into the mud of the streets.

The ancient, predatory instinct roared inside Banki’s chest—a violent, tyrannical urge born of his absolute power to hoard the vulnerable. To protect by force. To cage the weak behind iron doors, enforcing safety through total dominance so the city's meat-grinder could never chew them up again. He could freeze her nervous system with a single word. He could rewrite her terror, forcing her to see the stone walls as a sanctuary instead of a cell.

Sable stood rigid near the hearth, watching him. She didn't reach for a blade. She simply watched the war raging behind Banki's eyes.

Mara, unreadable, turned the page past the Voluntary Oath, ready to record a departure.

Banki forced his fists to uncurl. The physical strain of holding back his magic made his finger-joints pop. He exhaled a long, ragged breath, violently shoving the tyrannical hoarding instinct back into the dark vault of his mind. He locked the door on it.

"Pay her," Banki rasped, his voice sounding like grinding iron.

Mara handed over the silver coins. The kitchen team packed the bread in waxed paper. Hest departed with the coin, the food, and zero strands of magic bound to her name in the ledger. The heavy iron gate slammed shut behind her, the metallic boom echoing hollowly through the damp courtyard.

Banki stared at the barred entry, his jaw set so hard his molars ground together. "She will be dead within a month. The Rot-Tooth syndicate runs her old alley. They will smell the silver."

"Then she dies free," Sable said softly, the edge finally leaving her voice. She walked over, standing at his shoulder in the dim light. "A room you aren't allowed to leave is a cage, Banki. No matter how much bread is on the table. Especially when a man with power stands over you and calls it mercy."

Banki turned away from the gate, looking back into the deep vaults of the compound, listening to the muffled, rhythmic pacing of the beasts locked in the subterranean cells. The teeth of his new kingdom were growing sharper by the hour. Now, they simply had to learn who they were permitted to tear apart.
