"Possibly," Mara said.

"That does not trouble you?"

"Less than a kingdom that calls refusal treason."

Banki took the Citizen Contract and added a clause in his own hand:

Refusal of the Crown is not betrayal.

Mara's first victory over the kingdom landed quietly.

It would not be her last.

## The Eldritch Hour

Stillness felt too much like captivity, so Banki trained.

The first hour was not ambition. It was refusal. Refusal to lie still where anyone could strap him down. Refusal to let breath become waiting. Refusal to discover, too late, that some future cage had been built one weakness wider than his last escape.

Banki stood alone in the cellar beneath the Gull & Gasket. Marrow runes, bone-dust lines, blood-iron warded stone, three locks on the door, Sable's soundproofing in the walls. Enough precautions to make the room safe by ordinary standards. Banki had very little respect for ordinary standards.

This was where he came when the house slept and the city stopped asking to be managed. The work could wait. Stillness could not.

Stillness felt like straps.

He raised one hand. The Screaming Skull form of his Eldritch Blast bloomed above his palm, black-violet and grinning, the old familiar violence answering as easily as breath. He fired.

The wardstone took the blow and shuddered.

Again.

This time he narrowed the Screaming Skull blast until the screaming face compressed into a lance. It punched a clean hole where the first had left a crater. Less spectacle. More penetration. A better answer for armour, hinges, ribs, doors, anything in the world foolish enough to believe thickness was a moral argument.

Again.

The room filled with the smell of hot stone and the thin metallic taste of power. Banki adjusted angle, pressure, silence, pain. He did not write notes. He did not need to. The changes went into the archive behind his eyes, filed with every other lesson the world had taught him by trying to hold him down.

Then the small work.

A lockpick, a dagger, and a sealed vial of ink rose from the table. One invisible hand worried through a phantom lock. Another turned the dagger in the air, point always forward. The third carried the vial in a slow circle without shaking the meniscus against the glass.

It looked like discipline.

It was fear with manners.

Somewhere under the training, under the careful breath and perfect posture, lived the old room: iron lattice, cold light, hands that had known his body as material. A specimen did not get to choose when a test ended. A specimen waited until someone else decided the lesson was complete.

Banki had decided, long ago, that no lesson would ever end before he chose.

The vial cracked.

Not broke. Cracked. A single hairline opening through the glass, enough for ink to bead along the seam. His concentration had tightened too hard around the dagger when the memory rose. The mistake was small, private, and therefore intolerable.

His first instinct was punishment. Add weight. Add duration. Repeat the set until the hand, the spell, and the memory all learned hierarchy.

He did not.

That restraint was new enough to irritate him. Sable would have called it progress, which was one of the many reasons he did not intend to tell her. But the crack in the vial had not come from weakness of hand. It had come from the old mistake: treating fear as if it could be beaten into obedience without leaving splinters in the tool.

Banki had built entire systems out of that mistake.

He was becoming less tolerant of it.

He set the objects down.

For ten seconds he did nothing.

The cellar did not close around him. The walls did not become lattice. The door remained a door. Above him, Edric's tavern slept badly. Beyond that, Sablehook breathed through ledgers, kitchen ash, oath-salt, animal musk, and the small warm minds of people who had begun to believe his walls meant shelter.

Banki cleaned the ink from the table by hand.

Magic would have been faster, so he did not use it. The cloth rasped over wood. Ink caught under one fingernail. His hands, human-sized tonight, moved with forced patience until the table was clean enough for ordinary eyes and still stained enough for his.

Evidence, he thought.

Then he opened the other sight.

Aether-Sight turned the cellar into residue: old wards, yesterday's force scars, Sable's saltwork in the walls, the shape of every spell that had touched stone and left a bruise. Rooms lied after violence. Power left fingerprints. If he could read them faster than his enemies, he lived. If he could not, someone else would decide what he was.

He practised until the first grey light found the street grates.

At dawn, he brushed chalk from his knees, checked the wards, checked the locks, checked the disguise, and climbed to the tavern, where Edric Mallon was already ruining coffee with admirable consistency.

Edric looked at the ink under Banki's fingernail and wisely chose not to ask. He set the cup down with two hands. The saucer rattled anyway.

"Long night?"

"Useful one."

Edric nodded as if useful and survivable were close enough relatives for breakfast conversation.

He had slept three hours. He had trained for four.

Sleep kept the body from becoming stupid.

Training reminded the body that helplessness was not allowed.

## The Anvil and the Oath

Six hundred miles to the east, in a city that still believed in sunlight, a paladin knelt before an altar and prayed.

Aldric Thorne was not the kind of man who kneeled easily. He was built like a cathedral buttress—tall, broad, constructed from materials that had been selected for their ability to withstand enormous pressure without visible deformation. His shoulders were the shoulders of a man who had worn plate armour since adolescence and whose skeleton had remodeled itself around the weight the way a tree remodels itself around a fence post: slowly, permanently, with a structural commitment that went deeper than muscle and approached the geological. His face was weathered but not aged, carrying the combination of sun-damage and scar tissue that marked a man who had spent decades outdoors in hostile environments and had emerged from each one slightly more battered and considerably more certain that the hostile environments were wrong and he was right.

His armour was magnificent. Not as ceremonial armour is magnificent—all gold filigree and impractical flourishes designed to impress courtiers who wouldn't know a sword from a salad fork—but as functional excellence is magnificent: every plate precisely fitted, every join perfectly articulated, every surface polished to a mirror sheen that was not vanity but maintenance, because a paladin's armour was a reflection of his soul, and a tarnished surface implied a tarnished commitment.

The altar before which he knelt was simple: white stone, unadorned, lit by natural light from a window positioned to catch the morning sun and direct it onto the altar's surface in a column of gold that was, depending on your theological perspective, either a clever architectural trick or a genuine manifestation of divine attention. Solarian, the Dawnfather, patron of justice and truth and the absolute certainty that the world could be divided into right and wrong if you just looked hard enough, was represented not by a statue but by a symbol: a rising sun with seventeen rays, each ray representing one of his cardinal virtues.

Before the briefing, Aldric stopped to repair a child's cracked toy shield with a strip of leather from his own kit. He did it without audience, then ordered a prisoner questioned hard enough to make Maren stop smiling. Mercy and severity sat in him close enough to share breath.

Aldric knew all seventeen virtues by heart. He had known them since he was twelve, when a cleric with kind eyes and an iron spine had taken him from the streets of a city whose name he no longer spoke aloud and had shown him a path that led from hunger and violence and the bitter, corroding anonymity of poverty to something that felt, for the first time in his life, like purpose.

He had walked that path for thirty years.

The path had not kept his hands clean.

A week before the prayer, Aldric had held divine light against an informant's eye until the man gave up a marsh road and nine hostages came home breathing.

Seraphine had healed the burn afterward. She had washed her hands twice before breakfast.

Maren had written the names into a song and never performed it.

Aldric remembered the doors opening more clearly than the sound the informant made. The memory told him he still needed prayer.

Today, the prayer was specific.

"Dawnfather," Aldric murmured, his voice low and rough with the particular reverence that comes from thirty years of daily practice, "your servant asks for guidance. The reports from the western coast speak of a darkness gathering in the port city of Blackwater Quay. People are disappearing. Memories are being erased. A psionic cult operates with impunity while the local authorities look the other way, and a new criminal organisation—the ShadowNet—has appeared from nowhere and is expanding with a speed and efficiency that suggests leadership of extraordinary capability and moral bankruptcy."

He paused. Pressed his forehead against the cool stone of the altar.

"I am asking permission to go there. To bring my company. To do what must be done."

The sunlight on the altar brightened. It might have been a cloud passing. It might have been something else.

Aldric chose to believe it was something else.

He rose from his knees with the smooth, practised motion of a man who had been getting up in armour for longer than most people had been alive, and he turned to face the four people who were waiting at the back of the chapel, each of them watching him with the combination of patience and readiness that characterised people who had followed this man into danger so many times that waiting for him to finish praying had become as natural as checking their weapons.

"We ride for Blackwater Quay," Aldric said. "Pack for a long campaign."

Seraphine—the party's cleric, a woman whose healing hands had pulled more people back from death's edge than most hospitals and whose moral compass was, if anything, more inflexible than Aldric's—nodded once, sharply. Her thoughts, if Banki had been there to read them, would have shown a mind already cataloguing the supplies needed for a campaign in a coastal city: salt-ward components, anti-psionic prayer beads, restoration scrolls for memory damage, the holy water reserves that she maintained the way soldiers maintained ammunition.

Caelen—the ranger, elven, ancient by human standards, whose tracking skills had led the party to three separate cult hideouts and whose arrow had ended the lives of more evil creatures than any of them bothered to count anymore—tilted his head in the gesture that meant "about time." He had been reading the reports from Blackwater Quay for weeks. He had been arguing for intervention for days. He was not a patient elf.

Lyris—the wizard, human, young by the party's standards but not by anyone else's, with a mind like a razor and a moral framework that she had built from first principles rather than received doctrine, which made her simultaneously the most flexible and the most annoying member of the party—pushed her spectacles up her nose and said, "I've been researching the ShadowNet. Whoever's running it is smart. Very smart. We should not assume this will be straightforward."

And Maren—the bard, half-elf, whose songs could heal wounds and whose smile could open doors and whose heart was so thoroughly committed to the proposition that goodness would triumph if given sufficient support that she had turned the proposition into a career—strummed a minor chord on her lute and said, "Then we'd better be smarter."

Aldric looked at his party. His family. The four people who had followed him through darkness and flame and the kind of moral clarity that most people found either inspiring or terrifying, depending on which side of it they stood.

"The reports mention telepathy," he said. "The ShadowNet's leader may be able to read minds. Seraphine, I need anti-scrying wards for the entire party. Lyris, I need everything you can find about telepathic countermeasures. Caelen, I need a route to Blackwater Quay that avoids the main roads—if this ShadowNet has the intelligence network the reports suggest, they'll know we're coming before we arrive unless we're careful."

"And me?" Maren asked.

Aldric's jaw set. His eyes—blue, clear, the eyes of a man who saw the world in primary colours and was proud of it—hardened with the intensity that preceded every campaign he had ever fought.

"You're going to write a song," he said. "A song about what happens to people who prey on the innocent in cities that have forgotten how to protect them."

He drew his sword. The blade caught the morning light and turned it into a line of white fire that ran from hilt to tip, and the chapel filled with the warm, clean radiance that was the Dawnfather's endorsement—or, depending on your theological perspective, a very impressive enchantment on a very impressive sword.

"We're going to Blackwater Quay," Aldric Thorne said, "and we're going to clean it."

Six hundred miles to the west, in a cellar that smelled of salt and strategy, Banki woke from three hours of sleep.

Neither man knew the other's name.

The road between them already had room for blood.

## The Caravan Launches

The morning after the trials, Sablehook woke before dawn and never quite returned to sleep.

Handlers moved under lanterns. Tally reorganised ledgers based on the night's promotions and failures. Kessa checked jar seals and preservation notes with a concentration that made devotion look sloppy by comparison. Somewhere in the front rooms a hammer began and did not stop for half an hour. Somewhere else a man learned that crossing the black-path corridor without permission was a mistake with payroll consequences.

Banki rose into that noise with a clean shirt, the commander weave beneath it, and the sense that the organisation had crossed another invisible line while he slept. It was no longer a cellar gang pretending at future. It was scheduling labour.

Sable met him in the planning room with Expedition Dossier v1 spread over the desk like a loaded crossbow. She had not changed from work clothes yet. A fresh coat sat over old leathers. Her hair was tied high. No softness anywhere.

“Morning,” she said. “You want the short or the complete?”

“The complete.”

She gave it to him in pieces precise enough to stack.

Elira Pynn secured and breathing.

Wednesday, March 4th. Dawn. The caravan assembled in Sablehook's yard: three reinforced wagons, six true Dire Rhinos, fifteen personnel, two specialist handlers whose authority outranked profit, and enough permits to make a magistrate tired before breakfast.

The wagons were engineering. The rhinos were policy with legs.

Banki watched the handlers move around them and understood that any kingdom worth the name had to solve the problem of weight. Food, prisoners, timber, cages, rescued children, grief, law: all of it had to be hauled by something that did not panic when the road went ugly.

Ordinary horses belonged to ordinary roads.

The Dire Rhinos belonged to the roads Banki intended to build.

They were not elegant. Elegance was for blades, contracts, and lies. The rhinos were blunt answers to blunt questions: how much can be moved, through how much danger, with how little permission from the road? They were fifteen feet of hide, horn, patience, and refusal, and every handler near them moved with the humility of people whose profession required negotiating with weather that had learned to breathe.

Someday, Banki thought, roads in his territory would carry their own refusal.

Darric's lead handler walked the line without hurrying for him. Eyes, nostrils, hooves, harness rub, temperament, feed notes. "They eat before they pull," the handler said. "They rest before they sour. If one limps, the wagon stops. No exception for profit. No exception for pride."

"Even from me?" Banki asked.

"Especially from you, apparently."

Sable's mouth almost moved.

The rhinos breathed steam into the dawn: six moving walls, still only animal, still limited by harness and road. Their hooves worried the packed yard earth. Their hides wore old scars like weather. One lowered its head while a handler checked the nostril strap and did not crush him, which Banki marked as the first miracle any road empire required.

He looked at them and saw a beginning, not an answer.

Behind the ordinary launch documents, Tally kept a second sheet for Darric, Kessa, and the future Direction Four staff. Core paired teams, support teams, individual scars, left-shoulder defects, temperament notes, breeding-candidate flags, and one column Mara had insisted on calling WHY NOT. Null-Fang would later taste those records for hidden contamination. Kestrel would judge from rail, scent, posture, and stillness which animals understood rank and which only understood size. Vell's scaling notes would call the stock usable. Iria's welfare notes would call usable a dangerous word.

For now they were draft animals.

Kestrel watched the line from the rail above the loading platform, not human and never mistaken for it by anyone with useful instincts. She had come into Banki's possession by violence, patience, and one choice he still refused to cheapen by calling purchase. Broodline Prime. Cliff-Skitter Predator. A magical beast built for vertical killing, captured after she made three handlers reassess the wisdom of cages and made Banki, inconveniently, admire her.

He had not broken her. He had given her a perch no one else could claim, food without mockery, work sharp enough to respect her, and a public moniker she already wore like a blade. The deeper true-name bond had not yet been fully sealed, but the shape of it already lived between them: not pet, not stock, not trophy. Cohort waiting for law. Family waiting for the right wound in the world to make the word honest.

Her tail moved once. The rhinos noticed.

Banki watched them settle into harness and knew ambition had already begun taking notes.

The handlers had named them despite Banki's private opinion that draft animals did not require poetry. Slate. Rumble. Princess, which was ridiculous enough to be useful because anyone who laughed too close to her horn learned respect quickly. DR-03's left shoulder sat high, congenital rather than injured, and Toma was already building a compensating pad while explaining the problem to no one in particular with inventive profanity.

Kestrel scouted from above, along wagon roofs, branches, stone lips, and any height the road reluctantly offered, moving the way rumours moved through taverns: quickly, quietly, and arriving before anyone expected her. Vesper handled capture with the calm of someone who knew animals were not puzzles to be solved but languages to be respected. Gravewire made perimeters and took any threat to those perimeters personally. Tally kept the permits sharp enough to cut delay. Kessa kept everything valuable alive and did not care whose pride bruised when care required correction.

Banki stood on the loading platform and surveyed the machine.

Sable would not be going. While Banki extended reach on the road, she would keep Blackwater quiet, paid, watched, and breathing.

"Three weeks," Banki said. "Week one in the Brinewood fringe. Week two in Shattercliff for the rare specimens. Week three on the Salt Roads back, with sales and networking on the way."

"And the town?" Sable asked.

"Yours. Complete authority. I trust your judgment on everything except the Deepmind programme, which remains under my exclusive control and is not to be discussed, referenced, or thought about by anyone who doesn't already know it exists."

Sable's expression did not change. Her hand rested on the city map, two fingers braced over the canal district as if she were already holding it down. She was not sentimental about authority. She measured it by exits covered, wages paid, routes watched, and how many people could sleep without a knife in hand.

"It'll be here when you get back," she said. "Bigger and quieter."

"That's the standard," Banki replied.

He climbed onto the lead wagon's bench. The Dire Rhinos shifted in their harnesses, sensing movement, registering the change in the wagon's weight distribution with the mechanical sensitivity of creatures whose survival strategy was based on being aware of everything within trampling distance. The handlers murmured soothing nonsense—the universal language of "please don't crush anything"—and the rhinos settled.

Banki looked back at Blackwater Quay. At the fog. At the canals. At the city that he was slowly, methodically, irrevocably remaking in his own image.

Then he looked forward. Towards the road. Towards the Brinewood. Towards Shattercliff and the rare, strange, valuable creatures that waited there for someone with the skill to capture them and the ambition to profit from their capture.

"Move out," he said.

The caravan rolled forward. The Dire Rhinos' hooves struck the cobblestones with a rhythm that was not unlike the beating of a drum—steady, powerful, a march-beat for the advance of an empire that had just declared, in the most public way possible, that it existed and that it intended to stay.

Behind them, Blackwater Quay continued to rot quietly in its fog.

Ahead of them, the world opened up like a wound that was waiting to be explored.

The first day out of Blackwater Quay was the worst, not from danger but from emptiness. In the city, Banki's Mind-Sense was a living map: hundreds of minds, choices, appetites, fears, and lies moving in layered density. In open country, it hummed with too little to read. The silence was not peaceful. Peace had never been one of his native languages.

He filled the silence with observation: road, weather, rhino temper, harness fit, the argument between Princess's high left shoulder and Toma's compensating pad.

The first true encounter came on Day Two, under the Brinewood canopy.

Kestrel's warning arrived through the bond as a trained emotional flash: threat, east flank, four hundred feet, moving, large, agitated.

The mind Banki found was animal, psionic, hungry, and undecided. It read the caravan as scent, vibration, motion, and emotional weather: metal, leather, Dire Rhino musk, restrained nerves, too many bodies moving as one large thing. Vesper named it from his description: Brinewood Shadowcat, adult female, valuable if captured clean and nearly impossible to surprise because hostile intent would reach her before steel did.

So Banki approached without hostile intent.

Not falsely. That would have failed. He let himself feel curiosity, admiration, genuine calm. The Shadowcat emerged in a shaft of green light, black coat shifting with shadow, amber eyes old with animal intelligence. She did not crouch. She did not flee. She studied him as if the forest had produced an argument and placed it on two legs.

Banki walked slowly. Not hiding intent, because there was no point lying to a creature whose senses tasted intent before motion. He framed the approach in the emotional vocabulary she could read: curiosity, respect, no hunger, no chase.

At thirty feet, he deepened the calm into the Sovereign Thread.

The emotional layer was already true. He deepened it, not by adding false calm but by letting the real calm become dense enough for her to rest inside. No command first. No domination. Just the overwhelming psychic fact of safety offered with predatory precision.

The cognitive layer came after, light as a hand beneath water. Sleep, not surrender. Weight, not fear. The Shadowcat's alertness softened by degrees. Her forelegs folded. Her head lowered to the moss.

She slept in twenty seconds. Captured in thirty. Cradled in forty.

Vesper moved in with padded restraints, and the Shadowcat was loaded into Wagon Two, sedated and dreaming.

Kestrel watched him from the wagon rail, foreclaws hooked into the wood, and sent the thought through the speaking charm with dry precision. "You walked up to a psionic apex predator and put it to sleep by thinking nice thoughts."

"By being genuinely non-threatening," Banki corrected. "The thoughts were accurate."

"That's worse."

"Yes," Banki said. "It is." The caravan continued into the Brinewood, and the trees closed behind it like curtains, and the forest settled back into the ancient, patient silence that had been briefly interrupted by the passage of something that the forest had not yet decided whether to welcome or to fear.

By dusk, the Shadowcat's breathing had synced with the wagon's sway, and Banki caught himself listening for it the way he listened for useful machinery: not affection, he told himself, only quality control. Kestrel, from the rail above, let disbelief travel down the bond with the delicate cruelty of a claw-tip on glass.

## The Brinewood

Road discipline became liturgy by the second day out.

Wake checks before dawn. Harness inspection before speech. Jar audit before breakfast. Wheel touch before prayer, if anyone still bothered with prayer. Silence on the first mile. Reports only after rhythm had settled into axle and hoof. The dire rhinos respected the routine for the same reason soldiers do: repetition promises fewer surprises, and fewer surprises leave more room for sanctioned aggression.

Banki liked caravans.

A caravan stripped away city lies. Every object had a place or became a hazard. Every person revealed what mud, boredom, and the possibility of teeth made of them.

By then the field team had begun wearing their roles as visibly as their gear.

Kestrel moved above the vanguard whenever the trees allowed it, fitted in scout-barding over the prototype shell, goggles at throat, paired blades riding high on the harness where a climbing draw stayed clean. When the branches thinned she ranged low along wagon roofs and rock faces instead, a living warning with claws. Vesper ranged further and slower, her armour cut the same line but carrying more rope, more signal chalk, more patience.

The Brinewood was a forest that had opinions about visitors.

Not entirely metaphorically.

He was interested in catching them.

The first capture came on day two, in a clearing where the trees thinned enough to admit shafts of afternoon light that painted the forest floor in stripes of gold and shadow. Kestrel's route marks and scent-slate had identified the target: a Reedsinger—a creature that existed in the taxonomic borderland between "large reptile" and "small dragon" and that had been classified by the various academic authorities who studied such things as "valuable," "dangerous," "fascinating," and "do not approach without significant magical support."

The Reedsinger was twelve feet long, with a serpentine body covered in scales that shifted colour with its mood—deep emerald when calm, bright amber when curious, vivid crimson when angry, and the particular shade of pale, luminous silver that indicated it was about to use its primary offensive capability: a sonic attack that could shatter bone at fifty feet and scramble cognitive function at a hundred.

Vesper briefed the capture team in a low voice, her eyes never leaving the clearing where the Reedsinger sunned itself on a rock chosen with more care than most people granted reptiles.

"Standard net approach won't work," Vesper said. "The sonic attack triggers if it feels trapped. We need to immobilise it before it realises it's being immobilised. That means—"

"That means me," Banki said.

He stepped forward.

Then Greater Shroud folded around him like a closing hand, and he vanished.

The capture team blinked.

He flew towards the Reedsinger.

He crossed the fifty feet in pixie form, silent and invisible, riding warm air as if the clearing had offered him a road.

The Reedsinger's mind was not human. Not even close. Its thoughts registered in Banki's Mind-Sense field as patterns rather than words—colours, temperatures, textures, the sensory vocabulary of a creature that experienced the world through modalities that human language had not been designed to describe. Warm-rock. Sun-angle-good. Belly-heat. Something-approaching-maybe-nothing.

The last thought was Banki's presence registering at the edge of the creature's awareness. Not as a visual stimulus—the Greater Shroud handled that. But as... something. A disturbance in the ambient psychic field. A pressure. The reptilian equivalent of the feeling that humans describe as "being watched," which is less a specific sensory input and more a general unease produced by the deep, ancient parts of the brain that evolved to detect the presence of predators.

The Reedsinger's scales shifted from emerald to pale amber. Curious. Alert. Not yet alarmed.

Banki positioned himself directly above the creature's head, three feet up, and cast.

The spell was not one that any conventional spellcaster would recognise, because it was not a conventional spell. It was the Sovereign Thread—and the Sovereign Thread had been born in a cage.

The air changed first.

Ozone threaded the warm forest smell. The pressure dropped as if the clearing had inhaled and forgotten permission to exhale. The Reedsinger's throat-sac fluttered once; not fear yet, but its body understood that the world had acquired a new shape.

Year Fourteen. The Thessalan Consortium's holding wing.

The procedure had not killed Banki. Nothing the Consortium did ever killed him, because dead specimens were worthless specimens, and Banki's value—the value of a fey creature with demonstrable psionic capability, a combination so rare that the Consortium's acquisition logs listed him as "unique, irreplaceable, handle with maximum preservation rules"—was too high to risk.

But the procedure had done something that Voss had not intended.

Voss's scalpel had cut through the barriers.

In the six months of pain and darkness after the Consortium cut him open and put him back together, the three channels had begun to bleed into each other.

The Tri-Weave sang where ordinary diviners had never learned to listen because nothing alive should have produced that note.

Banki called the combat application the Sovereign Thread: a tri-fold lash of compulsion, disruption, and force.

The first level was emotional. The Geometric Lens opened in his mind and reduced panic to angles: stimulus, interpretation, response. Banki bent the middle angle. Calm entered the Reedsinger's affect centres like warm water through cracked stone, not comforting, not kind, merely irresistible. The creature's scales shifted from amber back to emerald. Its muscles loosened before they understood they had been commanded to loosen.

The second level was cognitive. Structural Synesthesia translated the Reedsinger's thoughts into temperature and pressure, warm-rock and sun-angle and the rising silver brightness of imminent violence. Banki laid one instruction through the pattern: safe. No threat. Sleep belongs here. The idea did not sound like language inside the creature. It arrived as weather.

The third level was physical. Telekinetic force formed around the jaw, the ribs, the throat-sac, and the delicate resonance chambers that made the animal dangerous. Gravity tilted by fractions. Oxygen itself seemed to stiffen into a pale, anaemic lattice around the Reedsinger's skull. If the creature tried to sing, the song would find its own body politely, absolutely unavailable.

The Reedsinger's eyes closed. Its breathing slowed. Its mind, in Banki's telepathic field, dimmed from the bright, active pattern of waking consciousness to the slow, rhythmic pulse of deep, dreamless sleep.

Banki signalled the capture team.

They moved in with the practised efficiency of people who had rehearsed this exact scenario dozens of times: net first, then restraints, then muzzle, then the specially designed transport cradle that would keep the specimen immobilised and sedated during the journey back to the wagon. The entire process took four minutes, and by the end of it, the Reedsinger was secured in the Isolation Wagon, breathing steadily, dreaming whatever dreams twelve-foot sonic reptiles dream, and officially catalogued as RS-APEX-01—the first named capture of the Sablehook Live Lot Division's inaugural expedition.

Banki reverted, checked containment, checked sedation, and filed the lesson for later captures.

"Next target," he said.

Kestrel tapped one foreclaw against the route slate she had marked through Tally. Vesper checked the wind direction. Gravewire scanned the perimeter. Tally updated the manifest. Kessa checked the jar rack.

The caravan rolled deeper into the Brinewood, and the forest's opinions about visitors were, for the first time in a very long time, irrelevant.

## Campfire in the Dark

Camp Eight sat at the edge of the Brinewood, where the canopy thinned and the air began to taste of exposed stone.

The fire was small.

Kestrel was cleaning blade-mounts, harness knives, and the outer ridges of her foreclaws—a nightly ritual performed with the meditative focus of a monk at prayer, each scrape of oilcloth and whetstone a mantra, each inspection of edge and claw a contemplation. Gravewire was on perimeter. Tally was updating the manifest. Kessa was in the Isolation Wagon, performing the evening care rounds on the specimens they'd captured over the past week.

The haul mattered less by list than by sound: jars clicking softly in the wagon rack, a Lantern Stalker breathing wrong in its covered cage, the Skulk-Wolf juveniles pressing against one another whenever Kestrel's claws scraped along her blade-mounts.

Banki sat near the fire and let the warmth touch the edges of his awareness without penetrating the centre.

The team had found its road rhythm. Gravewire's watchfulness had lost one layer of insult. Tally's manifests stayed dry. Kessa's evening rounds ended with two bitten fingers and no complaint worth keeping.

Kestrel's voice entered through the speaking charm, low and roughened by distance from a throat not built for human softness.

"Boss."

Banki looked up.

"The Shattercliff approach. Rinna says there's a passage through the Ironhorn Spur that cuts two days off the standard route, but it goes through territory that the locals consider haunted." She paused. "I asked what they meant by haunted. They said 'things that eat light and remember your name.'"

"Magical creatures," Banki said. "Not ghosts. The locals don't distinguish between 'supernatural predator' and 'restless dead' because they've never had a reason to learn the difference."

"Can you confirm?"

Banki closed his eyes. Extended his Mind-Sense field to its maximum range—sixty feet in a cone, but the range could be pushed further with concentration, sacrificing precision for breadth. The night around the camp registered as a constellation of small, bright minds: animals, insects, the ambient psychic noise of a healthy ecosystem going about its nocturnal business. No sapient minds. No undead signatures. No psionic resonance that would indicate the kind of creature that "eats light and remembers names."
