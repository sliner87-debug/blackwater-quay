The extraction took forty seconds. The reed post came free with a gentle twist. The wax cap peeled cleanly. Inside: a rolled strip of parchment, tightly wound, covered in handwriting that Sable recognised from her operational dossier as Nella Quist's—small, precise, the penmanship of a scribe who had been trained to produce legible text at speed and who did not allow urgency to compromise clarity.

Sable copied the useful parts only. Full accuracy was less important than getting out before the stair, the lantern, or the watching dark developed an opinion.

The report was a summary of the day's observations from three Choir listening posts. Wagon counts. Personnel descriptions. Timing data—who went where and when, the kind of granular surveillance intelligence that the Choir's network collected through patient, methodical observation and that served, when aggregated across multiple posts and multiple days, as the raw material from which operational pictures were constructed.

The report mentioned Sablehook three times. Once in the context of "wagon departure, pre-dawn, six units, rhino-drawn, Wharfside bearing"—which was the caravan's departure, observed and documented by a Choir post that had apparently been watching the Sablehook compound. Once in the context of "female operative, dark hair, command posture, inspecting construction"—which was Sable, doing what Sable did, visible enough to be observed but not visible enough to be identified by name. And once in the context of a coded notation that Sable could not immediately decipher but that she memorised verbatim for later analysis: "MH-active. Secondary confirmation pending. Priority re-route if positive."

MH. Mirrorhouse. The Choir's high-priority designation. "If Mirrorhouse Sings," the intelligence said, "no improvisation, no contact, no delay."

The Choir was watching Sablehook at Mirrorhouse-level priority.

Sable copied the report. Not the full text—copying the full text would take too long and would require removing the original for an unacceptable duration. She used the charcoal to trace the key passages onto her own parchment, reproducing the handwriting closely enough that her copy would serve as a reference document while the original returned to the dead drop.

She re-rolled the strip. Returned the reed post to its position behind the stair brace. Checked the placement against Sable's positioning notes. Confirmed that the post sat at exactly the same angle, the same depth, the same orientation as before.

Fifty-eight seconds. Total time from extraction to replacement.

Sable withdrew the way she had entered: through the shadows, up the stairs, into the fog that embraced her with the democratic indifference that was its most useful quality.

She returned to the Gull & Gasket, descended to the cellar, and placed the copied intelligence on the desk that Sable used for operational analysis. She added a note of her own, written in the cipher that she and Banki had agreed upon for private communications:

MH-active on Sablehook. Choir surveillance is at maximum. Recommend increased counter-detection on construction perimeter. Nella Quist's dead drop compromised—use for curated misdirection per Banki's protocol.—L.

She left the cellar. Climbed the stairs. Entered the tavern above, where Edric Mallon was wiping down the bar with the methodical resignation of a man who had accepted that the bar would never actually be clean but who continued wiping it because the alternative—admitting that the bar had won the war—was psychologically unacceptable.

"Drink?" Edric offered.

"Whisky. The good one. If you have it."

Edric produced a bottle from behind the bar—the real bottle, the one he kept for customers who knew enough to ask for it. The whisky was smooth, amber, and warm going down, and it tasted like peat and smoke and the particular comfort that comes from completing a task successfully and knowing that the task was worth completing.

Sable drank. She sat at the bar. She looked at the ring on her right hand—the silver band that granted her access to the cellar, to the network, to the world that Banki was building and that she had chosen, with full awareness of the consequences, to be part of.

She drank again. And she did not examine too closely the warmth she felt, because examining warmth was the first step towards depending on it, and depending on warmth was a vulnerability, and Sable's professional opinion on vulnerabilities had not changed.

But the ring stayed on her finger.

And the whisky was very, very good.

## The Brain and the Binding

The prisoner had stopped screaming three minutes ago.

Not because the pain had ceased. Banki had compressed the muscles of the throat until the scream became a thin, wet whistle, all terror and no volume.

Banki did not enjoy this. Enjoyment made violence sloppy. The Divine Calculus was what mattered—result, utility, and cost—and the man on the table had earned no innocence from the world he had helped damage.

The extraction began with a casting.

The Neural Savant discipline was biological theft carried by psionic force: find the living pattern of an ability, open the bridge, and take what the target's brain had learned to become. The body knew better than the polite vault-language.

Seven times before, Banki had crossed that line. A cutpurse's reflex had taught his body to lean before the blade arrived. A burglar's trap-sense lifted the hairs along his arms when a floor lied. Evasion lived in the knees. Charm in the breath before a word. Blind fighting in the patience between sounds. Bodily deceit in the smallest fraud of muscle and pulse. Religious knowledge in the shape of prayers he had never believed. Each stolen talent sat inside him like a key on a dark ring, useful and permanent, with a dead mind behind it.

Tonight's prisoner was a fighter turned enforcer. Deep scanning and reaction tests had confirmed the prize: Improved Initiative, the trained violence of moving first when a half-second decides who owns the room.

The man knew the talent only as survival. Banki knew it as pattern: a thousand drills, a sergeant's boot in mud, the first lesson that hesitation was a door death liked to use.

The taking was not elegant. Banki placed his hands on the man's temples, closed his human eyes, and opened the others. Mind, will, hunger, pressure. The skull became a house with the doors nailed shut. The ability hid where training had made instinct, a locked cluster behind fear, pride, and the last animal hope that survival might still be negotiated.

Opening the bridge changed the room. Air pressure sagged. The candle flames pulled thin and blue. Ozone sharpened under the copper stink of blood. The prisoner's teeth clicked once, not from fear but from resonance, jaw obeying a command it could not hear. Banki's thumbs held steady against the temples while the stolen pattern thrashed like a trapped nerve in a scholar's diagram.

Some part of the man tried to keep it by remembering the first time he had been fast enough: a training yard, rain in one eye, a wooden sword stopped an inch from his throat. The memory came with the talent. Banki took both. He did not need the rain. He needed the inch for himself.

He found it and drew it through the psionic bridge. The prisoner convulsed once as reaction tore loose: childhood, mentor, bruised drills, and the old terror of being slower than death coming away in Banki's grip. Then silence: not unconsciousness, but vacancy.

Banki withdrew.

The new sharpness settled into him immediately. Stimulus to decision. Decision to motion. A fractional improvement, permanent enough to matter.

Improved Initiative acquired.

The aftermath did not announce itself as guilt. Guilt would have been cleaner. The aftermath arrived two hours later, when a cup slipped from a handler's tray in the Yard and Banki's hand closed around it before the cup had finished deciding to fall.

Sable saw it.

The handler thanked him. Banki returned the cup. Sable watched his fingers a second too long, then looked at the closed door behind which the dead man had stopped being useful.

"That wasn't yours," she said quietly.

Banki's hand remembered the prisoner's rain, the wooden sword, the inch before death. His own body had already filed the reflex under survival.

"It is now," he said.

For the rest of the morning, Sable watched his hands rather than his face. The stolen quickness lived there first: catching a falling quill, turning a latch before the latch finished sticking, moving half a breath ahead of ordinary need. No one else noticed. The restraint made it worse.

Sable did not argue. Worse, she only touched the knife at her belt, reminding both of them that a thing taken into the body did not become innocent because the body learned to use it well.

Banki cleaned his hands again before leaving the room, though there was no blood left on them.





Then he climbed the stairs and stepped into the daylight, and the world was fractionally faster than it had been before, and one person who looked at him knew exactly why.

Sable did not ask what he had taken. She watched the prisoner-cart leave by the lower gate and understood that some thefts made no sound loud enough for witnesses. Then Banki turned his head a fraction too quickly, and daylight seemed to flinch.

## The Ironhorn Passage

The Ironhorn Spur looked less built than broken. The mountains rose from the Brinewood like teeth after a bad punch, all fractured ridges, narrow cuts, and stone that seemed offended by wheels. Rinna Claystep led the caravan into the passage at dawn, when the slit through the mountain allowed just enough light to make darkness visible.

The corridor held old, industrialized High Magic in the walls—faint traces of marrow-runes pulsing weakly—and something newer beneath it: territorial residue, recent enough to matter, deliberate enough to warn. Something lived here and used magic the way animals used scent.

The caravan moved through the passage in single file, the Dire Rhinos' hooves striking echoes from the stone walls that returned as distorted, elongated versions of themselves—sounds that had been stretched and compressed by the passage's acoustics until they sounded less like hoofbeats and more like heartbeats, the slow, ponderous rhythm of something enormous breathing in the dark.

Darric had given Banki the rule before the caravan left, and the Ironhorn made it feel less like caution and more like prophecy.

"No hybrid work without three approvals," Darric had said, standing in Sablehook's unfinished yard with Hesta Bramblewake at his shoulder and six Dire Rhinos making the ground reconsider its loyalties behind him. "Yours, because you own the madness. Mine, because I know when bodies are being asked to lie. Hesta's, because she knows when handlers are about to die because clever men liked an idea."

Hesta had spat to one side. "And because beasts don't care how expensive your theory was."

Banki had looked from one to the other, amused despite himself. "You are both comfortable telling me no?"

"No," Darric said. "I'm comfortable making no useful enough that you listen."

"Any cross," Hesta added, "needs stop-conditions. Feeding plan. Pen plan. Birthing plan if it breeds. Kill plan if it breaks. Mercy plan if it suffers. And if a handler says rest, food, medical, or larger enclosure, the work stops until the handler is answered."

"Even if I disagree?"

"Especially then," Hesta said. "A beast that kills a fool still leaves blood on the floor. I don't intend to mop up after your pride."

Sable had stood very still through that exchange, and when Banki accepted the rule she wrote it down before he could improve it into something worse.

Now, listening to the Dire Rhinos move through the dark, Banki understood the value of the irritation. Direction Four would not be built by ambition alone. Ambition made monsters. Doctrine kept them from eating the staff.

Kestrel was on point, fifty feet ahead of the lead wagon, moving through the darkness with a low, climbing gait that trusted claws, height, and balance but not the environment.

Banki rode the lead wagon's bench, invisible, his senses extended to maximum range.

Mind-Sense swept the passage ahead of the caravan like a searchlight.

Except that the footprints were fresh.

And they were moving.

"Contact," Banki said, his voice pitched low enough to reach Kestrel's ears and the bond-thread no further. The telepathic channel he maintained with his bonded scout hummed with the transmission: presences ahead, not standard fauna, psychic signature anomalous, recommend halt and assessment.

Kestrel stopped. One foreclaw lifted, three talons spread in the halt-sign she had taught the handlers by making mistakes memorable. The caravan ground to a halt behind her, the rhinos snorting and stamping in the sudden stillness, their handlers whispering calming nonsense into ears that were laid flat against skulls that were designed for exactly one purpose and that purpose was not "being calm."

The darkness ahead of Kestrel shifted.

Not moved—shifted. The way a shadow shifts when the light source changes angle. The way a curtain shifts when the window behind it opens. A redistribution of darkness within darkness, a movement of absence through absence, and if Banki had not been watching with every enhanced sense at his disposal, he would have missed it entirely.

But Banki did not miss things. Missing things was a luxury he had not been able to afford since the day he left the forest, and his sensory architecture had been designed—deliberately, obsessively, through decades of refinement—to ensure that the gap between "what exists" and "what he perceived" was as close to zero as the limits of magical enhancement allowed.

He saw them.

They were not creatures in any conventional sense.

Shattercliff Darklings. Rinna used the name for them, her voice dropped to a whisper that was less about stealth and more about respect—the kind of respect that prey animals show to predators whose capabilities are not fully understood.

"They eat light," Rinna said. "Not metaphorically. They consume photons. Their bodies are made of—I don't know the magical word—they're made of whatever is left when you remove light from a space. And they remember. If you shine a light at them, they remember where the light came from, and they come for it."

"How many?" Banki asked.

"Usually three to five in a group. But I've heard of larger clusters."

Banki extended his Mind-Sense to its absolute maximum range, sacrificing precision for breadth, casting the widest net his telepathic capability allowed.

Seven darklings between the caravan and the far side of the passage.

Combat would turn the narrow passage into a slaughterhouse with the caravan trapped inside it. Capture was riskier and more useful, which made it Banki's preferred kind of foolishness.

"Nobody move," he said. "Nobody light anything. Nobody cast anything that produces visible illumination."

Then he shed his human form.

The polymorph dissolved in the darkness of the passage like sugar dissolving in black water—silently, completely, without visible trace. Where a man had been sitting on the wagon bench, a pixie hovered: three inches of fey fury, wings dark and folded against his back, amber eyes the only points of light in the absolute darkness of the Ironhorn.

Even his eyes, he dimmed. He reduced their natural luminescence to the faintest possible glow—the bioluminescent equivalent of holding a candle behind ten layers of cloth—and launched himself from the wagon into the corridor ahead.

He flew towards the darklings the way he flew towards everything: carefully, precisely, with the absolute confidence of a creature that had been navigating darkness since before darkness had anything to be afraid of.

The Sovereign Thread technique—the three-level psychic capture approach derived from the strictures of the Asmodean Pact that had taken the Reedsinger cleanly—required modification for entities that were not, strictly speaking, biological. The emotional suppression layer would need to be replaced with something that interacted with the darklings' alien psychology on its own terms, balancing the Divine Calculus of risk and reward. The cognitive suggestion layer would need to account for a consciousness that processed reality through the consumption and retention of light rather than through the electrochemical signalling of neurons. And the telekinetic cradle would need to be inverted: instead of holding a physical body in place, it would need to contain an absence—to wrap telekinetic force around a gap in reality and prevent the gap from moving.

Banki had never attempted this before. He had never encountered creatures like these before. He was, in the purest sense, improvising—taking principles that he understood and applying them to a situation that he didn't, trusting his training and his power and his fundamental, bone-deep conviction that there was nothing in the world that couldn't be controlled if you understood it well enough.

He reached the darklings' perimeter.

They noticed him.

The nearest darkling turned towards him.

Banki felt its attention like a cold hand on his spine—a reaching, questing awareness that was not hostile in any way he could interpret as personal, but that was hungry in a way that transcended the concept of appetite and approached the concept of physics.

He deployed the Sovereign Thread—modified, improvised, adapted on the fly with the desperate, brilliant creativity that was the hallmark of someone who had survived a hundred situations like this by being faster at adaptation than the situation was at killing.

The emotional layer became an empathic resonance—not suppressing the darkling's emotions (which may not have existed in any form he could manipulate) but creating a resonance between his own consciousness and the darkling's anti-consciousness, a bridge of shared experience between light and dark, between being and un-being, between the pixie's fierce, burning aliveness and the darkling's patient, infinite emptiness.

The cognitive layer became a suggestion framed not in language or imagery but in mathematical abstraction—the kind of pure, structural logic that transcended the differences between biological and non-biological consciousness and communicated meaning through pattern rather than symbol.

The telekinetic cradle became an invitation rather than a restraint—a shaped space of psychic force that offered the darkling a container to rest in, a defined boundary within which its formless being could settle and stabilise, the way water settles and stabilises when placed in a vessel rather than left to spread across an infinite surface.

The darkling hesitated.

Banki felt its hesitation in his psychic field: a momentary suspension of the entity's fundamental nature, a pause in the endless consumption of light that defined its existence, a beat of something that was not quite thought and not quite feeling but occupied the space between both—the darkling equivalent of curiosity.

Then it settled into the telekinetic cradle.

It did not resist. It did not fight. It simply... allowed. Accepted the container. Accepted the boundary. Accepted the proposition that stillness was, for this moment, preferable to motion.

Banki's heart hammered.

One down. Six to go.

He worked through the remaining darklings with the methodical, focused intensity of a craftsman performing a task that required absolute precision and tolerated zero error. Each capture was slightly different—each darkling had its own resonance, its own pattern, its own particular relationship with the emptiness it inhabited—but the fundamental approach held: empathic bridge, logical suggestion, telekinetic container.

Twenty-three minutes. Seven darklings. Seven captures. Zero casualties. Zero alarms. Zero damage to the caravan, the personnel, the rhinos, or the passage itself.

When the last darkling settled into its containment, Banki reverted to human form on the wagon bench and let out a breath he hadn't realised he'd been holding.

"Clear," he said.

The caravan rolled forward through the Ironhorn Passage, and the darkness that surrounded it was, for the first time in centuries, slightly less dark than it had been.

Behind them, in the Isolation Wagon, seven Shattercliff Darklings rested in specially modified containment jars—jars that Kessa had hastily reconfigured by lining the interiors with light-absorbing material, creating miniature environments that replicated the conditions of the passage well enough to keep the specimens stable during transport.

They were, conservatively, worth a fortune. The academic community had been debating the existence of light-consuming entities for decades. The magical research community would pay any price for live specimens. And the military applications—sentient darkness that could be directed, contained, and deployed—were what made generals salivate and treasurers weep.

Banki filed the captures in his mental ledger, adhering to the cold logic of the Divine Calculus, and began calculating auction prices.

## The Shattercliff Hunts

They found the Flickerbeast by the argument it was having with reality.

At first there were only absences. Birds landed in the cedars and abandoned them for no visible reason. Ripples crossed the pool before anything touched the water. One side of the clearing smelled of wet cedar. The other smelled of meat gone nervous.

Kestrel indicated with one foreclaw, barely more than a flex. "There."

Nobody else saw anything. That, in its own way, was the clearest sign yet.

Banki lowered himself beside the pool. Aether-Sight opened. The creature was not hiding behind illusion. It was misplacing itself. Its body breathed in one location while light politely lied about another.

"Useful," he murmured.

The word thrilled Kessa and worried Bram, which were both correct reactions.

They set the capture slowly. Haste around a beast like this was just meat volunteering.

The Shattercliff highlands punished straight lines. Every ridge broke into shale teeth. Every valley collected fog, old bones, and the kind of cold that made fingers stupid. The caravan had stopped pretending the place was scenery by the second hour. By the second day, even Gravewire had begun checking the ground before trusting it.

Caution made people live longer.

Vesper set the first lane with chalk and quiet flags. Kessa rejected it because the wind would carry panic-scent towards the animal. Hark rejected the second because one rope would cross a blind rock lip and blind ropes killed handlers. Banki rejected the third because it gave the beast an escape angle he admired too much to leave unclaimed.

The fourth plan bored everyone.

Which was why it worked.

The Flickerbeast came on the third day in Shattercliff: six-legged, dark-furred, all predatory patience and wrong angles. Vesper saw the shimmer. Banki found the mind. It was hunting a mountain goat and had no interest in becoming part of anyone's inventory.

Banki jammed the lie first.

Not cleanly. Not elegantly. Reality snapped back around the beast with an audible pop, and the Flickerbeast appeared three feet left of where the eye wanted it. For one breath it was magnificent and furious, a predator insulted by the universe.

Then Banki brought the Sovereign Thread down hard.

Calm struck first. Sleep followed. The telekinetic cradle caught the body when its legs folded, and six hundred pounds of apex predator settled onto scree with a grace that would have been beautiful if everyone present had not been holding their breath.

Vesper moved in with restraints. Hark bound the tentacles. Kessa checked pulse, breath, eyes, and the places where fear makes animals hurt themselves.

"She's alive," Kessa said.

"Intact?"

"For now."

Banki heard the warning inside the two words and chose, for once, not to improve it into a plan.

The creature's flank rose and fell under Kessa's palm. Under the fur, the displacement still twitched in small aftershocks, trying to put the sleeping body somewhere safer than the place where it had been caught. A lesser crew would have tightened the restraints until the twitching stopped.

"Looser on the rear left," Kessa said.

Hark hesitated.

"Loose means it wakes."

"Tight means it wakes angry and injured."

Banki could have ordered speed. Instead he watched Hark loosen the strap by one finger's width. The Flickerbeast exhaled. The wrongness around it settled.

There were kinds of control that looked like slack from a distance.

The Shardmantle Stalker was worse because it did not think in any language his telepathy liked.

It waited among the rocks as if patience had become a body. Crystal plates folded into the cliff. Edges sharp enough to cut steel rested where a tired boot might seek purchase. Mind-Sense found nothing. Aether-Sight found the lie in the stone.

Banki touched the crystal locks with telekinesis.

The plates opened.

For half a second, the creature was breathtaking: a predator made of faceted light, old as pressure, beautiful in the way knives are beautiful before anyone bleeds.

Then it charged.

Banki unleashed an Eldritch Blast in its Screaming Skull form. The horrific projection of howling bone-dust erupted around it, tearing at the facets. The Stalker screamed in a register that made teeth ache. Banki struck the centre mass with force, not to shatter it, but to put fracture where certainty had been. Crystal split. The plates lost their perfect symmetry. Kestrel's spyglass cracked under one foreclaw.

The tentacles held.

When the Stalker finally stopped fighting, no one cheered. Kessa looked at the fractured creature and then at Banki with an expression that made the word viable feel like an accusation.

"It lives," she said.

"Good."

"That is not the same as fine."

"No," Banki said. "It is not."

Kestrel lifted the cracked spyglass between two careful talons.

"You owe me a new one."

"Charge it to specimen acquisition."

"I'm charging it to your face if the next rock decides to be alive."

The handlers laughed too hard because they needed to laugh at something. The Stalker sat in the reinforced sledge like a broken shrine. Its facets caught faces in pieces: Banki's eye, Kessa's mouth, Vesper's hand, Kestrel's harness blade. No whole reflection. No single truth. Just fragments held in sharp patience.

Banki disliked how much he understood that.

The Cracklight Manta hunted by making the air lie.

Vesper found it above the ridgeline, a prismatic shimmer moving against the wind. Birds followed the shimmer and dropped from the sky stunned, fooled by beauty into entering the wrong part of weather.

Banki went up in pixie form.

Below, the capture team became toy-sized and then irrelevant to the wind. Kestrel's voice came once through the speaking charm, mostly static and profanity. Vesper told everyone to shut up unless the sky started bleeding. Kessa said, very calmly, that if Banki dropped the Manta on the veterinary wagon she would resign, revive him, and resign again.

The high air took those voices away.

The high air hated him immediately. Thermals shoved him sideways. Cold pockets opened under his wings. The Manta rode those currents as if the sky belonged to it by inheritance.

Its mind did not understand calm as an animal did. Calm was not a feeling. Calm was a place in the air.

So Banki made one.

He smoothed the turbulence around it by inches, not touching the creature at first, only the wind it trusted. The Manta drifted towards the stillness. Towards him. Its membrane dimmed. Its body folded slightly inward, following safety as its senses defined it.

When the cradle closed, it did not fight.

The descent nearly ruined the work. One wingtip scraped stone. Banki felt the Manta's alarm spike and held the air steady around the wound until the creature believed the world remained safe.

On the valley floor, Kessa's open-frame transport waited under a translucent canopy. The Manta settled into it and shimmered slowly, dreaming of weather it could trust.

Vesper watched the membrane pulse. "How did you do that?"

"Made the air stop arguing."

"You used telekinesis on weather."

"On a small piece of weather."

Vesper searched for a professional response and failed. "That's something."

"Yes," Banki said. "It is."

Kessa was already touching the scraped wingtip with two fingers and a frown.

"It trusted the air," she said.

"Yes."

"Then do not make the air lie to it again unless you must."

Banki looked at the Manta, at the slow colour moving under its membrane, at the creature's peaceful acceptance of a cage that resembled sky closely enough to be believed.

"Noted," he said.

Later, Kessa would check the wingtip twice more than the rules required. The Manta trusted the air now, and she had no intention of letting Banki forget what that meant.

Kessa did not look reassured.

## Salt Roads and Silver Tongues

The Salt Roads connected the highland communities to the lowland ports through waypoints, trading posts, and small settlements that existed because someone had to sell food between Point A and Point B.

Banki used the return journey the way he used everything: as a weapon. Specifically, as a weapon of commerce.

At every stop—every trading post, every crossroads market, every settlement large enough to have a tavern and a person with money to spend—the caravan presented itself in full Sablehook regalia. The wagon tarps bore the Sablehook brand. Tally distributed printed handbills advertising the upcoming auction. Kestrel marked contacts from shadowed eaves and wagon roofs, letting Tally and Vesper do the smiling while she judged who flinched from an apex witness. Vesper demonstrated handling techniques to local trappers who had never seen anything more sophisticated than a leg-hold trap and a prayer.

And Banki performed: handshakes, drinks, edited stories, competence polished into brand. He read minds the entire time. Every handshake became a data point; power followed.

At the Tidepin Steps waypoint, Banki encountered the Choir's surveillance network for the first time on the return journey.

He felt them before he saw them: two minds at the edge of his Mind-Sense range, their surface thoughts carrying the mirror-glint resonance that he had learned to associate with Choir-adjacent consciousness. They were watchers—not agents, not operatives, just eyes, tasked with counting wagons, confirming identities, and reporting back to the Choir's intelligence infrastructure.

The keyword hooks armed in Banki's telepathic field triggered on the first watcher's surface thoughts: ...count the wagons, confirm the named, report if Mirrorhouse Sings...

"Mirrorhouse Sings." A priority code: no improvisation, no contact, no delay. Observe and report. The Choir was tracking the caravan and spending premium fear on him.

Banki dealt with the watchers.

The caravan had twenty-seven wagons instead of six. It was heading northeast instead of west. It was armed with military-grade siege equipment and accompanied by a company of mercenaries in matching uniforms. The named individual—Banki—was not present; the caravan was being run by a different person, someone taller, with a scar across his left cheek and a foreign accent.

The watchers would report exactly what Banki wanted them to report: fiction dressed as fact, misinformation presented with the absolute conviction of people who believed they were telling the truth because, as far as their altered memories were concerned, they were.

The caravan continued west.

Not everyone remembered the stop the same way. Tally remembered the wrong number of wagons and frowned until he found his own unaltered notes. Kestrel, from a roof beam above the last waystation, watched the watchers leave with their borrowed certainty and flexed one claw against the wood. The sound was small. Banki heard disapproval in it anyway.

By the time they reached Blackwater Quay seven days later, the Sablehook brand had touched the Salt Roads from the Shattercliff highlands to the coast.

Sable met them at the gates of Sablehook.

She looked different. Not in face or build; Sable did not waste herself on display. The compound behind her moved with her fingerprints on it: a probationer stepping aside before she spoke, a gate opening because she lifted two fingers, a kitchen runner slowing when her gaze found his muddy boots. Growth had happened in Banki's absence, and not all of it belonged to him.

"Welcome back," she said. Then, without pause: "Three things."

"Of course," Banki said. "Report."

"One: Phase Two construction is complete. The black-path corridor is operational, the holding cells are functional, and the isolation suite has been hardened to my specifications and yours. Two: the buyer pipeline is robust. Six Verified buyers, two Premium, with twelve more under evaluation. Three—"

She paused. Looked at him with the flat, unreadable expression that she used when delivering information that carried weight.

"Three: there's a party of adventurers asking questions about the ShadowNet in the Dockside district. A paladin, a cleric, an elf ranger, a wizard, and a bard. They arrived four days ago. They're Good-aligned. Capital-G Good. And they are asking the right questions to the wrong people."

Banki's expression did not change.

Behind the mask of the returning businessman, something cold began to calculate.

The heroes had arrived, and Blackwater Quay suddenly had more eyes than shadows to hide them.

## The Intake

The night after the expedition's return was not for celebration. It was for processing.

Sablehook's deep containment level held too much life, too much value, and too many ways for both to become disaster. The expedition haul exceeded projection by enough to make every containment plan look optimistic. The Brinehook Coldhouse acquisition added eleven more creatures. Tally brought packets. Kessa brought intake lanes. Mara brought a classification slate cleaner than anything in the room deserved to be.

SALEABLE.

QUARANTINE.

STUDYABLE.

DIRECTION CANDIDATE.

NEVER PUBLIC.

"Interesting problem is not a legal category," Mara said after hearing Banki and Kessa use the phrase twice.

"It is a useful one," Banki replied.

"It is what men say before paperwork catches fire."
