The Manta settled, its membrane shimmering in slow, rhythmic pulses of azure and violet as it dreamed of weather it could finally, truly trust.

The fey form hit the wagon bench and came apart. Bones punched outward through the reforming muscle, each joint popping with a wet, grinding crunch as the skeleton forced itself back to human scale. The skin stretched so fast it split along the forearms in thin, stinging lines that wept clear fluid before sealing shut. The lungs ballooned against a ribcage still cracking wide — each breath a raw, ragged act of violence against a chest cavity remembering how to be large. Organs swelled back into place behind the sternum, the heart slamming against the breastbone in thick, arrhythmic thuds, each beat landing like a fist against the inside of the ribs. The brutal cold bit immediately into the sweat-soaked clothes, setting the expanding muscles to violent shivering. Blood ran freely from the nose, the physical toll of holding the weather taking its due.

Vesper stood near the front wheel, staring at the gentle, pulsing light radiating from the wagon. The ranger’s voice was quiet, entirely stripped of its usual brash bravado. "How did you do that? The nets wouldn't reach. The harpoons fell short."

"Made the air stop arguing." The voice cracked, hoarse from the freezing wind and the immense psychic strain, sounding barely human.

"You used telekinesis on the weather."

"On a small piece of weather."

Kessa was already standing by the reinforced bars, pressing two gloved fingers against the cold glass near the creature's scraped wingtip. Her deep frown deepened further, the lines around her mouth tight with a complex mixture of professional respect and deep, fundamental unease. "It trusted the air."

"Yes."

"Then do not make the air lie to it again unless you absolutely must."

The slow, hypnotic colors moving beneath the massive membrane cast long, strange shadows across the rocky path. The Cracklight Manta had accepted a steel cage simply because the cage had been engineered to so perfectly resemble the open sky.

"Noted." The response carried the immense, crushing weight of the endless, freezing peaks, settling deeply into the bones like the ancient dust of the passage. The lie was necessary. The lie was the work. And the work was far from over.
