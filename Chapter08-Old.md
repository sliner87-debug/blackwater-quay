So the categories stood. Direction Candidate meant Sablehook was not selling the thing because it might teach the kingdom how to become more dangerous. Never Public meant Mara had decided the outside world did not deserve even a rumour.

The phase board went up beside intake.

One quarantine latch failed during intake. Kessa caught the mistake before the handler lost a finger; Mara wrote the fault into liability before Banki wrote it into blame.

Seed work before living crosses.

Utility before field service.

Field service before war.

War never without a lawful purpose beyond Banki finding something interesting.

The locked archive kept every approved line canon. Mara insisted that most of it stay there: useful danger in background records, not spectacle. Book One's active weight narrowed to five living questions: Moths, Graziers, Eels, Military Dire Displacer Rhinos, and Gate-Kings.

The Dire Displacer Rhino folder had its own spine by then.

Branch A: Caravan Line. Stable draft, haul, convoy work, and the public theatre of roads that knew who owned them.

Branch B: War Line, later Military Line. Shock impact, formation break, battle discipline, and every argument a wall loses when something large enough hits it correctly.

Branch C: Temperament and Obedience Refinement. Command recognition, anti-panic, multi-handler acceptance, and the quiet civilising work without which intelligence becomes catastrophe.

Branch D: Cull, Study, and Failure Analysis. Tissue notes, line correction, harness-failure diagnosis, displacement gland comparison, and the ugly mercy of learning from bodies that could not safely remain what ambition had made them.

Sable tapped the Cliff-Skitter line. "Say it."

"Kestrel is not stock."

"Again."

"Kestrel is not stock."

Darric wrote CONSENT SOURCE REQUIRED. Kessa added NON-INVASIVE ONLY. Mara added a threat involving ink costs that no one present doubted.

The Stonemaw file came last. Only the Gate-Kings received active Book One priority: thresholds, collapse rescue, and the terrifying mercy of a wall that could choose who passed. The other heavy-basin theories remained sealed as future contingencies no sane kingdom should hurry towards.

Mara wrote NOT STREET ASSETS. Hesta added NOT BREAKFAST HALL ASSETS. Sable added NOT PROVING-POINT ASSETS.

"Progress with brakes," Mara called it.

Banki disliked the phrase because it was accurate.

When the last cage quieted, he descended to the Stitchroom with fifteen minds in the prisoner sack: Choir acolytes, criminal operatives, and two new Brinehook captures whose knowledge was beginning to spoil in living skulls.

Mirell had the extraction matrices ready.

The Neural Savant work was not gentle. Mirell had taught him the Deepmind principles; Banki had adapted them until they fit his own impossible physiology. The result emptied minds and archived what they knew.

He had done it before. Too often, by most moral systems. Never fifteen times in one cycle.

By three in the morning, the archive had grown. So had the cost. Banki climbed back towards Sablehook's living levels carrying fifteen emptied minds and told himself the cold truth demanded by the Asmodean Pact and the Divine Calculus: the empire grew, and every transaction required its due.

Then Sable's work arrived.

The Mudcap Rise strike had been hers from conception to execution. She would not call it flawless. She called it clean.

The Choir warehouse processed compliance compound for distribution through the canal district. Sable entered through the roof, not the door, because doors were where foolish defenders put their confidence. Two guards went down without blood. The sub-level laboratory yielded formulas, logs, finished doses, raw samples, shipment schedules, and the courier route for Brine-Runner.

Banki's original deception for the withdrawal had been magical, perfect, and therefore almost unusable. Sable replaced half of it with crude human misdirection. A pair of stolen boots went into one alley. A false courier splashed through another. The ferry bell rang at the wrong bridge. Rain turned bootprints into opinions. A bored dock watchman accepted a bribe to remember a red cloak that had never existed.

"This is inefficient," Banki said through the link. He provided an invisible safety net—anchoring her mind psionically so she could fight autonomously, operating strictly as her peer—while watching three separate pursuit assumptions die in ordinary mud.

*It is human,* Sable answered. *Humans trust stupid explanations because stupid explanations happen to them every day.*

She was right. That annoyed him. It also saved the operation when a Choir spotter arrived six minutes early and followed the red cloak into a tannery lane that smelled bad enough to punish curiosity.

She disabled the stations rather than destroying them, blurred the guards' memories, and staged the scene as alchemical incompetence. By one-thirty, the Choir had lost its local processing capability and forty doses that would never enter a victim's blood.

Sable sat at her desk for thirty seconds after filing the intelligence. Completion required acknowledgment. Then she opened the next file.

Clean.

The Brinehook Coldhouse changed ownership before dawn.

Its legal pens remained untouched. Its illegal sub-level emptied quietly into Sablehook custody: eleven creatures, three unknown hatchlings, several inadequate restraints, and a facility whose owners would find it difficult to report the theft of assets they were not supposed to possess.

Kessa's first intake note on the hatchlings was only two words:

GLOVES FIRST.

Darric's was worse:

THEY LEARN HEAT.

Banki marked them Studyable, then crossed that out and wrote Direction Review. Mara saw the correction and did not speak for ten full seconds, which was how Banki knew the argument would be expensive.

The following morning, Sable's holding company formalised the buyout with immaculate paperwork and implications sharp enough to make refusal unwise.

The Coldhouse was Banki's legally, financially, and operationally.

Sablehook's specimen inventory expanded. Its containment capacity expanded. Its problems expanded with them.

The city was learning a new kind of consolidation: cages, ledgers, law, theft, rescue, and intelligence applied to opportunity until opportunity began to look like a kingdom.

## The Good Ones

Banki did not sleep that night.

Good-aligned adventurers were not a rumour category. They were a natural disaster with travel rations. If they found a target clean enough to name, they would walk towards it with courage, healing, songs, arrows, wards, and the dreadful confidence of people who believed the world improved when they struck hard enough.

Sable brought the dossier before dawn.

"Aldric Thorne," she said. "Dawnfather paladin. Thirty years. Survives battles that should kill him. Leaves witnesses who call it mercy and enemies who call it judgment."

Banki took the file.

"Seraphine Dawnmantle. Cleric. Restoration and purification. She can undo memory work if she gets close enough."

That mattered.

"Caelen Greenarch. Elf. Tracker. Old. The kind of old that makes roads feel young. He finds things without magic, which means your wards will not impress him."

That mattered more.

"Lyris Ashvane. Wizard. Abjuration and divination. Persistent, systematic, and probably irritated by unsolved problems."

"And the bard?"

Sable paused.

"Maren Goldstring. She listens."

Banki waited.

"Not interrogates. Listens. Buys drinks. Remembers names. Makes people feel less alone while they tell her where the bodies are buried."

"That's dangerous."

"Yes."

Sable set five sketches on the desk. Paladin. Cleric. Ranger. Wizard. Bard. Different weapons, same direction.

"They are not stupid," she said. "They are not corrupt. They are not pretending."

"Good people."

"Yes."

She said it without sneer or softness. Sable did not waste contempt on accuracy. One of her best habits was this: she would name danger whether it offended him or not.

"They gave half their medical purse to the Canal Row fever house yesterday," she said. "No witnesses worth impressing. No advantage. The cleric stayed until midnight. The paladin carried water."

"That does not make them less dangerous."

"No. It makes people want to open doors for them."

Banki looked again at Maren's sketch. Sable had drawn her smiling. Not prettily. Precisely. A smile as tool and truth together.

"Pike's sister talked to her," Sable said.

"How much?"

"Enough to be one accident away from useful."

The problem with goodness was simple. It did not always need leverage. Sometimes it only needed a chair, a drink, and the patience to let lonely people become relieved.

Banki looked at the sketches for a long time.

The paladin would be the obvious threat. That made him less interesting than he wished to be. The cleric could undo some of Banki's work and judge the rest by standards that did not care how useful it was. The ranger might find what magic missed. The wizard would tighten every gap the others left open.

And the bard would ask someone lonely the right question.

Banki hated that most.

"Can we remove the bard?" Sable asked.

"Not without making the paladin righteous in our direction."

"Can we buy her?"

"No."

"Can we frighten her?"

"Almost certainly."

"Would it work?"

Banki imagined Maren Goldstring afraid and still listening. "Not the way we would need it to."

"Options," Sable said.

"We do not fight them."

Her eyebrow rose.

"Not yet," he said. "They are built for open confrontation. If I become the target, they get what they are best at."

"So we give them another target."

"The Choir."

Sable understood at once. It was one of the reasons she was valuable and one of the reasons being near her had become increasingly inconvenient.

"Real evil," she said.

"Memory theft. Psionic slavery. Altered citizens. Everything a paladin will run towards."

"And while they run towards it, they are not looking at us."

"Not enough."

"You are using heroes as a disposal method."

"I am using available force against a mutual enemy."

"That is the prettier sentence."

"It is also true."

Sable tapped Aldric's sketch once. "He will know. Maybe not immediately. But men like that can smell when they have been aimed."

"Then we make sure the target deserves the arrow."

"It does."

"Then his anger can arrive after the Choir falls."

Sable gathered the sketches. "I'll prepare the packet. Choir locations, victim names, safe houses, enough truth to make refusal impossible."

"No trail back to us."

"Please insult me less efficiently."

Banki almost smiled.

At the door, she stopped. "They are trying to help people."

"I know."

Sable's finger rested on Maren's sketch. 'They are trying to help people,' she said. 'That makes them harder, not softer.'

"Does that change anything?"

Banki looked at the sketches again: five good people on paper, already dangerous because paper had made them manageable and reality would not.

"It changes how careful we are," he said. "Not whether we survive them."

Sable left.

The room stayed cold after her.

## Maren's Song

The woman at the corner table was crying into her ale, and Maren Goldstring was listening.

Maren was not eavesdropping. She was listening the way taverns taught listening: with one hand near the cup, eyes soft, shoulders unthreatening, and every silence given room to confess what speech could not manage.

The woman's name was Pella. She was fifty-three. She worked at the salt-press on the south canal. Her son had been working there too, until two months ago, when he had come home one evening with a smile that didn't reach his eyes and had told her, in a voice that was not quite his voice, that he had found "a better path" and would be "moving to a quieter place."

He had left the next morning.

Maren took Pella's hand across the table. The contact was deliberate: touch was a channel, and Maren's bardic training had taught her to use every channel available. The warmth of skin on skin. The pressure of fingers. The unspoken message that said I am here, I hear you, you are not alone.

"Tell me about the smile," Maren said gently. "The one that didn't reach his eyes."

Pella described it. Not in clinical terms—Pella was not a clinician—but in the language of a mother who had spent twenty-six years learning to read her son's face and who knew, with the certainty that only a mother's knowledge provides, that the face she had seen that evening was her son's face wearing someone else's expression.

"It was like someone had told him to be happy," Pella said. "Not like he was happy. Like someone had—had written 'happy' on the inside of his face and his face was just... reading the instructions."

Maren's blood went cold.

Maren kept her face warm. Inside, the note under Pella's words went cold.

Compulsion.

The Choir.

But mothers noticed. Love heard wrongness before doctrine named it.

Maren squeezed Pella's hand.

"I believe you," she said. "And I'm going to find your son."

It was not a professional assessment. It was not a tactical commitment subject to revision based on changing circumstances. It was a promise, made by a woman who kept her promises with the same inflexible determination that Aldric applied to his vows and Seraphine applied to her prayers, and the promise settled between them like a stone dropping into deep water: heavy, final, generating ripples that would spread outward until they touched every shore.

Maren returned to the party's lodgings—a rented house on the Canal Row, warded by Lyris and blessed by Seraphine, protected against scrying and intrusion and the kind of psychic interference that the Choir reportedly employed—and she told Aldric what she had learned.

Aldric listened with the focused intensity of a man hearing a battle report. His jaw tightened at the description of the smile. His hands closed into fists at the description of the Watch's indifference. And when Maren finished, he sat in silence for ten seconds—which was, for Aldric, an eternity of deliberation, because Aldric's normal decision-making process was about as long as the time it took to draw his sword—and then he spoke.

"The Choir is primary," he said. "Everything else is secondary. Seraphine, I need a comprehensive detection sweep of the Dockside district—every mind-affecting residue, every compulsion signature, every psychic anomaly. Caelen, I need the Choir's physical infrastructure mapped—meeting points, safe houses, observation posts, supply routes. Lyris, I need ward-breaks for psionic mind control—something we can deploy on affected individuals to restore their original personalities."

He looked at Maren.

"And I need more stories like Pella's. Every missing person. Every altered personality. Every mother who noticed the smile that didn't reach her son's eyes. Build me a picture, Maren. Build me a picture of what the Choir has done to this city, and I will use that picture as a map to find them, and I will find them, and I will end them."

Maren nodded. She picked up her lute. She did not play it—not yet—but the weight of it in her hands was comforting as a sword's weight was comforting to Aldric: a reminder that she had tools, and that her tools were equal to the task.

Before she answered Aldric, Maren thought of the song she had been writing before Blackwater: a foolish thing about summer rain, unfinished because saving people always sounded more urgent than wanting anything for herself.

"I'll start tomorrow," she said. "Dawn."

"Dawn," Aldric agreed.

Before dawn, Aldric reminded Maren why people followed him.

A Watch constable on Canal Row shoved a gutter-boy into a wall for sleeping under the wrong awning. The child came up with one ankle folded wrong and both hands lifted, already apologising for being hurt.

Aldric crossed the street before anyone else moved.

He did not raise his voice. He did not draw steel. He put himself between the constable and the child with such calm finality that the constable remembered an appointment elsewhere. Then Aldric knelt in the gutter, healed the ankle with a touch, and gave the boy his breakfast coin without asking for gratitude, confession, or proof of innocence.

"Mercy first," Seraphine murmured.

Aldric only nodded.

An hour later, they caught a Choir runner in an alley behind the cooper's yard.

He was seventeen, maybe eighteen, too frightened to be important and too rehearsed to be innocent. Seraphine saw the smear of compulsion around him and reached for mercy first.

Aldric stopped her hand.

"After the names."

Maren looked at him. "He is half-bound."

"Then half of him can still choose."

The runner gave them three false doors before Aldric pressed his gauntleted hand against the boy's broken wrist and asked again. He did not shout. He did not enjoy it. That frightened Maren more than enjoyment would have, because enjoyment would have made him easier to condemn.

"One guilty hour," Aldric said when Seraphine whispered his name, "for a dozen unbroken lives."

The boy gave the fourth door.

It was real.

Aldric released him at once. Seraphine healed the wrist. Aldric wrapped his own cloak around the runner's shoulders and stayed beside him until the boy stopped shaking enough to drink.

"You are not abandoned," Aldric told him.

Maren wrote the address down and felt the first real crack open in her faith that good people stayed clean by wanting good hard enough. Aldric's mercy was real. So was the cruelty he had chosen to reach it.

The address did not look heroic. It looked like ink drying on cheap paper.

Maren folded it into her journal and heard the runner's small hurt breath every time the parchment bent.

At dawn, they moved.

Banki’s always-active Detect Thoughts was a net that rarely let anything slip, but a net can only catch what is swimming. It cannot distinguish between a fish and a lure designed to look like one.

Two hours before the auction, the southern dead-drop clerk, a jittery man named Orris whose thoughts were usually a muddy wash of gambling debts and fear, sat at the corner table of the Guttered Lantern. Banki, wearing his middling human shape, sat two tables away, his Mind-Sense extended like a thin wire.

Orris was thinking about a crate.

*The black iron box. Under the floorboards of the oil-merchant’s shed. The third pier. Veller’s men left it there. If I don't move it, the paladin will find it. It has the seal-stamps.*

The thoughts were sharp, clear, and hot with panic. There was no resistance, no defensive occlusion. Banki tasted the memory of the floorboards, the smell of linseed oil, the specific weight of the box. It was a perfect read—clean, direct, and completely sincere.

So Banki went. He did not send a Shadow; the stamps were too critical a variable to trust to subordinates.

The oil-merchant’s shed at the third pier was quiet, smelling of rancid seed and wet timber. Banki slipped through the side shutter under a Minor Shroud, his boots stepping silent on the oily floor. His Mind-Sense swept the building. Empty.

He found the loose floorboards under a stack of linseed barrels. He cleared them with a telekinetic nudge, exposing the black iron box. He reached down and pulled it free.

It was light. Too light.

The box was empty.

The instant his fingers closed on the handle, a trip-wire beneath the floorboards snapped.

Banki’s Mind-Sense exploded with the sudden, chaotic roar of five minds—not waiting, not thinking, but *acting*. They had been sitting in the adjacent cooperage, their minds shielded by lead-lined caps and a silence-ring, their triggers linked to the physical movement of the box. They didn't think about the trap; they simply waited for the trip-wire to pull a cord in their room.

A heavy iron grate dropped across the shed's doorway with a deafening clang. From the rafters above, three casks of quicklime were tipped.

The white dust billowed instantly, filling the small shed with a dry, choking cloud. Banki’s polymorph lungs flared with heat. Quicklime. If it touched moisture—if it reached his eyes or throat—it would boil.

He tried to cast, but his psionic focus was shredded by the chemical burn in his nose and the sudden, physical panic of the human form. His Telekinetic Shield buckled under the weight of the falling wood as the rafters themselves began to collapse. Alchemically-treated bone-dust bolts ripped through the dust from the side shutters, a hallmark of the city's horrific High Magic armaments.

One bolt caught him in the left shoulder, the iron head driving deep through the leather of his jerkin, twisting the polymorph collarbone. The pain was sharp, wet, and entirely physical.

His telepathy was useless here. The mercenaries outside weren't thinking about their plans or Veller's gold; they were simply reloading and firing into the dust on a count of three. There was no mind to read that could stop a bolt already in the air.

*Calibration,* Banki thought through the white heat in his shoulder. *An error in the intake.*

He did not scream. He did not waste breath.

He dropped his Minor Shroud—the invisibility was useless in the thick white dust anyway—and collapsed his human skeleton, polymorphing his frame down two inches, just enough to let the second crossbow bolt split the air where his ear had been. He lunged toward the drainage grate in the center of the oily floor. It was iron, rusted, and barred.

He didn't use psionics. He didn't have the focus to spare. He grabbed the rusted bars with his right hand, his muscles straining against the human limit, and wrenched the grate upward by leveraging the dislocated left shoulder against the floorboards. Bone popped. The iron groaned, the solder breaking under the sheer, ugly mechanical leverage.

He rolled into the muck of the drain just as a third volley of bolts splintered the floorboards above.

The sewer run was narrow, cold, and smelled of horse-piss and salt-sunk silt. Banki lay in the black water for three breaths, his dislocated shoulder screaming, his lungs coughing up white specs of lime that hissed in the muck.

He reached up with his right hand and popped his shoulder back into its socket against the stone arch of the drain. The pain made his vision turn grey for a heartbeat, but he kept his teeth clamped together.

*Orris was not a traitor,* he calculated. *Orris was a decoy who did not know he was a decoy. Veller's captains fed him the location of a box they knew was empty, knowing I would scan him. They used his sincerity to bait a physical trap.*

He looked up through the iron bars at the shed above. The mercenaries were laughing, their boots clumping toward the door to inspect the ruin.

Banki wiped the salt-muck from his face.

They had cost him a bolt, a shoulder, and twenty minutes of the auction's setup time. The ledger required balance.

He slipped through the sewer tunnel toward the canal exit. He would correct the mercenaries first, then Veller's captains, and finally the jittery clerk who had permitted his mind to be used as a billboard. He would do it without magic, because when a system fails, the builder must prove he can still use a hammer.

## The Auction

Private Viewing Day taught Banki two useful truths.

The first was that rich people became children the instant they believed they might be permitted near danger without consequence.

The second was that children wrapped in velvet and law-costume could still be excellent prey, provided one never bit too soon.

Sablehook opened its public face by degrees.

Morning brought the free daylight flow, which was less free than advertised in every way except the clerks’ ledgers.

Sable ran the floor as if she had been born to it.

No, Banki thought once while watching her from the edge of the scrim corridor. Better. People born to things often trust them too easily.

She wore the same dark scale-work as before, but on auction eve it had been set under a sleeveless overcoat cut to carry authority instead of stealth.

Banki let the house shine through them and kept himself, for most of the day, as a rumour with a pulse.

When he did walk the lanes, he did it alone or with only Null-Fang in quiet company.

Friday, April 17th. Seven o'clock in the evening. The auction began.

Sablehook's main hall had been transformed.

The hall had become a shallow amphitheatre, every buyer visible to Banki behind one-way glass. The Preview-24 programme had already done its work: five hours of supervised hunger, bribery attempts filed by Tally, and enough anticipation to make wealth behave badly.

Renn—the clerk Banki had assigned to manage the sealed-bid process—stood at the podium with the calm authority of a man who loved paperwork more than sleep and who considered the proper administration of a sealed-bid auction to be a form of art. His voice carried through the hall with the precise, unhurried cadence of someone who understood that clarity was more important than volume and that the buyers who mattered were listening regardless.

"Welcome to the inaugural Sablehook Live Specimen Auction," Renn said. "Tonight's offerings are organised in two categories. Category One: medium containment specimens. Category Two: rare and unique specimens. Bidding is sealed. Each bid envelope includes the offer amount, the intended use disclosure, and any additional terms—research access, breeding partnerships, future consideration—that the bidder wishes to include for the owner's evaluation."

He paused. Adjusted his spectacles.

"The owner evaluates bids on a discretionary basis. The highest monetary offer does not guarantee acceptance. The owner reserves the right to select bids based on total value, which may include non-monetary considerations."

Banki watched from his alcove. His Mind-Sense swept the hall in a continuous, invisible scan, touching every mind in the room, reading the surface impressions of forty-three buyers and their attendants with the effortless, multithreaded attention that decades of practice had made as natural as breathing.

The buyers were a cross-section of the western coast's wealth and ambition. Merchant princes from the coastal cities, their minds bright with calculation and competitive anxiety. And a handful of buyers whose surface thoughts were so carefully controlled that they were almost certainly professional intelligence operatives, sent by organisations whose interest in exotic specimens was less academic and more strategic.

Three of the buyers—S. Marrow, Orlisse Vant, and Berren Kyl—had been granted Verified status through the buyer pipeline that Sable had managed during Banki's absence. Two of those—Marrow and Vant—had been elevated to Premium, which gave them early access, private viewing time, and the implicit assurance that their bids would receive preferential evaluation. Not because they had paid more—though they had—but because the evaluation process had confirmed that their resources, their connections, and their potential as long-term partners justified the investment of privileged treatment.

The Not-for-Sale-but-Viewable specimens were displayed at the far end of the hall, separated from the auction lots by a ten-foot buffer zone and a subtle barrier ward that discouraged casual approach without being obviously hostile.

The sign above them read: SUBMIT OUTRAGEOUS OFFERS AT THE CLERK WINDOW.

Banki had written SUBMIT OUTRAGEOUS OFFERS AT THE CLERK WINDOW himself. Three buyers were already composing offers too embarrassing to admit aloud.
