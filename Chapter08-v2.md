# Chapter 8

## The Ironhorn Passage

The Brinewood’s humid, suffocating rot finally gave way to the brutal, vertical violence of the Ironhorn Spur. The transition offered no gentle foothills to ease the heavy wagons upward. The mountains did not slope; they ruptured from the earth like shattered bone punching through diseased skin. Jagged ridges of black granite scraped against a bruised, bleeding dawn sky. No soil clung to these inclines—only treacherous scree, deep blade-like shadows, and stone permanently offended by the passage of iron wheels.

The gap through the mountain—the Ironhorn Passage—was merely a suggestion of a path, a geological throat waiting to swallow the unwary. Dawn light died ten yards past the threshold, bleeding out against the stone until darkness became a physical weight pressing against the lungs. The cold was absolute—a sudden, shocking absence of the wood’s feverish heat that sank straight into the marrow.

Rinna Claystep led the caravan into the maw. The air inside the passage smelled of cold iron, centuries of dead dust, and the sharp, metallic tang of dormant magic. Industrial marrow-runes thrummed weakly in the tunnel walls, glowing with the dull crimson rhythm of a dying heart. The faint illumination revealed nothing useful, merely marking the boundary of an ancient territory carved by the empire before the fall. But beneath the stale stink of dead history lay something sharper: ozone—the crisp, electrical reek of a predator that hunted not by scent or heat, but by the subtle friction of living souls rubbing against reality.

The caravan crawled in single file. Six massive dire rhinos hauled the reinforced isolation wagons. Their iron-clad hooves struck the uneven floor, the impacts amplified by the narrow granite walls into the ponderous thud of a subterranean heart. The thick axles of the wagons groaned under the weight of lead plate and runic bracing—a high-pitched, grinding squeal that promised mechanical failure at the worst possible moment. The rhinos were tense, their iron-hard hides twitching at invisible stimuli, their breath pluming in the freezing air like steam from broken boilers.

Darric’s demands from the Sablehook yards echoed loud in the gloom, heavier than the stone overhead.

*No hybrid work without three signatures.* The factor’s voice remained a solid anchor in memory. He had stood in the muddy yard, refusing to yield an inch despite the mountains of armored muscle shifting behind him. *Yours, because you own the madness. Mine, because I know when bodies are being asked to lie. Hesta’s, because she knows when handlers die because clever men got ambitious.*

Hesta had spat tobacco into the dirt, driving her own spikes into the enterprise. *Any cross needs stop-conditions. Feeding plan. Pen plan. Birthing plan. Kill plan. Mercy plan. And if a handler says rest, food, or larger cages, the work stops until the handler is answered. A beast that kills a fool still leaves blood on the flagstones. I don't intend to mop up after your pride. You want to build a menagerie that defies the gods? Fine. But you pay for their anger in coin and steel, not in my people's flesh.*

They were right. The logistics of building an empire of flesh demanded unflinching pragmatism. Ambition untethered from strict doctrine produced only uncontrollable monsters. The beasts locked in the reinforced wagons were assets—investments of blood, time, and capital. Monsters born of vanity were catastrophic liabilities. Doctrine kept the handlers breathing.

Fifty feet ahead of the lead rhino, Kestrel moved with liquid silence. The scout used a low, climbing gait, trusting her fore-claws and balance over the treacherous scree. Dust rose faintly around her boots, instantly swallowed by the stagnant air.

Fresh tracks marred the gray powder.

Moving tracks.

The dust held the distinct shapes of heavy boots, then blurred instantly. The impressions shallowed and vanished, as if the stone itself were rapidly forgetting the weight that had just pressed into it.

*Contact.* The telepathic burst along the bond bypassed spoken words, delivering tactical data straight into Kestrel’s mind. *Presences ahead. Anomalous psychic signature. Halt and assess.*

Kestrel froze mid-stride—a statue of corded muscle and worn leather. One talon raised in a rigid halt-sign. The caravan ground to a jarring, squealing stop. The lead rhino snorted violently, throwing its plated head against the traces. Handlers swarmed the beasts instantly. Gravewire clamped onto the lead rhino's harness, his boots grinding into the shale. They whispered rhythmic cadences into ears flattened against armored skulls, pressing heavy gloved hands into gray hide, bleeding off the rising panic before it could turn to a stampede.

The darkness ahead of Kestrel shifted.

Nothing physical moved. The air simply redistributed its emptiness—a shifting of absence within absence, like a heavy velvet curtain yielding to a draft in a pitch-black vault. The temperature dropped fifteen degrees in a single heartbeat. White frost bloomed instantly across the iron rims of the wagon wheels. The marrow-runes on the walls flickered, their crimson glow swallowed by encroaching shadows.

*Nobody move,* Banki’s command lashed across the crew's mental network, rigid with absolute authority. *Nobody light anything. Zero illumination. Stand dead still. Breathe shallow.*

Human flesh became an immediate liability. He initiated the fey transformation.

The spine compressed first—vertebrae grinding together like stacked coins crushed in a vise, the discs between them rupturing in wet, silent bursts. Ribs folded inward, splintering at the cartilage, puncturing the lungs flat against a heart that seized, stopped, and restarted at a rate that turned the blood to a screaming, pressurized torrent through shrinking veins. Muscle tore from bone and re-knitted in the same instant—fibers shredding lengthwise, the raw nerves firing a white-hot cascade that the brain processed as one obliterating shriek. Organs crushed down, folding in on themselves like wet cloth wrung in a fist—kidneys, liver, stomach compressing to the size of seeds as the diaphragm lost the space to draw breath. Skin split and re-sealed, tight as a drum over every joint. Less than a second. An eternity of agony compressed into a single heartbeat, and then the tiny, perfect body hung in the dark, thrumming with fierce vitality. The pain was gone—amputated by the fey biology as cleanly as a severed limb.

The fey form vanished into the Ironhorn’s gloom, dead silent. No footfalls on stone. No displaced air in the dust. The bioluminescence of his large amber eyes was dialed down to a micro-smolder—a dying ember hidden behind thick wool.

He launched. The tiny body shot forward, riding the thermal currents generated by the rhinos' body heat, surfing the warmth toward the freezing anomaly ahead.

Shattercliff Darklings.

The scholars of the inner cities debated their existence over wine, dismissing reports as high-altitude hallucinations brought on by thin air. Research academies offered fortunes for a single viable specimen. Desperate generals traded the lives of thousands for a weaponized shadow. Entities that consumed ambient light and remembered the true names of the terrified. They were not beasts; they were hungry phenomena.

The nearest darkling turned.

A cold, crushing pressure settled directly onto his spine—a questing awareness sweeping the dark like a lighthouse beam of absolute zero. No malice lived in that touch; it possessed only an infinite, devouring hunger—a biological imperative that functioned less like a predatory appetite and more like a law of physics. It needed the energy of the living to maintain its grip on reality.

Standard mental subjugation meant instant death. The Sovereign Thread demanded rapid adaptation. Throwing telepathic weight at a void would only feed it, offering a banquet of psychic energy. An empathic bridge had to be built between the burning vitality of the fey form and the darkling’s endless emptiness.

Language fell away. Imagery dissolved into static. The cognitive suggestion stripped down to mathematical abstraction—equations of boundaries, the structural logic of containment, a bridge spanning the chasm between carbon biology and anti-biology. The geometry of nothingness.

Instead of wrapping telekinetic force around a mass to crush it, the psychic cradle inverted. Force projected outward, creating a defined boundary in the empty air—a spherical wall of rigid telekinetic pressure containing an absolute absence. An offering. A flawless, empty vessel for the void to occupy.

The darkling paused. Its endless consumption snagged on a beat of alien curiosity. The mathematical perfection of the container intrigued the entity.

It slid into the telekinetic sphere. It accepted the stillness. The cold pressure vanished from his spine, locked safely within the invisible psychic boundary.

One secured.

Six remained.

For twenty-three agonizing minutes, the black passage became a microscopic surgical theater. Each darkling possessed a unique resonance—a slightly altered frequency of hunger that demanded instant recalculation of the mathematical suggestion. A micro-adjustment to the empathic bridge. A rapid shift in the logical puzzle. The second capture required a absolute vacuum of thought. The third required an equation built on prime numbers.

Sweat did not form on fey skin, but the mental friction burned like battery acid behind his eyes. The focus required to hold multiple inverted telekinetic cages while navigating total darkness pushed the limits of the Sovereign Thread. The psychic weight threatened to shatter his tiny skull.

During the sixth capture, Gravewire shifted his weight on the scree. A shower of pebbles rattled against the granite. The lead rhino snorted—a sharp exhalation of panic. The sixth darkling reacted instantly, the void expanding to consume the sudden spike of sound. The mathematical cage warped, the boundary stretching thin. The calculation had to adjust dynamically, incorporating the acoustic wave into the sphere's geometry, expanding the boundary to swallow the sound before the darkling could feed on the fear behind it. The strain nearly snapped the bridge, driving a spike of pain through his temporal lobe, but the math held. The void settled.

Seven captures. Zero alarms.

The flight back to the wagon bench was sluggish, the air heavy against gossamer wings. The return to human form offered no relief. It never did.

Bones split lengthwise first—hairline fractures racing through the fey skeleton as each bone forced itself back to human proportions, the marrow expanding hot and sick inside the widening shafts. Tendons stretched past tolerance and tore, the damage radiating from joints in long waves that cramped his stomach. Lungs inflated like drowning in reverse—the diaphragm wrenching downward, the ribcage cracking outward, alveoli blowing open in a chain of tiny ruptures as they re-learned how to hold human volume. Organs bloated back to original mass, each pressing against its neighbors in a suffocating fight for space—liver swelling against stomach, kidneys grinding against spine, intestines uncoiling in a hot tangle that seized every muscle in his abdomen. Skin came last, stretching over the expanded frame like hide pulled taut on a tanner's rack, every nerve ending raw where the new surface met the freezing air.

The dull aches of human flesh settled into his joints, compounded by devastating psychic exhaustion. His lungs heaved, dragging in cold dust. Blood pounded in his temples.

"Clear," Banki rasped, his voice tasting heavily of copper.

The caravan lurched forward. The iron axles groaned, the sound echoing hollowly. The passage grew fractionally lighter, the crushing weight of the shadows lifting just enough to let lantern light reach a few feet further into the gloom.

In the reinforced Isolation Wagon, Kessa worked with silent efficiency. She transferred the contained entities from the telekinetic spheres into heavy glass jars lined with light-absorbing metamaterials. The jars replicated the Ironhorn's void, offering the darklings permanent stillness. They sat on the shelves, seven jars of absolute, perfect nothingness.

## The Shattercliff Highlands

The Shattercliff highlands punished soft boots and unearned confidence. By the second day out of the passage, the terrain had declared open war on the caravan.

Ridges broke into jagged shale teeth that shredded leather and snapped ankles. Deep valleys collected thick gray fog, sun-bleached bones, and a biting cold that turned fingers into wooden blocks. The wind scoured the rock, carrying ice particles and sharp dust that ground against the rhinos’ armor. Even Gravewire checked the stone twice with his spear-haft before committing his weight. Every step was a negotiation with gravity.

The Flickerbeast did not announce itself with a roar or bloody tracks. It announced itself with an argument against reality.

Absences marked the creature's position long before its body came into view. The northern edge of a cedar clearing smelled of wet rot and pine needles. The southern edge, identical in flora, reeked of ozone, panic, and sweating meat. High in the canopy, a flock of raptors abandoned sheltered branches in a scattered explosion of feathers, fleeing a threat with no visible mass. Thirty yards away, a pool of snowmelt rippled violently, the dark water splashing over mossy banks ten seconds before the wind crested the ridge to touch the surface.

Kestrel crouched low on a schist outcropping, pointing a talon toward the center of the clearing.

Aether-Sight peeled back the optical lie. The beast was not cloaking itself with simple illusion magic. It was actively misplacing itself in the fabric of space. Its multi-limbed feline lungs drew breath near a shattered cedar stump, while light bouncing off its fur reported its position ten feet to the left, near the pool. A localized, perpetual displacement. The ultimate evolutionary defense against targeting.

*Useful.*

The tactical value of an asset that could separate its physical mass from its visual representation far outweighed the logistical nightmare of catching it alive. A shock troop that could not be targeted. A strike unit that existed ten feet away from where the enemy aimed.

Vesper knelt in the scree, unrolling a canvas map of the clearing. The ranger set the first capture lane using chalk and flags—a funnel of steel netting anchored to ancient cedars to force the beast down the slope toward the wagons.

Kessa rejected it immediately, pointing a gloved finger at the wind currents. The updraft would carry the stink of human sweat and rhino musk straight into the lane. The beast would displace into the high peaks before touching the first net.

Hark grunted, chewing bitter root, proposing drop-snares triggered by tension tripwires.

Banki dismantled the second plan in three seconds. The heavy ropes required anchoring across a blind rock lip with zero visibility. Handlers operating blind lines against a creature that could shift its center of mass would end up with severed limbs or dragged over the cliff. Physics dictated that a displaced mass would violently snap a tether if anchor points were misaligned.

The third plan involved a three-point telekinetic pin, holding the physical mass while the capture team moved in with sedation poles. Banki rejected his own strategy. The displacement magic created an unstable disruption in the aether. A telekinetic grip would slip the moment the beast shifted coordinates, leaving an escape angle straight to the peaks.

The fourth plan relied on absolute patience, basic arithmetic, and a sacrifice of initiative. Which meant it would work.

They did not pursue the beast; they invited it to the wagons.

The crew slaughtered a mountain goat traded three days prior. Hark dragged the bleeding carcass across the rocks, laying a thick scent trail that overpowered human musk and rhino stink. The blood steamed in the freezing air, freezing into scarlet ribbons on the shale. They laid the carcass inside the open steel cage of Wagon Three.

No nets. No ropes. No magic.

Raw meat and an open box.

The wait stretched through two hours of freezing stillness. Frost coated rifle barrels. Handlers controlled their breathing with monastic discipline. Muscles cramped; joints locked. The cold seeped through heavy wool into the marrow.

The Flickerbeast took the bait.

The visual projection of the creature stalked from the treeline—a six-legged panther the color of storm clouds, muscled and fluid. Its eyes burned with cold violet light. But the physical sounds—the crunch of snow, the wet tearing of meat—occurred eight feet to the right of the projection.

It ate the goat. It stepped further into the box to reach the bloody cuts.

Banki tracked the true mass through Aether-Sight, ignoring the violet eyes in the rocks, focusing purely on the spatial displacement of the aetheric signature. He watched the invisible weight press into the wagon floor, saw the suspension sag under the unseen mass.

The true mass crossed the threshold.

The lever slammed down. The steel door of Wagon Three dropped like a guillotine, the locking mechanism engaging with a concussive boom that echoed off the ridges.

The Flickerbeast howled—a sound that shook dust from the rocks and set teeth rattling. The visual projection snapped instantly to the physical body as the localized displacement collapsed. The beast slammed against the steel bars, the wagon rocking violently on its axles, but the steel held. Violet eyes burned with trapped fury behind the bars.

Plan four. Executed.

## The Shardmantle Stalker

The Shardmantle Stalker communicated in a language the telepathic network refused to translate. No words, no internal imagery, no emotional resonance—only geometric certainty and the mathematical conviction of the kill.

The cliff face loomed over the caravan path—a sheer expanse of gray slate and razor-sharp schist. Exhaustion hung sour over the crew. The continuous climb, freezing winds, and adrenaline spikes had worn the handlers down to raw nerves. Reactions were slow; cold fingers fumbled on triggers.

The Stalker waited seventy feet up the rock.

Its crystalline plates folded into a configuration identical to the surrounding schist. The creature did not mimic stone; it became an extension of the geology. Edges sharp enough to shear steel rested where a tired boot might seek a foothold.

Mind-Sense swept the cliff face and reported inert minerals. No heartbeat. No respiration. Dead rock and ice.

Aether-Sight found the lie.

A violent concentration of predatory magic coiled within the "stone." The aether burned with massive kinetic potential—a crystalline spring waiting for the caravan to pass beneath the drop-zone.

Silence rippled through the crew. Handlers stopped the rhinos without a word, gloved hands gripping iron spears and repeating rifles.

Banki did not order a retreat. The Stalker held the high ground; lateral movement would trigger the strike. Retreat meant exposing their backs to a predator faster than a musket ball.

Telekinesis reached up the frozen stone—invisible, silent, precise. The grip bypassed the creature's impenetrable mind, locking directly onto the molecular structure of its crystalline plates.

Banki twisted the pressure.

The crystalline locks disengaged with a sound like grinding glass—a screech that set teeth on edge. Heavy plates shifted, forced outward by the telekinetic wedge.

For half a second, the creature was breathtakingly beautiful. The Stalker unfolded from the cliff—a massive predator constructed of faceted, razor-sharp light. Living prism catching the miserable highland sun, shattering it into a rainbow spray.

Then it dropped.

Gravity and lethal intent dragged it down toward the caravan like a falling star of glass and malice.

Complex magic took time. Subjugation required a psychic bridge. Containment required calculation.

The plunging Stalker offered zero time for math.

Banki bypassed the Sovereign Thread and tapped directly into the unrefined nightmare of the fey courts—the ancient magic that predated empires. Bracing his boots on the wagon bench, he raised his palm and unleashed raw force.

The Silent Screaming Skull erupted from his palm.

An Eldritch blast woven from abyssal horror and fey malice—a colossal projection of bone-dust and kinetic fury tearing through freezing air. The shockwave shattered frost on the surrounding rocks, leaving the reek of ozone, burnt marrow, and decay.

The blast slammed into the descending Stalker mid-air.

The creature shrieked—a sound that turned inner-ear fluid to ice, a mechanical screech of grinding glass and dying magic. The wave of pressure forced handlers to their knees.

Kinetic impact focused on the center mass. The calculation executed in a fraction of a millisecond: deliver enough blunt force to break the structural geometry, but not enough to shatter the core and destroy the specimen.

Crystal splintered in a explosion of light and shrapnel. Lethal symmetry broke. The creature spun out of control, slamming into the cliff face, tearing chunks of slate from the wall. One flailing claw clipped the outcropping where Kestrel crouched, cracking her brass spyglass in half before the weighted containment tentacles launched from the wagon below snapped taut. Rubberized cables wrapped tightly around the broken entity, dragging the thrashing mass of crystal down to the stone path with a heavy crash.

The Stalker thrashed against the cables, its rainbow refractions dulling rapidly into a broken, milky glare.

Kessa stood by the wagon wheel, staring down at the fractured entity leaking opalescent fluid that smoked in the cold air, smelling of crushed quartz and blood. She turned slowly, leveling a glare at the bench with more cutting power than the Stalker’s claws.

"It lives," her voice carried measured calm.

"Good."

"That is not fine. Carapace damage will take months of high-calorie feeding and mineral supplements to calcify."

"No. It is not fine." The response held zero apology. "But it is in the box. A broken asset is better than a dead crew."

Kestrel dropped lightly down from the rocks, holding up the ruined spyglass—its brass casing bent beyond repair. "You owe me a new one. Imperial glass. None of that cheap coastal rubbish."

"Charge it to specimen acquisition."

"I'm charging it to your face if the next rock decides to be alive."

A ragged burst of laughter rippled through the exhausted crew—the sudden release of tension almost physical. Gravewire leaned on his spear, chuckling darkly.

In the reinforced sledge, the broken Stalker caught their reflections in its shattered facets—jagged, distorted fragments held in the creature's cold patience.

## The Cracklight Manta

The Cracklight Manta hunted by making the air itself lie.

By the fourth day of the ascent, the Shattercliff peaks pierced the cloud layer. The air grew thin, burning lungs with every breath. The sky above the ridge was a brutal, flawless blue, untethered from the weather systems below.

Vesper spotted the anomaly drifting against prevailing wind currents—a prismatic shimmer moving effortlessly against a fifty-knot gale. High above, a flock of migratory snow-geese altered their formation to chase the shimmering light. The moment the lead birds crossed into the Manta's wake, they folded their wings and dropped like stones, stunned into unconsciousness by a localized pocket of altered, oxygen-starved air.

Ground harpoons and weighted nets fell hundreds of feet short, crashing uselessly into scree.

Banki went up.

The human body collapsed inward—spine compressing, ribs folding, organs crushing flat in a blinding instant of total-body agony that stopped his breath. Gossamer wings punched open from raw shoulders, catching violent highland thermals that threatened to tear the delicate membranes.

The ascent turned into a brutal physical struggle. Air currents around the peaks fought back with active malice. Pockets of cold negative pressure threatened to drag the tiny body into jagged granite spires. The cold bit deep, threatening to freeze the rapid beating of fey wings. The Manta rode those currents without a single flap of its translucent membrane—a supreme master of atmospheric pressure.

Down below, the wagons and crew shrank into tiny specks on gray slate.

The Manta’s alien psychology presented a complex problem. It did not process calm or fear as internal emotions. Calm was a stable place in the air. Fear was a drop in barometric pressure, a turbulent shear in the wind.

Direct combat against an entity that controlled the atmosphere would result in the Manta altering the weather to freeze him out, or fleeing into the stratosphere.

Strategy shifted from assault to architecture.

Telekinesis reached across the violent turbulence. The grip did not touch the creature; touching the Manta invited a localized hurricane. It touched the wind instead.

Inch by inch, telekinetic force smoothed the chaotic air currents in the Manta's flight path, flattening the gale into a stable, slow-moving column of air—an artificial pocket of perfect weather constructed in the center of a hurricane.

The Manta drifted toward the anomaly, its membrane dimming slightly as it tracked the scent of atmospheric safety. It swam gracefully through the sky, abandoning violent thermals for the calculated stillness of the construct.

The moment the creature entered the stable column, the psychic cradle closed around it—a sphere of hardened air. The Manta offered zero resistance, content to float within simulated tranquility, its bioluminescence strobing in a slow rhythm.

The descent demanded absolute focus.

Bringing the massive sphere down through hostile wind layers required continuous recalculation. A violent updraft tearing off a lower ridge slammed into the bubble, forcing a hard correction. The force of the gale threatened to shatter the construct, throwing the Manta against the inner edge. One sweeping wingtip scraped against a sharp slate spire.

Alarm spiked in the alien mind as internal pressure shifted violently. The Manta thrashed, colors on its membrane flashing angry red, preparing to alter the weather and tear the sphere apart.

Banki's response was instantaneous. His telekinetic grip clamped down on the air inside the sphere, holding the currents mathematically rigid around the wound. The pressure forced the atmosphere to insist, with unyielding authority, that the world remained safe—that the pain was an anomaly, and the air was an unbroken sanctuary.

The Manta calmed, angry red fading back to peaceful azure.

On the valley floor, Kessa waited beside an open transport wagon covered by a translucent canopy designed to trap the beast without blocking its view of the sky. Faint wards on the canopy mimicked high-altitude light.

The sphere lowered smoothly into the enclosure. Heavy doors locked shut with a definitive clang. Inside the wagon, the telekinetic sphere dissolved. The Manta settled, its membrane shimmering in rhythmic pulses of azure and violet as it dreamed of weather it could trust.

The fey form hit the bench and came apart. Bones punched outward through reforming muscle, each joint popping with a wet crunch as the skeleton forced itself back to human scale. Skin stretched so fast it split along forearms in thin lines that wept clear fluid before sealing. Lungs ballooned against a cracking ribcage, each breath a raw act of violence against a chest cavity remembering how to be large. Organs swelled back into place, the heart slamming against the breastbone in thick thuds. Cold bit immediately into sweat-soaked clothes, setting expanding muscles to violent shivering. Blood ran freely from his nose.

Vesper stood near the front wheel, staring at the gentle light radiating from the wagon, his voice quiet. "How did you do that? The harpoons fell short."

"Made the air stop arguing." Banki's voice cracked, hoarse from freezing wind and psychic strain.

"You used telekinesis on the weather."

"On a small piece of weather."

Kessa stood by the reinforced bars, pressing gloved fingers against cold glass near the creature's scraped wingtip, her expression tight with professional respect and fundamental unease. "It trusted the air."

"Yes."

"Then do not make the air lie to it again unless you must."

The hypnotic colors moving beneath the membrane cast long shadows across the stone path. The Cracklight Manta had accepted a steel cage because the cage had been engineered to resemble the open sky.

"Noted." The response carried the crushing weight of the freezing peaks, settling into the bones like ancient dust. The lie was necessary. The lie was the work. And the work was far from over.
