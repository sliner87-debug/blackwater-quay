Kestrel had chosen her priorities already, which pleased him more than obedience would have.

## Auction Night

The auction floor smelled of lanternlight, lacquered wood, and the predatory patience of rich people pretending they were not hunting.

Banki stood at the podium—not behind it, at it, because the distinction mattered.

The reactions were satisfying. Three buyers registered the sceptre's magical signature and felt their confidence contract by measurable degrees. "Before we begin," Banki said. His voice carried the Tri-Weave's motivational harmonic—not coercive, not magical in any detectable sense, but shaped by the same frequency that the Sovereign Thread used to influence consciousness, dialed down to a level that produced not compulsion but attention. No personal handling beyond the line unless I grant it. Payment clears at the clerk window, and our people move what you've won."

Laughter. Appreciative murmurs. Two offended nobles who forgot they were in his house.

Different side of the glass.

Around him, the house breathed as if it had found its lungs.

## The Rinse Below

The Rinse Below was a workshop that existed in the space between crime and craft.

It occupied the sub-basement of a condemned Wharfside tenement whose relationship with legality was as flexible as its relationship with gravity. The Crafters' Compass had led him through three false approaches, two damp stairwells, and a door that pretended not to be a door.

Banki intended to own all three.

The Rinse Below fell first.

He entered invisible.

The guard fell first. A Screaming Skull blast of eldritch force moved through damp air and struck consciousness like a hammer touching a bell. His sword clattered; forge noise swallowed the sound before it became alarm.

The assistant went next. Same technique. Same result. Two unconscious bodies, positioned carefully to avoid injury during the fall, because Banki's violence was precise and deliberate and did not include collateral damage that could be avoided through minimal additional effort.

The craftsman—the Glasswright, as the criminal network's internal nomenclature identified him—was more complex.

He was standing at a workbench when Banki materialised behind him, and his first reaction was not fear but annoyance—the particular irritation of a master craftsman who has been interrupted mid-process and whose immediate concern is not the intruder but the delicate, horrific industrial enchantment that the interruption might have destabilised.

"Don't touch the flux," the Glasswright said, without turning around. "If the flux destabilises, the whole batch is—"

He turned. Saw Banki. Saw the expression on Banki's face.

Stopped talking.

The Glasswright was a gnome. Small, elderly, with the oversized eyes and the nimble fingers of a race that had been building things since before humans had discovered the concept of "things" and that regarded every other race's craftwork with the particular blend of condescension and pity that master artisans reserve for talented amateurs. His workshop was extraordinary—a miniature cathedral of glass and metal and precision instruments, every surface covered with the tools and materials of a craft that operated at the intersection of chemistry, metallurgy, and horrific High Magic.

Banki read his mind.

Banki took the Glasswright carefully, which was worse than haste. Names first. Then hands. Then the little craft memories that lived below language: heat by colour, fraud by weight, the precise shame of a maker who had spent decades making false wonders for clients rich enough to prefer fraud to failure.

The information was extensive. The Glasswright had been operating in Blackwater Quay for thirty-seven years. He had produced, by his own count, over four thousand forged magical items—amulets that appeared to be enchanted but contained only a cosmetic aura, weapons that registered as magical on standard detection but whose "enchantments" were visual effects rather than functional enhancements, identity tokens that could fool any mundane verification process and most magical ones. His work was, by any objective standard, brilliant. His ethics were, by the same standard, nonexistent.

Banki extracted everything: names, techniques, client lists, the two nodes he had not yet visited—Dry Kiln and Quiet Loft—and enough craftsmen, guards, couriers, and fences to make the ring legible.

Then he did something that he had never done before.

The Neural Savant theft had always been flesh to flesh: brain tissue to brain tissue, skill ripped from a living source and lodged where Banki could use it.

But what if the transfer could be redirected? What if, instead of incorporating the target's stolen skill-web into his own brain, Banki could channel it outward—into an object? A vessel. A container that could hold a consciousness without requiring a biological substrate to support it.

The idea was obscene enough to be useful.

But the Glasswright's workshop contained something that might serve: a bone-fused gem artifact inscribed with marrow runes. A twin-pointed crystal formation that the Glasswright had been using as a focus for his enchantment work—a piece of mineral whose natural crystal lattice had been modified by decades of proximity to active, industrialized High Magic crafting until it had developed the kind of resonant capacity that made it, theoretically, capable of holding a consciousness the way a bottle holds wine.

Theoretically.

Banki had never tested the theory. He was about to.

He set one hand on the Glasswright's temple and the other on the twin-gem crystal. The bridge opened through him. Not inward this time. Out.

The Glasswright screamed.

The scream was not the physical scream of a man in pain—Banki's telekinetic suppression of the vocal apparatus prevented that.

The crystal pulsed. Its facets blazed with light—not reflected light but generated light, the luminous exhale of a mineral lattice that had just received a charge of raw consciousness and was reorganising its internal lattice to accommodate the new contents. The twin points glowed with opposite colours—one warm amber, one cold blue—and between them, in the heart of the crystal, something moved. Not physically. Not visually. Something that existed behind the light, beneath the facets, in the space where matter became mind and mind became trapped.

The Glasswright's body went limp. His eyes remained open, but they were empty—not the emptiness of death, which retains a residual expression, but the emptiness of vacancy, the particular blankness of a shell that has been vacated by its occupant and has not yet been repurposed.

Banki examined the crystal. The consciousness within it was alive—pulsing, aware, trapped in a prison of mineral and High Magic that would hold it indefinitely, preserving every memory, every skill, every piece of knowledge that the Glasswright had possessed, in a format that could be consulted, interrogated, and exploited at any time in perfect alignment with the Divine Calculus.

The Twin-Gem Glasswright Reliquary. An artifact. Created through a technique that no one had ever attempted and that Banki, with characteristic understatement, considered "a successful experiment."

He pocketed the reliquary. He secured the workshop's contents—tools, materials, client records, the forge itself. He bound the unconscious guard and assistant. He composed a brief, businesslike note that he left on the workbench:

NEW MANAGEMENT. REPORT TO SABLEHOOK FOR REASSIGNMENT. NONCOMPLIANCE IS INADVISABLE.

Then he climbed the ladder, passed through the tunnel, emerged behind the fishmonger's stall, and walked into the fog.

The Rinse Below was his.

In the fog ahead, two doors in the web still kept their lights on.

## The Predator's Verdict

Banki did not give Kestrel her true place in public.

Public things could be bought, praised, envied, misunderstood, and stolen. Kestrel had earned more than a collar, more than a lot number, more than the lazy language men used when they meant valuable animal and hoped the animal would not understand.

The controlled pen waited behind three thresholds, each one quieter than the last.

This was not a creature stall. Not a public holding. Not even an owner's lane. It was private dominion: iron-banded door, shadow-sunk hinges, climb wall, bare perch beam, ceiling rails, room enough for a thinking predator to choose height before anyone offered ground.

She waited on the beam when he entered.

Cliff-Skitter Predator. Broodline Prime. Tri-Weave Soulblade Line.

The dossier had called her lean. Accurate only if famine could be called winter. In Kestrel, lean meant no ounce wasted: hooked talons, double-jointed hind limbs, tail held in a questioning curve whose answer was probably blood.

Null-Fang shifted slightly at his side.

"Observe," Banki said without taking his gaze off Kestrel. "Do not taste."

The sceptre obeyed.

Kestrel remained on the beam. Fine. Not cowering. Not challenging. Awaiting terms.

Banki gave it.

He crossed to the centre of the pen, stopped within leaping distance, and tapped once on the lower shelf beam built into the wall.

"That is yours if you choose it. Home-base. Perch-zone. No one takes it from you without my permission. No one but family uses it."

Kestrel's head tilted. One foreclaw lifted, flexed, and settled again.

Then, with a movement so exact it felt chosen for the effect it would have on the room, she came down from the upper beam, crossed the floor in three silent steps, and leapt to the indicated perch. There she crouched, tail wrapped once around the wood, all the lines of her body narrowing into an acceptance more eloquent than submission.

A beginning.

Banki stood a long breath and let the moment be only itself.

Many men would have reached to touch. Many men deserve the scars that follow such impulses.

He instead opened his mind, carefully, and offered not command but question.

You may choose a name.

Not the stable tag. Not the lot mark. Not the public convenience by which lesser people would discuss her when forced. A true name, if she wanted one. Something that held more than one truth at once.

What came back was not language as men used it, but layered impression given enough shape to become words inside him. Wing-shadow over a ledger. Talon writing where ink feared to dry. Moth dust on lamplight. A veil that cut while covering. Height. Motion. Witness. Family.

He said it aloud piece by piece, letting her refuse if she wished.

"Kestrelveil."

Her eyes brightened.
