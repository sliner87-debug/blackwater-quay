# Chapter 09

## SECTION 1: THE TAXONOMY OF EVOLUTION

The architecture of Sablehook functioned less as an unyielding fortress of stone and iron and more as an enormous, predatory lung. Day and night, it drew in the desperate, the ruined, and the ruthlessly ambitious of Blackwater Quay, straining them through a filtration system of extreme violence, marrow-siphon magic, and unsparing accounting, exhaling pure profit and terrifying leverage. Banki stood at the central pivot of this respiratory cycle, acutely aware that a single uncorrected tear in the ledger could trigger a catastrophic collapse faster than an invading army carrying siege engines.

In the wake of disaster, lesser administrators sought the dulling narcotic of sleep. Banki preferred the razor-thin friction of cold administration. Sleep was a biological vulnerability masquerading as rest; relentless work was blood-slicked armor.

He traversed the vaulted corridors of the administrative wing, where ancient stone walls bled condensation and deadened the muffled screams echoing from the breeding pens far below. The air in this sector carried a deliberate, suffocating sterility—a sharp, stinging brew of crushed chalk, beeswax, polished ironwood, and the ozone tang of minor scrying wards. It was the sterile stench of bureaucracy attempting to chain volatile, ravenous biology.

He pushed open the heavy oak double doors of the primary review chamber.

The room was dominated by a massive, scarred table cut from the heart of an ancient ironwood tree. The timber bore a bloody provenance: deep gouges, scorched brand-marks, and dried stains spoke silently of past administrative failures that had not ended in mere dismissal. At the far end, the Direction Two Head sat pinned to a brass-backed chair like a specimen awaiting dissection. Beadlets of cold sweat gathered at the man's hairline, glistening under the unblinking, interrogative glare of lumen-globes overhead. To his left, a pale attendant shuffled a thick sheaf of vellum performance reports, handling the sheets with the terrified, exaggerated care of a man trying not to ignite a live fuse.

"Then we should review by head performance and line state," the attendant offered, his voice a dry, hollow rasp. "Not by simple vacancy."

Banki cut through the dim room to the head of the table. He did not sit. The physical elevation forced the Direction Two Head to crane his neck upward, compressing his windpipe—a subtle, immediate exertion of sovereign dominance.

"Correct," Banki said. The single word dropped like an anvil onto a stone floor. "The board is ready for that version now."

He leaned forward, planting his knuckles into the gouged ironwood. He allowed the silence to stretch, measuring the precise psychological pressure required to fracture the Head’s carefully constructed excuses without shattering the man’s underlying utility.

"We will examine the live breeding floor," Banki continued, his cadence flat, devoid of theatrical fury. Anger was sloppy, wasteful energy; the unadorned delivery of undeniable fact cut far deeper. "We will judge the heads by the monstrosities they breed and the handlers struggling to survive them. But first, we address the cowardice festering at this table."

The Direction Two Head flinched. The tremor was microscopic—a slight spasm in the jaw muscle, a fractional tight shift of the shoulders—but to Banki’s hyper-attuned senses, it shrieked of profound, undeniable guilt.

"Direction Two is bifurcating," Banki declared, stripping away all clerical euphemisms. "The biological line is splitting in half. And you have squandered the last three weeks attempting to bury a living genetic mutation beneath romantic administrative rhetoric."

"My lord," the Head began, the words breathy and frantic. "The line was... fluctuating. Output B demonstrated a far more violent trait concentration, yes, but we anticipated a natural regression to the mean. We were watching."

"You were waiting," Banki corrected, severing the lie with surgical coldness. "You were nursing the weak hope that the flesh would make the executive decision for you, allowing you to merely record the outcome in a tidy ledger. You used 'watching' language long after the split became a living reality. You permitted a variance spike before your department possessed the reporting taxonomy to codify what it might unleash."

Through the deep, humming channels of the Tri-Weave, Kestrel’s consciousness brushed against his own like a razor edge scraping stone. *Cowardly clerical romance. Always ugly. Always weak.*

Banki allowed a fraction of a heartbeat to absorb her evaluation. It was flawlessly accurate. Bureaucrats loathed raw biology because living flesh refused to abide by ink and parchment.

"The line has not gone sterile," the Head argued, his voice acquiring a thin, reedy edge of desperation. "It has not collapsed into mindless, rotting mutation! This is a wildly productive split. Output B is granting us unprecedented upside in targeted expression—faster emergence of complex predatory behaviors—"

"And far more dramatic, blood-soaked test moments," Banki interrupted. "And vastly higher risk per successful specimen. That is precisely the breed of split that seduces incompetent managers into lying to themselves."

"The broader side grants us a durable, resilient base!" the Head pleaded, pressing his palms flat against the ironwood.

"The Direction's sole merit is that neither branch is worthless," Banki agreed, shifting his weight. "The sturdier branch grants us operational scale. The sharper side grants us the possibility of a lethal breakthrough. The failure does not lie in the flesh. The failure lies in your cowardice and your timing. You permitted handlers to believe they were managing a single unified program with odd outliers, rather than two distinct evolutionary vectors with entirely different lethality profiles and containment demands."

Banki stepped away from the table, turning to the massive chalkboard that covered the eastern wall. He seized a stick of hard, white chalk. The sharp *clack* of mineral against slate echoed like a hammer strike.

"Tell me what occurs if this split is executed crudely."

The Head swallowed hard, his throat clicking. He was an intelligent man; his fundamental flaw was conflict avoidance, not stupidity. Banki intended to excise the cowardice while preserving the intellect.

"If split too crudely," the Head recited, staring down at his own trembling hands, "support resources will fray. One branch will cannibalize allocation, food stock, and containment wards from the other. Personnel will divide themselves by personal temperament rather than specialized discipline. Procurement logic will rot unless ruthlessly centralized."

"And the biological reality on the floor?" Banki pressed.

The Head looked up, the unvarnished truth finally spilling out without administrative polish. "The sharper branch is already selecting favorites. Certain specimens respond violently depending on who enters their pen. It is not affection, my lord. It is the cold recognition of handler competence, pulse-rhythm, and pressure style. The sharper side is actively sorting our staff into two distinct categories: the useful, and the meat."

Banki paused, the chalk hovering a fraction of an inch from the slate.

That was the critical, terrifying data point. The specimens were evaluating their captors. The monsters were forging their own hierarchy of respect based on the tactical competence of the humans attempting to cage them. Evolution was asserting its own sovereign dominance structure.

"The sharper branch is the vital seed for future specialty hybrids," the Head continued, sensing the weight of Banki's silence. "It will yield the raw material Direction Four will eventually covet. But the sturdier branch risks being neglected because its violence is less theatrical. That would be a fatal error. It remains our premier long-term reservoir for scale, bone-density, and foundational support stock. This is not a healthy line paired with a broken mutation. It is two wildly successful impulses that now demand separate, uncompromising futures."

Banki turned back to the room. It was a sound analysis. A rigorous, structurally sound diagnosis. Once backed into a corner, a manager capable of perceiving the floor's raw reality remained a valuable asset.

"Status," Banki declared, his tone bearing the finality of an iron portcullis slamming shut. "You remain in command."

The Head exhaled a long, shuddering breath, his shoulders sagging with relief.

"However," Banki added, crushing the man's relief in the same breath, "formal bifurcation doctrine is in effect as of this second. Direction Two remains administratively unified on paper, but the biological line is formally recognized as split. Separate the operational doctrine, the handler training logic, and the reporting language immediately. Do not attempt to force a single containment assumption onto two divergent evolutionary demands."

"Yes, my lord. At once."

"A formal reprimand is inked into the high registry," Banki said, his voice dropping into a quiet, cold instructional register. "You are not being disciplined for speaking the truth now. You are being punished for withholding it then. Learn the difference. If a future threshold decision cannot be resolved at your level, you escalate to me directly. Next time, contact me when you require a decision, before the flesh forces your hand."

"I will," the Head promised, the desperation vanishing, replaced by the rigid focus of a man who had stared into his own execution and emerged with a clear operating mandate.

"Dismissed."

The Head and his attendant gathered their files, bowed deeply, and retreated from the chamber. Banki stood alone in the cold silence. He stared down at the scarred ironwood table, mentally remapping the shifting architecture of his domain. Blood, bone, and ledger ink. To rule Sablehook demanded absolute mastery over all three, and the unrelenting discipline to ensure none ever corrupted the rest.

## SECTION 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF RECOVERY

By late afternoon, the muted light filtering through the high, spell-reinforced glass of Sablehook Hall’s receiving room cast long, geometric shadows across the polished floorboards. Outside, the corridor remained buried in deep, expectant quiet. It was neither the reverent stillness of a cathedral nor the terrified silence of an execution block, but the heavy, vigilant hush unique to Sablehook—an atmosphere where every stone seemed conscious of rank and discretion.

Banki pushed open the heavy timber door, stepping inside without a sound.

The ambient air smelled of scorched ozone, clean linen, and the copper-bitter tang of exhausted adrenaline. Along the far wall, the children sat on low, padded benches, their postures laying bare the narrative of their ordeal.

Penny Reed cradled the stitched companion, Larklet, against her sternum. She did not clutch the fabric-and-marrow construct with the frantic panic of a child expecting it to be snatched away; she held it as a physical anchor. Her breathing rose and fell in precise synchronization with the engineered heart-pulse of the construct, grounding her shattered mind against tactile reality.

Elid Fen sat with both arms wrapped loosely around Cinder. The boy stared blankly into the shadows, his eyes hollowed out by horrors no child was built to absorb. Yet his fingers remained woven tightly into the companion's coarse fur—a subconscious refusal to sever his last link to sanity.

Kestrel stood shadowed near the door frame. She made no attempt to soften her lethal silhouette or adopt a docile posture. She remained fully, terrifyingly present—a living miracle of hooked talons, coiled tendon, and razor-sharp geometry. Yet her complete immobility radiated an uncanny, predatory reassurance. She was not a pet; she was violence held in reserve, an unyielding promise that whatever horrors had hunted these children would be stalked, gutted, and dismantled in turn.

Sable materialized in the hall a heartbeat later, slipping through the doorway like a shadow taking form. She did not disturb the fragile sanctuary of the room. She simply stood, a dark leather folder tucked beneath her arm, her sharp features set in the expression of a woman carrying three hidden daggers and calculating which one to draw first.

Her gaze executed a single, sweeping audit of the space: Penny, Elid, Larklet, Cinder, Kestrel, and finally Banki.

"Both bonded cleanly," Sable reported, her voice kept at a low, precise murmur that barely carried beyond Banki's ears. She indicated Penny, then Elid. "No sympathetic panic spike upon transfer. No biological rejection of the marrow-threads. No immediate psychological dissociation."

The neural engineering had held. The companions functioned precisely as Banki had designed them—acting as living cognitive bridges between paralyzing trauma and functional recovery.

"The house line is established," Banki said, turning his back to the room to face Sable directly in the corridor. "No external contact. No sympathetic interviews from well-meaning staff attempting to ease their own consciences. They speak to the assigned handlers, they speak to you, and they speak to me. Anyone else attempting to extract a narrative from these children answers directly to the extraction chambers."

"Understood," Sable said. She tapped the heavy folder against her thigh. "Political containment is currently holding. The House Denholt and Veyl Marrin fallout control requires immediate structural narrative architecture. If we do not feed the rumors precisely what we want them to digest, the Quay will invent them. I have the first Silent Mouth names ready for review, along with the Section Four successor movement."

"We will review the packet-hunt escalation tonight," Banki confirmed.

He allowed his gaze to drift back toward the children for a fraction of a second. They were assets now. Not out of malice, but out of the cold, undeniable reality of Blackwater Quay. Safety was a premium commodity, paid for exclusively with utility. Their utility, for the moment, was simply survival.

"Ensure their housing rules are enforced rigidly," Banki instructed, his tone shifting back to pure logistics. "Structure is the only known antidote to profound chaos. Feed them. Guard them. Let the companions do the heavy, invisible lifting of restoring their neural pathways. The world outside this fortress is currently burning; they do not need to smell the smoke."

"It is already done," Sable said, her demeanor radiating the crisp autonomy of an undisputed peer. She slid a single sheet of vellum from her folder—a heavily ciphered manifest of operational targets. "The board is prepared for the evening."

Banki took the parchment. The ink was still damp, the target names cut sharp. The afternoon had belonged to the maintenance of the saved and the stabilization of internal biology; the night belonged to the Salt Roads, and the acquisition of dangerous things.

## SECTION 3: THE HARDWARE OF DISASTER

By eight in the evening, the character of Blackwater Quay underwent its nightfall metamorphosis. The daylight pretense of legitimate, taxable commerce burned away, exposing the predatory, transactional throat of the Salt Roads. Deep within the Oddities Market of Brineglass Lane, thick coastal fog rolled in off the bay, trapping the foul stench of stagnant brine, rotting timber, and unwarded magic against the cobblestones. Flickering amber lantern light struggled through the gloom, illuminating stalls draped in heavy velvet and spell-hardened glass.

Banki cut through the crowded aisles with the terrifying assurance of an apex predator. The dense tide of rogues, cutpurses, and disgraced mages parted before him instinctively, pulling back like water striking the iron prow of a warship. The deference was not requested; it was demanded by the sheer density of his presence.

Kestrel prowled at his right shoulder, her tail flicking in a tense, questioning curve, her double-jointed limbs moving without a whisper of sound. Bound to his left wrist, the newly forged Null-Fang rested against his forearm. The Tri-Weave enchantment nested within the dark metal hummed directly into Banki’s nerve clusters—a coiled, sentient intelligence tasting the chaotic ambient magic of the lane.

They arrived at the iron-shod stall of Lysa—a vendor specializing in the exact intersection of high paranoia and lethal survival.

Her shop bore no gaudy signboards, no barkers hawking wares. It smelled of old ozone, cold polish, and the bitter, coppery tang of alchemical preservatives. Lysa stood motionless behind a reinforced counter, her gnarled hands resting atop a black velvet pad.

"Material fitting costs and immediate quantities," Banki demanded, eschewing all greeting. Courtesy was a tax on time he refused to pay.

Lysa offered a single nod, her face a mask of practiced neutrality. Her fingers moved rapidly across the felt, sorting her inventory into immediate hand-offs, reserved stock, and long-term contract lines.

She lifted a shimmering, translucent coil with delicate, gloved fingers. "Ghost-Shed Tendril Fiber. Three thousand four hundred gold pieces for a single coil. Nine thousand five hundred for a matched three-coil lot harvested from the same specimen batch. I hold five coils in reserve. Two are paired tightly enough for high-precision containment work."

Banki calculated the structural applications in an instant. Ghost-Shed fiber possessed immense tensile strength combined with near-absolute magical invisibility. It was the ideal substrate for containment harnesses that required invisible reinforcement, entirely immune to standard dispel-craft or scrying wards.

"Hold the matched pair from wandering into lesser hands for the duration of this exchange," Banki said. It was not a formal purchase yet, but the order carried the implicit threat of violent ruin if disobeyed.

Kestrel let out a low, guttural click of approval from her throat. *Good.*

Null-Fang’s psychic voice vibrated cold and sharp against Banki’s mind. *Now to the hardware that waits for the right catastrophe.*

Lysa’s eyes narrowed into sharp slits. "Ah. Those."

She gestured to her apprentice. Three dark-wrapped parcels were retrieved from a hidden wall safe, alongside two heavy, iron-banded caskets from beneath the counter. The ambient noise of the lane seemed to die away as the velvet wrappings were peeled back. These were not public goods. These were the tools kept exclusively for patrons who worked in post-breach recovery, corridor reconstruction, and the bloody business of hunting entities that evolved faster than men could build cages.

The first item unveiled was a reel of Brineglass Echo Thread.

Null-Fang approved immediately, sending a ripple of cold delight through the bond. *Exquisite. A thread to prove where the lie stepped.*

Banki grasped its utility at once. It operated as a post-incident spatial tracker, maintaining an indelible memory of physical displacement, rendering invisibility, phase-shifting, and localized teleportation completely traceable.

The second object claimed his total attention. It was a minuscule capsule of metal and blown glass, no larger than the knuckle of a thumb, bound by a dark iron ring and capped with an impossibly intricate stopper.

"The Three-Breath Capsule," Lysa whispered, her tone dropping into the quiet register of high-lethality trade. "Shattered under pressure, it grants the wielder three completely clean, uncorrupted breaths in a hostile atmosphere."

"Define parameters," Banki said.

"Necrotic gas. Suffocating smoke. Drowning panic. Acidic mist. Arcane vacuum. Foul rooms."

The tactical applications surged through Banki’s mind. Field units operating in forgotten ruins; hybrid pens flooded with suppressing agents; underwater recovery; auction house sabotage. It was the precise definition of an item that was entirely useless right up until the exact microsecond it became the sole barrier between an asset and a agonizing, lung-melting death.

Kestrel’s golden eyes locked onto the small glass cylinder, her pupils shrinking to vertical slits. *That category. Yes.*

Lysa unclasped the iron bands of the next casket, revealing a slender pin of blackened steel, its head crowned with an iridescent, void-black bead.

"The Drowned Pin," Lysa said. "It shatters a single, cascading panic-spiral. Once."

Banki stepped closer, tracing the microscopic marrow-runes etched into the steel. Mental armor in physical form. "Elaborate."

"Not generalized fear," Lysa clarified. "Not madness or direct enchantment. But that specific, horrific moment when the mind fractures from 'I am in severe danger' into 'I am already dead and lost.' The pin interrupts that downward drop. It forces a cold cognitive reset, granting a narrow window to fight back."

Probationary Shadows navigating psychic horrors; child trauma units; handlers exposed to sudden psionic assault.

*A small mercy forged from cold iron,* Null-Fang mused, his thoughts laced with dark amusement.

The final casket held the Crooked Measure—an articulated brass folding rule. But as Banki adjusted his angle of vision, the etched increments warped and shifted. None of the lines remained true; they bent according to the observer's perspective.

"An oddity-grade survey tool," Lysa explained. "For environments where standard spatial geometry becomes a fatal delusion."

"Folded space," Banki stated instantly. "Dimensional pockets. Containment geometries for entities that manipulate their own physical footprint."

"This is uglier than it looks," Kestrel noted through the Tri-Weave, eyeing the twisting brass with deep instinctual distrust.

"Which is precisely why it functions," Lysa said.

Banki synthesized the Triad’s feedback. Kestrel favored the Echo Thread, the Crooked Measure, and the Three-Breath Capsules—her logic anchored in predatory survival: reveal the displacement, measure the folded space, survive the poisoned room. Null-Fang favored the Echo Thread, the Drowned Pin, and the fiber—his logic rooted in metaphysical truth: track the lie, preserve the self, reinforce the cage.

From an administrative perspective, Banki required the entire arsenal.

"Package them," Banki ordered without room for negotiation. "The matched Ghost-Shed pair, the Echo Thread, the Capsules, the Pin, and the Measure. Draft the total directly to the Sablehook primary logistics account. Have your courier deliver them to the receiving vault within the hour."

Lysa bowed low, her eyes glinting. "A pleasure doing business with the architecture of the future, my lord."

## SECTION 4: THE TAXONOMY OF JUNK

By half-past eight, Banki had descended into the lower subterranean rings of the Salt Roads, arriving at a chaotic stall labeled Salvage Curios. The vendor here possessed none of Lysa’s cold elegance; he was a twitchy, grease-stained scavenger standing behind a disarray of stripped brass gearwork, cracked scrying lenses, fractured containment plates, and unidentifiable technological viscera.

Banki did not waste words on the man. He pointed a leather-gloved finger at the cluttered workbench.

"Kestrel. Audit the faceplate and the anchor hearts." He tapped the dark metal of Null-Fang against his wrist. "Null-Fang. Taste the key cylinder and the classification tags."

The scavenger took an immediate step back, raising both hands in unconditional surrender. He possessed excellent, life-saving instincts; he recognized instantly that this was no routine haggle over junk. This was an audit by apex predators. The ambient noise of the surrounding alley withered away as nearby shoppers gave the triad a wide, terrified berth.

Kestrel moved first. She did not examine the Counter-Ratchet Anchor Hearts like a clockmaker evaluating gears; she evaluated them like a monster deciding whether an object belonged in the chain between predator and prey.

She dropped into a low crouch, her golden eyes narrowing. Her ears twitched, sifting the ambient noise of the market before tuning it out completely. She sniffed the heavy iron casing, smelling the old oil and cold forge-scale. She watched with terrifying focus as the nervous vendor turned one of the cores under the lantern light.

Slowly, deliberately, she extended one foreclaw and tapped the iron housing with the tip of her razor-sharp talon.

A single, metallic click echoed from the mechanism.

Kestrel froze.

She struck it again, applying significantly greater force. She probed for mechanical play, for structural slop, testing whether the internal springs yielded lazily or fought back with honest, engineered tension.

Her reaction flooded Banki’s mind through the Tri-Weave—a wave of raw, unedited instinct. Deep interest. Cold approval. A total absence of contempt. She let out a low, vibrating rumble from her chest—the specific sound she reserved for things that were fundamentally *real*.

The impressions surged into Banki's mind, unburdened by mathematical abstraction. *Solid core. Not a toy. Demands a proper frame. Exceptional for loads that must hold when the wall breaks. Fatal in foolish hands.*

She picked up the second core, feeling its mass against the first. "Built by craftsmen who anticipated violence, not display," she said aloud, dropping the heavy iron back onto the mat with a dull thud. "Not generic fittings. That is the virtue. The danger is they will demand the correct frame around them. These are not counter repairs; these are seed-parts. Strong enough to matter, specific enough that an incompetent builder will ruin them. Buy them if they go directly to clever hands. Useless in a general storage drawer."

Banki logged the assessment into his operational ledger. He shifted his focus to a tray of ancient intake priority tags—thick, battered rectangles of stamped brass and chipped enamel, scarred by decades of institutional cruelty.

Null-Fang did not smell the metal; he tasted the hierarchy buried in the enamel. The psychic feedback vibrated with dark, predatory intellect.

*These were not decorative tokens,* the blade whispered into Banki's nervous system. *They belonged to a system that cared deeply about what moved first, what waited in the dark, and what was too dangerous to ever grant a choice. They preserve taxonomy. And taxonomy becomes doctrine when seized by a house intelligent enough not to copy blindly.*

Null-Fang pushed deeper into the artifact's memory, scraping at the psychic grease clinging to the brass. *Tied to their original framework, they lay bare the exact values of the minds that constructed it. Values are frequently more lethal than physical inventory. Not urgent, but vital to own before someone patient and less imaginative recognizes their worth.*

A quiet-value acquisition. Perfect for reverse-engineering the operational logic of defunct containment facilities.

Finally, Banki directed Null-Fang toward the Sealed Cage-Key Cylinder—a heavy, featureless plug of dark, spell-deadened iron, locked tight and silent.

The analysis took longer. The cylinder was quiet, which in the Salt Roads meant it was either worthless scrap or disciplined enough to conceal its lethality until triggered.

*This is not merely a key-part,* Null-Fang concluded, his tone sharpening into cold fascination. *It demands relational context. Sequence. Authority. Alone, it is a dormant template or a trap. Matched correctly, it becomes a sovereign key. Purchase it before another ruin-prowler realizes what it locks.*

The vendor remained frozen, hands raised, watching the noble, the beast, and the sentient blade dissect his inventory without casting a single visible spell.

"My lord?" the man squeaked, unable to bear the suffocating quiet.

"The Anchor Hearts. The Tags. The Cylinder," Banki commanded, his voice echoing with absolute authority. "Crate them. Deliver them to the Sablehook receiving desk."

He did not ask the price. The vendor dared not offer one until the items were safely packed, terrified of offending forces that had just stripped the secrets from his stock. Banki tossed a heavy pouch of gold onto the counter, knowing the massive overpayment bought absolute discretion and immediate compliance.

## SECTION 5: THE PHILOSOPHY OF RESTRAINT

The containment architecture of Sablehook required immediate, massive escalation. The Direction Two audit had exposed the fatal flaw in relying on generalized holding cells for rapidly specializing, mutating biology. Banki required infrastructure engineered around leverage, resistance, and the cold psychology of confinement.

He tracked down the senior tinker gnome in the deep artisan arcades of the lower market, amidst a labyrinth of half-assembled brass armatures, venting steam pipes, and glowing parchment schematics.

"Containment tables," Banki said, cutting ruthlessly through the gnome’s manic sales pitch regarding a clockwork scrying bird. "I require three distinct tiers of handling benches. Not decorative. Not standard issue."

The gnome blinked, dropping the clockwork bird instantly as his mind pivoted to the structural challenge. "Tiers, my lord? Define the pressure parameters!"

Banki laid out the architecture of absolute control. "Tier One: Reinforced practical handling bench. Routine restraint, fitting, and physical examination. Modular, but not excessive. Tier Two: Heavy anchor points. Integrated dosing and life-support architecture. Multi-angle locks, heavy iron restraint logic, problem-solving capacity built directly into the frame."

The gnome was sketching feverishly, graphite snapping against heavy paper as his fingers struggled to match Banki's words. "And Tier Three?"

"Advanced intervention," Banki said softly, his voice dropping into a register of cold, terrifying promise. "Leverage-aware restraint geometry. High-adaptation hostile-containment latches. Engineered specifically for entities that learn rapidly because they desperately want to breach the room. It assumes spatial testing, fake compliance, and continuous intelligence pressure from the subject."

Null-Fang thrummed with vicious delight. *Tier three sounds like a proper insult. The version that understands arguments become philosophy if left unpinned. Yes. Build the philosophy-pin.*

The gnome practically vibrated with excitement, his eyes wide behind heavy magnifying goggles. "A philosophy-pin! Leverage-aware! My lord, this is... structural genius! But the cost..." The tinker hesitated, professional pride warring with sheer terror. "If all three product families run three tiers each... that demands separate containment assumptions, separate handler doctrine, variance tracking..."

"Scale it," Banki commanded, crushing the hesitation beneath the weight of his ambition. "Three hundred Thinking Lane Upgrade Kits. Full production run. No more one-line paperwork masking two-line biology."

The gnome swallowed hard, his throat dry. The sheer scale of the order represented staggering wealth, but also a catastrophic, lethal liability if his engineering failed to hold the horrors Sablehook actively cultivated.

"It will be built, Lord Banki," the gnome whispered, bowing until his nose nearly touched the oil-stained worktable.

Banki turned away, already computing the installation schedule and the necessary shifts to the breeding floor. The Salt Roads provided the raw material and the engineering; Sablehook would provide the bloody crucible.

## SECTION 6: THE WIDOW'S FAVOR

The receiving vault of Sablehook Hall felt radically different at half-past nine than it had in the afternoon. The children had been moved deep into the interior, their trauma managed by the companions. The air in the stone chamber felt hollow, cold, and heavy with impending consequence.

Aveline stood in the exact center of the room. In her hands, she held a bulky, heavily wrapped parcel. The outer layers consisted of thick, lead-lined canvas, bound tightly with heavy silver wire etched with microscopic containment runes.

Banki stood motionless near the threshold, his gaze fixed not on the lead-weave, but on the precise, unnatural geometry beneath it. The ambient temperature in the room plummeted rapidly—a localized thermal drain indicating a high-density magical artifact violently suppressing its own aura to evade detection.

"State its true name," Banki commanded, his voice cutting the cold air like a scalpel.

Aveline stiffened, her surface thoughts flashing with rapid, desperate calculation.

Before she could craft a politically safe answer, Banki struck again, tightening the trap. "And explain what you meant by 'badly' regarding the appraiser. And detail the wrappings before anyone touches the wire."

The three-part command locked her into a structure she could not evade: Name. Consequence. Containment.

Aveline’s mind tightened under the pressure. Banki felt the sharp friction of her thoughts through his expanded awareness. *Good. Name first. He understands the wrappings are part of the truth, not just an obstacle. Speak the old name, not the house euphemism. Do not conceal the appraiser now.*

Kestrel’s cold approval rippled through the link. *Sharp cuts.*

Null-Fang hummed low, a vibration felt in Banki's marrow. *Names first. A civilized cruelty.*

Aveline took a slow, deep breath, steadying her voice. She attempted no melodrama; she offered no salesmanship to inflate the object's value. She delivered the cold, raw fact.

"It was recorded as the Widow's Favor," she said.

The stone walls absorbed the title. It possessed none of the theatrical threat of weapons named for slaughter; it sounded intimate, personal, and profoundly lethal.

"That may not have been its original name," Aveline added, realizing that names alone were merely blood-bait for a mind like Banki's. "It is the title it carried in the archives that reached my hand. Earlier notes refer to it only as *the Favor*, or *her Favor*—which I do not trust."

"A sound instinct," Banki murmured. Artifacts that survived centuries rarely did so by advertising their true nature. They survived by presenting a localized, manageable threat, concealing their apocalyptic architecture beneath a modest veneer.

"The appraiser," Banki prompted, keeping the pressure unyielding.

Aveline’s grip on the lead-canvas tightened until her knuckles turned white. "He attempted a routine sympathetic resonance scan. Merely to determine the age of the binding spells. He did not breach the physical seals."

"And the result?"

"The resonance inverted," she said, her voice dropping flat, stripping the horror of emotion to present it as pure data. "The artifact did not answer the scan. It pulled the spell inward. It read *him*. By the time I severed the feedback loop, he was weeping dark blood from his tear ducts and trying to shatter his own fingers with an iron mallet, screaming that he needed to 'break the sequence before she finished the stitch.'"

The horrifying implications assembled themselves behind Banki's eyes in a fraction of a second. Not a passive ward. Not a simple trap. A living, predatory malice—a curse that fed through the very scrying channel the appraiser had opened. The artifact had used the man's diagnostic magic as an open door, crawling back along the sympathetic link like a parasite threading through living flesh, burrowing into his neural pathways and seizing his motor control until his own hands became weapons turned against his body. It did not defend itself; it *hunted* anyone foolish enough to peer inside.

"The wrappings," Banki demanded.

"Lead-weave canvas," Aveline said instantly. "Saturated with a null-magic deadening fluid. Silver wire carrying a closed-loop stasis rune.
