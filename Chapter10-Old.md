"Talonscript."

The tail-tip flicked once.

"Mothledge."

The shoulders lowered by a degree so small only obsession would have noticed it, and obsession was one of Banki's core literacies.

"Family-only," he said. "Public moniker?"

The reply came quicker.

Kestrel.

A dangerous joke, perhaps, or a preference for a simpler knife in ordinary company. Either way he accepted it. Public and private truths were the two lungs by which institutions breathed.

He drew out the crest-token then, the one cut for family seals rather than staff assignments. Not large. Grand symbols were for those who thought affection required witnesses. This token was dark metal worked with the house sign in a form subtle enough that one had to look twice and honest enough that the second look hurt more than the first. He touched it lightly to the scar-lattice along Kestrel's neck.

The response was immediate.

Something in the room changed pressure.

Recognition.

"Good," he said.

Null-Fang, at last permitted to do more than stand, sent one curious pulse of thought across the room.

*She is sharp.*

Banki's eyes did not leave Kestrel's. "She."

The correction hit the sceptre like a finger flicked against a cup rim. Slight. Clear. Nonnegotiable.

*She is family,* Null-Fang amended after a breath.

Better.

The harmonic overlay came next. Not because the house needed more wonders that night. Because some doors, once opened, demand to be stepped through before courage notices the cost.

Banki crossed the pen and laid one hand against the side of Kestrel's neck where scar-lattice cooled under hide. She did not flinch. Through her, the room became vertical first: beam, rail, wall stress, air path, kill angle. Through him, she tasted the administrative ugliness of ownership and nearly approved.

Tier One. Active.

Tier Two. Unlocked, dangerous, held in place by choice.

Tier Three. Possible later if the world continued deserving them.

When the bond eased back, both of them stood without embarrassment and without pretending the act had been ordinary.

He smiled then, a private expression unsuited to council halls.

"Welcome home," he said.

Kestrel blinked once, slow and deliberate.

The nearest translation his mind could make of what crossed back from her was this:

Already was.

The first true Direction Four inspection happened three nights after Kestrel's verdict.

It began with a bell that did not ring.

The alert lived in the floor instead: three short pulses through the stone beneath Banki's boots, a pause, then one longer pulse that told every person cleared for deep access that the creature-side spine was active. The signal did not travel into the upper compound. It did not reach the kitchen, the dormitories, the public offices, or any room where a frightened clerk might mistake controlled danger for disaster. Direction Four did not announce itself to people who could not do anything useful with the knowledge.

Banki descended with Sable on his left—his peer in command, moving with her own unassailable autonomy—Kestrel above and ahead in the ceiling-dark, Darric and Hesta Bramblewake waiting at the first gate, and Kessa standing behind a reinforced observation slit with her hands folded as if she were attending a chapel service conducted by jaws.

The Direction Labs were awake around them, but not every file deserved the room's attention.

Direction One handled routes. Direction Two held relics, residue, and dead things not finished being problems. Direction Three made doctrine wearable. Direction Four smelled alive.

Tonight, Book One's active centre narrowed to the creatures that changed people when they entered a room.

Choirless Siren Moths clung to copper hoops above the recovery corridor. One hummed note from a Choir transcript made their pale wings open and eat the sound, leaving a silence soft as wool and just as smothering. Protection, yes. Also erasure if used without mercy. Banki thought Maren would hate them and perhaps understand them first.

Mercy Thorn Graziers lowered warm muzzles over injured handlers and carried shock out through red buds along their crowns. Kessa corrected him when he called it feeding. "It carries pain," she said. "There is a difference."

Mirror-Maw Canal Eels moved where the water remembered them wrong, a mouth appearing in reflection half a breath before the body surfaced. "No free canal release," Sable said, and Kessa answered, "Net gates at every Crown waterline."

The Stonemaw work remained mostly floor, tremor, brace, and argument. No living Gate-King breathed in Sablehook yet, but the floor learned impact, the braces learned weight, and the gates learned the difference between holding against collapse and holding a person in. The old problem had already arrived: when does protection become a locked door?

The supporting lines stayed in Mara's records: useful, dangerous, and background until the story earned them.

Kestrel's file stayed sealed. Banki wrote the restraint in his own hand.

Kestrel is not stock.

The war-line came last because it had to. Teeth were easy. Purposes were difficult.

Musk. Blood-warm hay. Salt. A trace of ozone. Stone dust. Wet hide. The particular sourness that came from creatures large enough to make fear seem sensible.

"Before we go farther," Hesta said, "you hear the rule again."

"I have heard the rule."

"You hear it again because you remember things you want and improve things you dislike."

Sable's mouth made the smallest possible movement.

Banki inclined his head. "Proceed."

Hesta held up one blunt finger. "No staff in a lane without two exits. No open gate without three watchers. No watcher without a kill angle. No kill angle without a rescue angle. No feeding during temper-shift. No breeding permission until Darric signs body-stability and I sign handler-stability. No command test if the creature has not eaten, slept, and accepted a quiet hour."

"And if I overrule?"

"Then I resign in writing, loudly, with a copy to Mara."

"A devastating weapon."

"The most dangerous kind. Paperwork with witnesses."

Kessa's eyes did not leave the pen beyond the slit. "She is right."

The pen held three prototypes.

They had no official name yet. Hesta's handlers called them RhinoWhips only when they were far enough away to be foolish. Dire Rhino mass carried displacer wrongness through shoulder, horn, and tentacle; the pen survived because Direction Four had been built by pessimists.

Darric passed Banki the slate.

CHASSIS STABLE.

DISPLACER INTEGRATION REAL.

TENTACLE RESPONSE NONCOSMETIC.

STRESS SHIMMER UNSTABLE.

CLOSE-KILL PREDATORY OVERRIDE PRESENT.

COMMAND RECEPTIVITY UNRANKED.

DO NOT FIELD.

Banki read the last line twice.

The largest prototype turned its head and looked at him.

The afterimage lagged behind the motion like an apology told by someone who did not mean it. For one breath the creature's true skull, displaced skull, horn-shadow, and tentacle-shadow all occupied different places. A handler behind the secondary gate swore softly and was not mocked for it.

Banki extended his Mind-Sense.

The mind he touched was not a soldier's mind. Not yet. It was appetite organised around mass, territorial patience stitched to trained response, displacer instinct constantly testing the location of every warm thing in reach. The graft had worked in the crude sense. The creature could misplace itself. It could strike from the wrong angle. It understood pressure, gate, food, command, pain, and rank.

It did not yet understand law.

Kestrel dropped from the ceiling beam without a sound.

All three prototypes reacted.

Not panic. Not rage. Recognition sharpened into threat. The largest one's horn dipped. Its tentacles spread with the beautiful, terrible precision of a killing engine discovering that the room had acquired a target worth the room.

Kestrel did not move.

She only breathed.

Banki saw the moment the prototypes noticed what most people missed until it was too late: Kestrel was not prey-shaped. She was not handler-shaped. She was an end-point. A thing that moved when motion was useful and stopped when stopping made the world come to her. The largest prototype held her gaze for three heartbeats, four, five.

Then it lowered its head one inch.

Not submission.

Rank acknowledgment.

Darric exhaled through his nose. "Well. That answers one question and makes six worse."

"It recognises apex hierarchy," Banki said.

"It recognises Kestrel," Hesta corrected. "Do not turn one good sign into a doctrine by breakfast."

"Noted."

Sable looked through the observation slit at the three enormous bodies, then at Banki. "You want them."

"Yes."

"For caravans?"

"Eventually."

"For streets?"

"If the city becomes a battlefield."

"For fear?"

Banki did not answer quickly enough.

His silence answered.

Sable's voice lowered. "No spectacle releases. No proving-point releases. No using them to make respectable people soil themselves because it amuses you."

"Respectable people so often need encouragement."

"Banki."

The name was quiet. It cut harder because it was not a rebuke performed for witnesses. It was private even with five people hearing it, a dialogue between equals.

He looked at the prototypes again. Teeth for Sablehook. Teeth for roads, kitchens, dormitories, children, workers, Blackwater citizens who had put their names into his ledgers and believed, perhaps foolishly, that the Crown meant shelter.

Not trophies. Not theatre.

Guardians, if he could force the impossible shape of that word onto beasts made from siege mass and misdirection.

"Crown Shield rules," he said, acknowledging her authority in the matter. "If they ever leave Direction Four, they protect routes, people, and wards. They interpose before they kill. They do not pursue into homes. They do not hunt civilians. They do not become a lesson I teach because my temper wants a blackboard."

Hesta stared at him.

"Write that down," she said to Mara, who was not present.

"I will tell her."

"No need," Sable said. "She will know by lunch."

The largest prototype breathed steam against the gate. Its afterimage breathed half a second late.

Banki stepped closer to the observation line, not inside the pen, not past the safety mark Hesta had painted onto the floor in red salt.

"Not ready," he said.

Darric nodded. "No."

"But possible."

"Yes."

Direction Four had teeth.

They were not ready for a mouth.

## The Heroes' Trail

Caelen found the first dead drop on Tuesday morning.

Caelen worked the Dockside in a slow grid, not because he lacked imagination but because mud, loose stone, and old habits told truth more reliably than cleverness. Banki saw only pieces of it through borrowed eyes, and the pieces were enough to irritate him.

The dead drop was a sealed envelope wedged behind a loose stone in the canal wall near the Tidewash Steps. The stone had been selected for its accessibility—easy to spot if you were looking, invisible if you weren't—and the envelope bore no markings, no seal, no identifying characteristics that could connect it to anyone.

The information was accurate. The handwriting was nobody's: legible, steady, and so bare of personality it made Caelen narrow his eyes. Someone had paid for absence, and absence was never cheap.

Caelen brought the envelope to Aldric within the hour.

From there, Banki had only fragments: a sweeper's glimpse of five heads bent over paper, a dockhand's memory of Aldric's hand flattening the map, a child's report that the elf had found wax under a plank and smiled at the wrongness of it.

Aldric did not rush, and Banki disliked him more for it.

"Caelen: confirm the safe house locations. Physical reconnaissance only—no contact, no engagement, no magical detection that might alert them. I want entry points, exit points, guard patterns, and ward signatures."

"Seraphine: begin preparing restoration protocols for the affected citizens. If the information is accurate, there are twelve people in this district who are living with altered personalities, and we are going to fix them."

"Lyris: analyse the resonance grid map. I need to know how the hymn-stones connect, which one is the primary node, and what happens to the network if we destroy the primary first."

"Maren: find the affected families. Quietly. Personally. Build trust. When we move against the Choir, those families need to know that someone is coming for their people and that the someone is us."

Maren nodded and folded the names carefully before speaking to the families. By evening she had six stories and one button from a missing man's coat. Seraphine touched the button once and went pale before she said, 'The compulsion did not erase love. It walked around it.'

Maren kept the button in her palm long after she should have put it down. Fabric remembered pressure; people did too. She wondered what kind of man could return a family member and still make the room colder by having helped.

Caelen noticed and said nothing. Maren liked that about him: he could identify a wound without putting his fingers in it. He only set a scrap of clean cloth beside her elbow, then returned to the map.

Seraphine prayed over the names before she sorted them. The Dawnfather's light warmed the paper and left the ink unchanged. That unsettled her more than darkness would have.

"We go tomorrow night," Aldric said. "The primary node first. We destroy the hymn-stone, break the resonance grid, and then sweep the safe houses before the Choir can relocate."

The party prepared. Weapons were sharpened. Spells were prepared. Prayers were offered. In the small hours, while fog rolled in from the harbour, five Good people knelt in a circle and asked the Dawnfather for the strength to do what needed to be done.

Aldric checked every route twice and then checked the routes other people had rejected. Mercy, in his experience, failed most often at the exits.

Six blocks away, Banki felt the divine surge: a warm gold pressure through the fog, conviction given weather.

The heroes would hit the Choir tomorrow night. Something would go wrong. Something always did.

On his worktable, the contingency map waited with one corner pinned under a knife.

## The Choir Burns

The captured lamp observer looked less like a conspirator than a clerk who had wandered too far from his own courage.

They found his station half a mile beyond Crooked Lantern Pool where a stand of dead cedar leaned over a shale rise and gave narrow view of the road below. He had built himself a little nest of canvas, field glass, signal mirror, dry rations, and note slips sealed in wax against damp. A patient arrangement. Someone expected a long watch and low comfort. Someone also expected not to be found.

Kestrel found him anyway, from above and downwind.

She came back through the brush with one foreclaw hooked in the man's collar and a harness knife laid so flat along his jaw it looked almost affectionate. Vesper followed with the mirror kit, face expressionless in the way people wear expressionlessness when they are pleased to have proven a point. The observer's boots dragged furrows. His thoughts screamed so loudly Banki barely needed to pry.

Mirrorhouse Sings. Count wagons. Count jars. Note chains. Note beast lines. Sablehook growing teeth. Do not be taken alive.

“Bad instruction,” Banki said.

The man tried to spit. Fear dries the mouth; nothing came of it.

They bound him under the lee of the shale and went to work.

At midnight, Banki watched the heroes through borrowed eyes: a rooftop cat, a wall-rat, two sleepless dockhands, and the ambient minds of a city that had no idea it had become surveillance.

He was far enough away to risk nothing and close enough to learn what Good did when time ran out.

Aldric's sword sang when it struck the first Choir guard's shield.

The shield broke. The guard saw the opening in his own life and understood it too late.

Aldric turned the strike at the last instant. Flat, not edge. Mercy with enough force behind it to end the argument. The man dropped into the warehouse water and did not rise.

Behind him, Seraphine filled the room with light.

The Choir's illusions went thin as wet paper. Crates became cages. Dusty tarps became operating screens. Three clerks blinked in the glare with borrowed smiles collapsing on their faces. Caelen's arrows found hands, bows, whistles. Lyris's ward caught the first hymn and drank it before it could enter anyone's skull.

Maren played.

Not loudly. Loud would have been fear. She played a line steady enough to stand on, and the altered clerks heard it. One of them looked up with his own eyes for the first time in weeks.

"Help me," he said.

Maren did not stop playing. The song saved one person by refusing to turn away from the room.

"Seraphine," she called. "Three civilians. Still in there."

Seraphine moved to them with both hands bright. One by one, names returned. A clerk sobbed into her sleeve. Another vomited black hymn-water onto the floor and apologised for the mess. The third kept asking whether his wife knew where he was.

The upper level fell in four minutes.

Aldric found one man still conscious near the trapdoor to the lower chambers.

The operative had crawled behind a prayer-engine and clamped both hands over his ears as if refusing to hear his own defeat might delay it. He was young enough to have soft fear and old enough to have chosen the work. Ink marked his fingers. Choir ink. Routing ink. The ink of a man who knew where people were sent after their names stopped mattering.

Caelen dragged him out.

"Where are the altered kept?" Aldric asked.

The man spat blood and said nothing.

Seraphine stepped forward with a binding prayer ready, but Aldric raised one hand. Not harshly. Worse: calmly.

"We have minutes," he said.

He set the flat of his consecrated blade against the man's cheek.

Light entered the room.

Not flame. Not heat as a kitchen knew heat. Divine pressure. Moral certainty made physical enough to blister skin where it found corruption and drive pain through every lie the body tried to shelter. The operative made a sound that Maren would remember later when she tried to sing herself calm and could not.

"Where?" Aldric asked again.

"Below the cistern," the man gasped. "Left grate. Mercy. Please."

Aldric did not move the blade.

"How many?"

"Twelve. Maybe more. I don't know."

"Who bought the last shipment?"

The man sobbed.

Seraphine's face had gone white. "Aldric."

"Twenty minds are at risk," Aldric said, eyes never leaving the man on the floor. "If his pain buys them back, then his pain is cheap."

Seraphine's light trembled once before she steadied it. The man on the floor was guilty. That did not make the sound holy.

The names came after that. Not all. Enough.

When Aldric finally lifted the sword, the imprint of the Dawnfather's light remained on the man's cheek like a second confession. Maren looked at the rescued map in her hand and understood two things at once: Aldric had saved lives, and he had done it by making a helpless man scream.

Banki, watching through borrowed senses, went very still.

So, he thought. There you are.

The descent to the hymn-stone chamber was through a trapdoor that Caelen found in the north corner—the same trapdoor that Banki's intelligence package had described, positioned exactly where the anonymous document had indicated it would be.

Aldric went first because he always went first. The sword's light made the stairwell honest and ugly.

At the bottom: the hymn-stone chamber.

Three crystals. Sitting on a raised platform. Humming.

The hum was audible at this distance—a low, continuous vibration that Aldric could feel in his chest and that Lyris's Globe absorbed and nullified before it could reach the party's minds. The crystals were beautiful. Aldric noted this the way he noted beauty in all weapons: as a feature of their design rather than a redeeming quality of their purpose.

"Lyris," he said. "Analysis."

"Three stones," she said. "Centre leads. Break that one and the song dies."

"Defences?"

"We already passed them."

Aldric considered this. He looked at the crystals. He looked at Seraphine. He looked at his sword.

"Seraphine," he said. "Consecrate."

The cleric stepped forward. She placed her hands on the platform. She prayed—not the quick, functional prayer of a combat restoration, but the deep, sustained, resonant prayer of a consecration ritual: the invocation of divine authority over a physical space, the declaration that this ground—this stone, this air, this water, this darkness—was no longer the property of the forces that had claimed it but was, from this moment forward, sanctified.

The consecration hit the hymn-stones like sunlight hitting ice.

The crystals screamed.

The primary stone cracked.

Silence.

Aldric looked at the broken crystals, then at the people blinking their way back into themselves.

"Secure the prisoners," he said. "Document everything. Seraphine, restoration sweeps. Every last one."

They moved.

Later, in the Canal Row house, Maren tried to tune her lute and could not make the third string hold.

The burned man's sound kept hiding under the note.

She loosened the peg until the string went flat, then closed the case without writing the rescue into a song.

## The Dry Kiln

The Dry Kiln operated on the principle that the best hiding place for a criminal workshop was inside a legitimate one.

The building was a real ceramics kiln: salt-glazed stoneware above, enchanted containers below, taxes paid on the honest work and silence paid on the other.

Torren Ashpell ran both. Middle-aged, stooped from cellar ceilings, permanently squinting from kiln fire, he was tired, underpaid, and desperate for someone to recognise the value of his craft without treating him like a replaceable hand.

Banki entered through the legitimate entrance. In human form. In daylight. With his Mind-Sense running as always, reading Torren's surface impressions as he walked through the ceramics showroom and complimented the glaze work on a display piece that was, genuinely, excellent.

"You made this?" Banki asked, turning a salt-glazed serving bowl in his hands with the attention of a man who appreciated craftsmanship.

Torren blinked. Customers did not, as a rule, ask about the maker. Customers asked about the price. The fact that this customer had asked about the maker registered in Torren's surface thoughts as a novelty—a small, unexpected warmth in a daily routine that was otherwise composed entirely of transaction and tedium.

"I did," Torren said. "Twenty-seven hours in the salt kiln. Four coats. You can see the crystal formation in the glaze if you hold it to the light."

Banki held it to the light.

"Preservation effect," Banki said quietly, tracing the crystal pattern with one finger. "Approximately two weeks of organic stasis at room temperature. Possibly three if the seal is tight."

Torren's thoughts froze. The warmth vanished. In its place: fear. Sharp, immediate, the terror of a man whose secret has just been spoken aloud by a stranger in his own shop.

"I'm not here to threaten you," Banki said, before the fear could calcify into panic or flight. "I'm here to hire you."

He set the bowl down. Reached into his coat. Produced a pouch containing a hundred platinum pieces—more money than Torren's criminal employers had paid him in the last three years combined.

"This is a retainer," Banki said, binding the man with the inevitable logic of the Asmodean Pact. "It buys your exclusive services, your discretion, and your agreement to operate under my protection rather than the network's. You keep the kiln, the cellar workshop, your tools, your techniques, and your pride. Fewer people try to kill you. More people pay you what your work is worth."

Torren stared at the platinum. His thoughts went through the rapid, desperate calculation of a man who has been offered a lifeboat and is trying to determine whether the lifeboat is also a trap:

*...that's real platinum... a hundred pieces... I've never seen a hundred pieces in one place... but if I take it and the network finds out...*

Torren's fear receded by inches. Hope took its place, cautious and kiln-warm.
