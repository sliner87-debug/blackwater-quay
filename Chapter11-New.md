"Talonscript."

The tail-tip flicked once.

"Mothledge."

The shoulders lowered by a degree so small only obsession would register it. Obsession anchored the core literacies of Sablehook. 

"Family-only," Banki said. "Public moniker?"

The reply came quicker, slicing through the chill air of the training pen.

*Kestrel.*

A dangerous joke, perhaps, or a preference for a simpler knife in ordinary company. Either way, he accepted it. Public and private truths were the two lungs by which institutions breathed. 

He drew out the crest-token then, the one cut for family seals rather than staff assignments. It was a modest piece of dark metal, worked with the house sign in a form subtle enough that one had to look twice, and honest enough that the second look hurt more than the first. Grand symbols belonged to those who believed affection required witnesses. He touched it lightly to the scar-lattice along Kestrel's neck.

The response was immediate. The room's air pressure shifted. Recognition settled in the dust.

"Good," he said.

Null-Fang, permitted at last to do more than stand rigid by the doorway, sent one curious pulse of thought across the room.

*She is sharp.*

Banki locked his gaze with Kestrel's. "She."

The correction hit the sceptre like a finger flicked against a cup rim. Slight. Clear. Nonnegotiable.

*She is family,* Null-Fang amended after a breath.

Better.

The harmonic overlay came next. The house required no more wonders that night, but some doors, once opened, demand to be stepped through before courage tallies the cost. Banki crossed the pen and laid one hand against the side of Kestrel's neck where the scar-lattice cooled under hide. She did not flinch. Through her, the room became vertical first: beam, rail, wall stress, air path, kill angle. Through him, she tasted the administrative ugliness of ownership and nearly approved.

The primary bond anchored into blood and marrow. The deeper tether unspooled, dangerous and held in place entirely by choice. A final depth remained, waiting for a day when the world proved it deserved such a union.

When the connection eased back, both of them stood without embarrassment and without pretending the act had been ordinary.

He smiled then, a private expression unsuited to council halls.

"Welcome home," he said.

Kestrel blinked once, slow and deliberate.

The nearest translation his mind could make of the pulse crossing back from her was this:

*Already was.*

***

The first true inspection of the Fourth Vault happened three nights after Kestrel's verdict. 

It began with a bell possessing no clapper. The alert lived in the floor instead: three short pulses through the stone beneath Banki's boots, a pause, then one longer thrum communicating to every cleared soul that the creature-side spine had awakened. The signal did not travel into the upper compound. It did not reach the kitchen, the dormitories, the public offices, or any room where a frightened clerk might mistake controlled danger for disaster. The Fourth Vault did not announce itself to people unable to wield the knowledge.

A senior night handler fell in beside his path with a relay-shadow as he moved toward Direction Four. The report came in quick, clean pieces. A disturbance during the night-cycle observation. No escape alarm. No full breach. No handler casualty. No structural collapse. 

"Dire RhinoWhip Prototype One," the handler said, his voice tight. "Abnormal route-testing. Repeated attention to one specific containment transition. Altered tentacle use. Apparent anticipatory movement before a handler correction."

The handler swallowed hard. "It executed one successful exploitation of room timing that should not yet have been within its learned pattern."

Kestrel’s eyes narrowed beside Banki in the dim corridor.

"So it didn't just test," Banki murmured. "It solved something."

"Yes, my lord. The smart-room element—a timing-based transition piece, one of the newer control features. Not fully defeated, but partially predicted. The floor handlers felt the room had been read rather than merely pressured."

That was exactly the kind of sentence that woke a sovereign in the deep hours. Prototype One remained physically contained, showing no signs of full frenzy. It was not battering blindly. It was already thinking in weather, ambush, yield, and terrain. 

Banki descended with Sable on his left—moving with her own unassailable autonomy—Kestrel above and ahead in the ceiling-dark, Darric and Hesta Bramblewake waiting at the first iron portcullis, and Kessa standing behind a reinforced observation slit with her hands folded as if attending a chapel service conducted by jaws.

The beast-wards stirred around them, but only specific files commanded the room's focus. The First Vault handled routes. The Second held relics, residue, and dead things refusing to finish being problems. The Third wove doctrine into wearable armor. The Fourth smelled of hot breath and copper.

Tonight, the vault's active center narrowed to the creatures capable of altering a room the moment they entered it. 

Choirless Siren Moths clung to copper hoops above the recovery corridor. One hummed note from a captive hymnist made their pale wings open and devour the sound, leaving a silence soft as wool and just as smothering. Protection, yes. Also erasure, if deployed without mercy. Banki suspected Maren would despise them and perhaps understand them first.

Mercy Thorn Graziers lowered warm muzzles over injured handlers, drawing shock out through red buds along their crowns. Kessa corrected him when he had once called it feeding. *It pulls the agony away. There is a difference.*

Mirror-Maw Canal Eels moved where the water reflected them wrong, a mouth appearing in the glass-still surface half a breath before the scaled body surfaced. *No free canal release,* Sable had decreed. Kessa nodded in agreement. *Net gates at every Crown waterline.*

The Stonemaw work remained mostly floor, tremor, brace, and argument. No living Gate-King breathed in Sablehook yet, but the floor learned impact, the braces learned weight, and the iron gates learned the difference between holding against collapse and holding a person in. 

Kestrel's file stayed sealed. Banki wrote the restraint in his own hand: *Kestrel is not livestock.*

The war-line came last because it had to. Teeth were simple to forge. Purposes were difficult to instil. Musk. Blood-warm hay. Salt. A trace of ozone. Stone dust. Wet hide. The particular sourness rolling off creatures large enough to make fear the only sensible response.

"Before we go farther," Hesta said, her voice cutting through the heavy musk, "we review the rules."

"I know the rules."

"We review them because memory shapes itself around desire, and convenience erodes discipline."

Sable's mouth tightened. Banki inclined his head. "Proceed."

Hesta held up one blunt finger. "No staff in a lane without two exits. No open gate without three wardens. No warden without a kill angle. No kill angle without a rescue route. No feeding during temper-shifts. No breeding permission until Darric signs body-stability and I sign handler-stability. No command test if the creature has not eaten, slept, and accepted a quiet hour."

"And if I overrule?"

"Then I resign in writing, loudly, with a copy to Mara."

"A devastating weapon."

"The most dangerous kind. Paperwork with witnesses."

Kessa's gaze remained locked on the pen beyond the slit. "She is right."

The pen held three prototypes. They had no official name yet. Hesta's handlers called them RhinoWhips only when they were far enough away to be foolish. Dire Rhino mass carried an unsettling wrongness through shoulder, horn, and tentacle; the pen survived because pessimists had built the Fourth Vault.

Darric passed Banki the slate.

*Bones hold the new weight.*
*The shadow-slip takes root.*
*Lash-vines move with intent.*
*The void-blur falters under pressure.*
*Hunger eclipses command at close range.*
*Loyalty untested.*
*Keep caged.*

Banki read the last line twice. 

The largest prototype turned its heavy, plated head toward him. The afterimage lagged behind the motion like a poorly rehearsed lie. For one breath the creature's true skull, its shadow-displaced skull, the horn-ghost, and the tentacle-blur all occupied different spaces. A handler behind the secondary gate swore softly and received no mockery for it.

Banki cast his thoughts outward, brushing the edges of the beast's mind. The Tri-Weave followed—Arcane, Divine, and Psionic threads braided into one lucid control architecture. Finally, he pushed the Sovereign Thread through it all, not to dominate blindly, but to test the prototypes against the law they were meant to answer.

The room changed. Handlers stopped breathing. The shimmer around the pens sharpened.

Inside the reinforced holds, the prototypes answered. Not verbally. Heavy rhino mass. Wrong-angle predatory drift. Displacement pressure. Muscle memory that didn't entirely belong to any natural beast, and the deep biological truth of things built to survive correction or fail trying. 

The core body-plan correction held. The dire rhino chassis remained dominant and stable. 

Kestrel dropped from the ceiling beam without a sound.

All three prototypes reacted. Recognition sharpened into threat. The largest one's horn dipped. Its tentacles spread with the beautiful, terrible precision of a killing engine discovering the room had acquired a target worth destroying.

Kestrel did not move. She only breathed.

The moment arrived when the prototypes registered what most missed until it was too late: Kestrel lacked the shape of prey. She lacked the shape of a handler. She was an end-point. A creature that moved when motion proved useful and stopped when stopping made the world come to her. The largest prototype held her stare for three heartbeats, four, five.

Then it lowered its head one inch. A clear acknowledgment of rank.

Darric exhaled through his nose. "Well. That answers one question and births six worse ones."

"It recognizes apex hierarchy," Banki said.

"It recognizes Kestrel," Hesta corrected. "Do not turn one good sign into doctrine by breakfast."

"Noted." Banki turned his attention back to the slate. "We lock the breeding lines tonight. The override holds. Four breeding pairs. Each line-true, each distinct in spirit and intellect, all fully corrected."

Darric blinked, struggling to process the sudden leap from observation to full-scale infrastructure. "Four pairs? My lord, the gestation—"

"Three months," Banki dictated, rewriting biological reality with the casual certainty of a crown. "Working first-generation target of two-to-twenty-four young per female. Magical acceleration is authorized. Start drafting the cohort testing plans."

The researchers forgot to write. One of the specialists visibly re-evaluated the size of the universe. 

Sable peered through the observation slit at the three enormous bodies. "You want them."

"Yes."

"For caravans?"

"Eventually."

"For streets?"

"If the city becomes a battlefield."

"For fear?"

Banki let the silence stretch a fraction too long. His hesitation answered for him.

Sable's voice lowered. "No spectacle releases. No proving-point deployments. No using them to make respectable people soil themselves because it amuses you."

"Respectable people so often need encouragement."

"Banki." 

The name arrived quiet and sharp. It cut harder because it lacked the theatricality of a public rebuke. It was private even with five people present, a dialogue between equals.

He studied the prototypes again. Teeth for Sablehook. Teeth for roads, kitchens, dormitories, children, workers, Blackwater citizens who had put their names into his ledgers and believed, perhaps foolishly, that the Crown meant shelter. These would be guardians, if he could force the impossible shape of that word onto beasts made from siege mass and misdirection.

"Crown Shield rules," he said, acknowledging her authority. "If they ever leave the Fourth Vault, they protect routes, people, and wards. They interpose before they kill. They do not pursue into homes. They do not hunt civilians. They do not become a lesson I teach because my temper wants a blackboard."

Hesta stared at him. "Write that down," she told the empty space where Mara usually stood.

"I will inform her."

"No need," Sable said. "She will know by lunch."

The largest prototype breathed steam against the gate. Its afterimage breathed half a second late. Banki stepped closer to the observation line, careful to stay behind the safety mark Hesta had painted onto the floor in red salt.

"Keep them caged," he said.

Darric nodded. "No question."

"But the foundation is there."

"Yes."

The Fourth Vault possessed teeth. They were just waiting for a mouth.

## The Heroes' Trail

Caelen found the first dead drop on Tuesday morning.

The ranger worked the Dockside in a slow grid, trusting mud, loose stone, and old habits to reveal truth more reliably than cleverness. Banki tracked the hunt through the scattered minds of the district—a beggar's idle stare, a stray dog's scent-memory, a gull's wide aerial pass. The fragmented surveillance irritated him, but it served the purpose. 

The dead drop rested in a sealed envelope wedged behind a loose stone in the canal wall near the Tidewash Steps. The location offered perfect accessibility—obvious to a seeker, invisible to a passerby. The envelope bore no wax seal, no identifying crest.

The enclosed intelligence was flawless. The handwriting belonged to a ghost: legible, steady, and entirely devoid of personality. Banki had paid dearly for that perfect absence.

Caelen delivered the envelope to Aldric within the hour.

From there, Banki pieced together the heroes' preparations through a mosaic of borrowed impressions. A street-sweeper catching a glimpse of five heads bent over a table. A dockhand noting Aldric's heavy palm flattening a map. A runaway child watching the elven ranger pry wax from beneath a floorboard and smile at the wrongness of it.

Aldric refused to rush. That methodical patience only made Banki despise him more.

Through the thin psychic tether bridging the district, Banki caught the cadence of Aldric's commands.

"Caelen: confirm the safe house locations. Physical reconnaissance only—no contact, no engagement, no magical detection. I want entry points, exit patterns, and ward signatures."

"Seraphine: begin preparing restoration rites. If this intelligence holds, twelve citizens are living with altered personalities, and we are going to repair them."

"Lyris: analyze the song-stone web. I need to know how the ley-lines connect, which crystal acts as the anchor, and what happens to the network when we shatter the root."

"Maren: find the affected families. Quietly. Build trust. When we strike the Choir, these people need to know someone is coming for their lost kin."

Maren spent the afternoon slipping through the district's shadowed doorways. By evening, quiet devastation radiated from her, bleeding across the psychic tether as she returned with six stories and a single brass button from a missing man's coat.

She offered the button to Seraphine. The cleric brushed the tarnished metal, her mind recoiling. *The compulsion did not erase love,* Seraphine realized, the thought broadcasting loud enough for Banki to catch. *It built a wall around it.*

Maren kept the button resting in her palm long after she should have set it down. Fabric held onto pressure; people did too. Banki tasted her bitter wonder at what kind of entity could return a family member to their hearth and still make the room colder for having helped.

Caelen slid a scrap of clean cloth beside her elbow, identifying her quiet distress without demanding an explanation.

Across the room, Seraphine murmured a prayer over the list of names. The Dawnfather's light flared, warming the paper, yet the ink remained unchanged, a stubborn stain of reality resisting the divine. Seraphine shivered, her unease echoing through the link, unsettled by the golden light's failure to cleanse the ink.

"We go tomorrow night," Aldric announced. "The anchor stone first. We shatter the crystal, break the song-lines, and then sweep the safe houses before the Choir can relocate."

The party settled into preparation. Whetstones rasped against steel. Spells were gathered and bound. In the small hours, while fog rolled in from the harbor and muffled the city's bells, five companions knelt in a circle and asked their god for the strength to survive the morning.

Aldric checked every planned route twice, then checked the alleys other people had rejected. Mercy, the paladin knew, failed most often at the exits.

Six blocks away, Banki absorbed the sudden divine surge: a warm gold pressure pushing through the fog, conviction given the weight of weather.

The heroes would strike the Choir tomorrow night. Banki recognized the structure they were hunting. The Choir was not merely a cult. It was an administrative behemoth hiding beneath the skin of religion. It employed holding dens, smuggling trails, outer carry ledgers. The very bones of the lower trade quarter's tenement warrens supported it. 

Something would inevitably go wrong. Something always did.

On Banki's worktable, the contingency map waited, one corner pinned beneath the blade of a knife. 

## The Choir Burns

The captured lamp observer looked less like a conspirator than a clerk who had wandered too far from his own courage.

Banki found his station half a mile beyond Crooked Lantern Pool, where a stand of dead cedar leaned over a shale rise and gave a narrow view of the road below. The man had built himself a little nest of canvas, a spyglass, a signal mirror, dry rations, and note slips sealed in wax against damp. A patient arrangement. Someone expected a long watch and low comfort. Someone also expected to remain hidden.

Kestrel dropped from the canopy downwind.

She dragged him back through the brush with one foreclaw hooked in his collar, a harness knife laid so flat along his jaw it looked almost affectionate. Vesper followed with the mirror kit, wearing an expression of quiet satisfaction at having proven a point. The observer's boots dragged furrows in the dirt. His panicked thoughts screamed so loudly Banki barely needed to press his awareness forward to catch them.

*Mirrorhouse sings. Count wagons. Count jars. Note chains. Note beast lines. Sablehook is growing teeth. Do not be taken alive.*

“A terrible set of instructions,” Banki said.

The man tried to spit. Fear dried his mouth; nothing came of it.

They bound him under the lee of the shale, leaving Banki free to turn his attention back toward the city. 

At midnight, Banki cast his mind across the district, anchoring his awareness in a rooftop cat, a wall-rat, two sleepless dockhands, and the ambient thoughts of a populace unaware they served as his surveillance network.

He sat far enough away to risk nothing, remaining close enough to study what Good did when time ran out.

In the warehouse, Aldric's sword chimed against the first Choir guard's shield.

The shield shattered. The guard recognized the fatal opening too late.

Aldric turned the strike at the last instant. The flat of the blade, not the edge, delivered a blow with enough force to end the argument. The guard dropped into the canal runoff and did not rise.

Behind the paladin, Seraphine flooded the room with light. 

The Choir's illusions dissolved. The warehouse stripped off its mundane disguise. Crates became cages. Dusty tarps became operating screens. The space revealed itself as a fully functional holding-node. Intake cages, sorting chains, transfer ledgers stacked against the far wall. Banki recognized it instantly. It was a second-tier custody pen — a false-ledger clearing house hidden beneath a common smuggling front. 

Three clerks blinked in the glare, the borrowed, vacant smiles collapsing on their faces. Caelen's arrows pinned hands, bows, and alarm whistles to the wood. Lyris's ward swallowed the first hymn before it could burrow into anyone's skull.

Maren played her lute.

She played a steady, grounding line, and the altered clerks latched onto it. One of them looked up with clear eyes for the first time in weeks.

"Help me," the man rasped.

Maren kept her fingers moving. The song anchored the fractured minds by refusing to turn away from the ugliness of the room.

"Seraphine," Maren called out. "Three civilians. Still in there."

Seraphine rushed to them, both hands radiating warmth. One by one, stolen names returned. A clerk sobbed into his own sleeve. Another vomited black, oily fluid onto the floor and apologized profusely for the mess. The third kept asking if his wife knew where he was.

The upper level fell in four minutes.

Aldric found one operative still conscious near the trapdoor leading to the lower chambers.

The man had crawled behind a massive prayer-altar, clamping both hands over his ears as if refusing to listen to his own defeat might somehow delay it. He was young enough to harbor soft fear, yet old enough to have chosen this brutal work. Dark ink stained his fingers. Choir ink. Routing ink. The ink belonging to a man who knew exactly where people were sent after their names ceased to matter.

Banki leaned forward into the psychic tether. He tasted the man's surface thoughts. *Second Desk... Velis Rowe... Maelis... Harrow Fen...* The mental architecture of a vast administrative conspiracy spilled out in uncoordinated panic. 

Caelen dragged him out into the open.

"Where are the altered kept?" Aldric asked.

The operative spat blood and clamped his jaw shut.

Seraphine stepped forward, a binding prayer rising to her lips, but Aldric raised one hand. He moved with a terrifying calm.

"We have minutes," Aldric said.

He pressed the flat of his consecrated blade against the man's cheek.

Light flooded the room.

It carried no heat a kitchen would recognize. This was divine pressure. Moral certainty made physical, blistering skin wherever it found corruption and driving pure agony through every lie the body tried to shelter. The operative unleashed a ragged, tearing scream.

"Where?" Aldric asked again.

"Below the cistern!" the man gasped, thrashing against Caelen's grip. "Left grate! Mercy! Please!"

Aldric kept the blade pressed to the blistering flesh.

"How many?"

"Twelve. Maybe more. I don't know."

"Who bought the last shipment?"

The man sobbed, a wet, broken sound.

Seraphine's face drained of color. "Aldric."

"Twenty minds hang in the balance," Aldric said, his gaze locked on the weeping operative. "If his pain buys them back, then his pain is a cheap price."

Seraphine's light trembled once before she forced it steady. Her revulsion bled across the tether; the man on the floor was guilty, she reasoned, but that did not make the torture holy.

The names spilled out after that. Not all of them, but enough. 

When Aldric finally lifted the sword, the imprint of the Dawnfather's light remained seared onto the man's cheek—a second, permanent confession. Maren stared at the recovered map. A cold knot of understanding pulled tight in her chest, the stark realization echoing across the link: Aldric had saved lives, and he had done it by making a helpless man scream.

Banki, sitting in the dark miles away, went very still.

*So,* he thought. *There you are.*

Banki appreciated the revelation. Aldric was not a fool constrained by pacifism. Aldric was a weapon that simply chose its own targets. The paladin had just shattered a delicate intelligence asset — a third-hand routing clerk, a man who knew where every body was moved and whose seal opened the next door in the chain — for the immediate gratification of finding twelve missing people. Aldric did not care about Outer Carry doctrine or Dead Stone records. Aldric did not care that this man could have unraveled the entire ledger of Maelis and the Second Desk. Aldric only saw the suffering in front of him and broke the machine to stop it. It was a devastating, short-sighted goodness. It was exactly the kind of goodness that left the root of the problem alive to grow back tomorrow. 

The descent to the song-stone chamber led through a trapdoor Caelen found in the north corner—the exact location Banki's anonymous intelligence package had specified. 

Aldric descended first, his sword's light rendering the stairwell honest and ugly. This was the second throat — the hidden descent. Banki recognized the maintenance scars, the narrow stone, the smuggling passage built for moving bodies between sanctioned hands. 

At the bottom lay the lower chamber.

Three crystals sat upon a raised stone dais, humming.

The vibration carried a low, continuous thrum. Lyris's protective ward absorbed the sound, nullifying the compulsion before it could breach their minds. The crystals possessed a sharp, geometric beauty. Through the tether, Aldric's cold pragmatism echoed; the paladin registered this beauty exactly the way a soldier registers the edge of an enemy's blade—as a feature of design rather than a redeeming quality.

"Lyris," Aldric said. "Analysis."

"Three stones," she replied. "The center leads. Shatter the anchor, and the song dies."

"Defences?"

"We already bypassed them."

Aldric studied the crystals. He glanced at Seraphine, then down at his sword.

"Seraphine," he commanded. "Consecrate."

The cleric stepped onto the dais. She placed her palms flat against the stone. She did not offer the quick, functional prayer of a battlefield healing. She began a deep, sustained invocation of divine authority—a declaration that this ground no longer belonged to the forces that had claimed it.

The consecration struck the song-stones like sunlight hitting black ice.

The crystals shrieked.

The anchor stone cracked down the center. 

Silence rushed back into the room.

Aldric studied the ruined shards, then turned toward the prisoners blinking their way back to sanity. 

"Secure the operatives," he said. "Document everything. Seraphine, begin the restoration sweeps. Every last one."

They moved to obey.

Later, in the Canal Row house, Banki let his awareness linger just long enough to catch Maren tuning her lute. She could not make the third string hold. The burned man's ragged scream kept hiding under the note. 

She loosened the peg until the string went flat, closing the case without weaving the rescue into a song.

Banki withdrew his mind, leaving the heroes to their troubled peace. He had the names now. He had the architecture. Tomorrow, he would secure the rest of the quarter. 

## The Dry Kiln

The Dry Kiln operated on the principle that the best hiding place for a criminal workshop was inside a legitimate one.

The building functioned as a real ceramics kiln: salt-glazed stoneware above, enchanted containers below, taxes paid on the honest work and silence paid on the other. It served as a third-ring waystation — a dead-drop den for the broader smuggling network Aldric had just blindly mangled. 

Torren Ashpell ran both operations. Middle-aged, stooped from cellar ceilings, permanently squinting from kiln fire, he was tired, underpaid, and desperate for someone to recognize the value of his craft without treating him like a replaceable hand. 

Banki entered through the legitimate entrance in human form, under the midday sun. His awareness pressed outward, catching Torren's surface impressions as he walked through the ceramics showroom and complimented the glaze work on a display piece that was, genuinely, excellent.

"You made this?" Banki asked, turning a salt-glazed serving bowl in his hands with the attention of a man who appreciated craftsmanship.

Torren blinked. Customers did not, as a rule, ask about the maker. Customers asked about the price. The fact that this customer had inquired about the artisan registered in Torren's surface thoughts as a novelty—a small, unexpected warmth in a daily routine otherwise composed entirely of transaction and tedium.

"I did," Torren said. "Twenty-seven hours in the salt kiln. Four coats. You can see the crystal formation in the glaze if you hold it to the light."

Banki held it to the light. 

"Preservation effect," Banki said quietly, tracing the crystal pattern with one finger. "It halts rot for a fortnight at room temperature. Possibly three weeks if the seal holds tight."

Torren's thoughts froze. The warmth vanished, replaced instantly by sharp, suffocating terror—the absolute panic of a man whose secret had just been spoken aloud by a stranger in his own shop. 

"I'm not here to threaten you," Banki said, before the fear could calcify into flight. "I'm here to hire you."

He set the bowl down, reached into his coat, and produced a pouch containing one hundred platinum pieces—more coin than Torren's criminal employers had paid him in the last three years combined.

"This is a retainer," Banki said, binding the man with the irrefutable logic of an infernal contract. "It buys your exclusive services, your discretion, and your agreement to operate under my protection rather than the network's. You keep the kiln, the cellar workshop, your tools, your techniques, and your pride. Fewer people try to kill you. More people pay you what your work is worth."

Torren stared at the platinum. His thoughts raced through the rapid, desperate calculation of a man offered a lifeboat, trying frantically to determine whether the lifeboat was also a trap.

*...that's real platinum... a hundred pieces... I've never seen a hundred pieces in one place... but if I take it and the network finds out...*

Torren's fear receded by inches. Hope took its place, cautious and kiln-warm. 

"The network," Banki added casually, reading the exact contour of Torren's hesitation, "is currently burning. The Choir is broken. The Outer Carry routes are compromised. The Second Desk will be dismantled before the end of the week. You are standing on the edge of a collapsing building, Torren. I am offering you the only solid ground left in the quarter."

Torren swallowed hard. He looked at the pouch, then at Banki's utterly calm, utterly certain face. 

"When do I start?" Torren asked.

"You already have," Banki said. "Close the shop. We have a great deal of work to do."
