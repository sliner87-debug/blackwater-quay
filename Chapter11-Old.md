"Who are you?" Torren asked.

"I'm the man who noticed that your glaze work is exceptional," Banki said. "And who intends to compensate you at a level that reflects that assessment."

He extended his hand.

Torren took it.

The handshake lasted three seconds.

Before the contract, Banki asked to see the lower kiln in operation.

Torren hesitated. Too late. Jessa Vell, the night glaze-minder, was below because honest labour rarely received a schedule separate from criminal necessity. She was thirty, clever with her hands, and poor enough to believe a locked cellar meant overtime rather than conspiracy. When the hidden shelf opened, she saw too much: preservation sigils, false shipping seals, the second ledger, Torren's face losing colour.

Fear makes witnesses expensive. Banki could have killed her. Torren knew it. Jessa knew it a heartbeat later.

He did something tidier.

He took seven minutes.

Not her life. Not her skill. Only the room, the shelf, the second ledger, and the exact blue glaze recipe her mother had taught her because memory rarely tears along the line one intends. Jessa blinked afterward, looked at the bowl in her hands, and began to cry because she could no longer remember why that shade of blue mattered.

Torren signed while she sat on an overturned crate, alive, employed, unharmed in every way a court would know how to prove, and missing something no compensation clause could name.

The Silk Leash contract was formalised that evening: paid per contract plus bonuses, protection guaranteed, no outside jobs without approval, one strike for leaks, refusal equalled ejection. Torren signed with the particular penmanship of a man who has been practising his signature his whole life for a moment exactly like this one.

Turnkey, Whisperstamp, six rotating Shadows, and enough shibboleths to keep the wrong hands off the doors made the Dry Kiln an outpost by nightfall.

The Dry Kiln was his.

Somewhere past the fog, the last door in the web remained closed.

## The Stitchroom

Rank in a shadow house was not awarded for enthusiasm. Enthusiasm got a person dead in front of witnesses.

Black Lab Two existed to discover who could stay useful after the room learned what work it was built for. Sable ran the floor as an equal sovereign. Banki expected nothing else, acting as an invisible safety net—anchoring her mind psionically so she could lead and fight with absolute autonomy. The candidates stood in order against the wall: S1, S2, S3, S6, and the probationary line trying not to look raw.

Before the trials began, Direction Four sent up its morning report as three slates, one blood-marked glove, and Hesta Bramblewake in a temper that cleared corridors.

"Three problems," she said. "Shimmer instability, close-kill override, command hierarchy."

Darric set the slates down. "The good news is the bodies are holding."

"With experimental siege animals," Sable said, "that is your good news?"

"Yes."

The glove had been sliced across the knuckles. One prototype had struck at the space beside a handler. The tentacle arrived where the hand would have been if Hesta had not trained her people to move like pessimists.

Mirell's note sat under the slate:

On the back of the bottom slate, Mirell had added a mark no handler had been meant to see: KIN-HUNGER? The ink had bled outward in a crescent, as if the paper itself had listened too long.

PSIONIC OBEDIENCE WOULD SOLVE THE WRONG PROBLEM. RECOMMEND RANK-CADENCE, NOT MIND-COMMAND.

Sable read it twice. "No mind-editing loyal people."

"These are not people."

"No turning living things that serve us into laboratory furniture because obedience is easier than trust."





Banki could have argued categories sharp enough to cut hair. Beast, citizen, staff, Crown, tool, ally, asset. He did not, because he had once been called specimen by people who found clean words for cages.

"Rank-cadence," he said.

The test took place behind triple glass. Mirell adjusted collar-plates without touching the minds inside them. Darric stood at the release winch. Hesta held the red stop-cord and a hammer because redundancy, in her theology, was mercy.

Banki fed the collar-plates a cadence: hearth, gate, child, worker, ward, Crown. Not think this. Never that. More like a road built into aggression before aggression became appetite.

The first prototype struck the armed dummy and stopped one handspan short of the heat bladder.

Interpose first. Maim second. Kill only against active lethal threat.

The second turned towards the child-sized heat shape.

Every watcher stopped breathing.

It lowered its body across the lane instead of striking, becoming a wall. The collar sparked. Banki tasted failure, caught it, and did not force the mind.

Kestrel clicked low in her throat.

The beast settled.

"Again," Banki said.

"No," Hesta said. "It succeeded tired. That is not permission to make it fail exhausted."

Want was not proof. Banki ended the test.

By afternoon, the smaller lines had left marks no report could hold. A Choirless Siren Moth settled above a handler with old song-damage and made him weep without knowing why. A Grazier drew shock out of a bitten arm and left the patient ashamed of how badly she wanted to trust it. Behind netted canal gates, an eel obeyed the food bell while every watcher kept one hand near the cut-line.

Nothing in the room felt safe. Some of it helped anyway.

Then the first Stonemaw Gate-King frame nearly killed Hesta's best handler.

It happened during the boring trial. A west drainage seam groaned. The inert Gate-King lattice took the load exactly as designed, anchored, braced, held, and then learned pressure faster than anyone expected. One hook fired early. It pinned Tovan's glove, crushed three fingers, and would have taken the hand if Hesta had not driven a pry-bar between living stone and bone.

Banki paid for the healing. He restored the fingers.

He could not restore the half second when every handler in the room had seen the thing obey design and still hurt one of His.

Hesta wrote the next line into the file with her left hand because her right was still shaking:

SUCCESS IS NOT SAFETY.

Banki signed beneath it.

"Pause the line," Hesta said.

"For how long?"

"Until I stop wanting to punch you when I see the frame."

"That is not a measurable interval."

"Then measure this: no handler goes back into that lane until every person who watched Tovan's hand get crushed can sleep without hearing it."

Banki wanted to object. He wanted to say that fear faded through correct repetition, that the safest way to restore confidence was to demonstrate controlled success, that the work could not wait for nerves to become convenient.

All of it was true.

None of it mattered to Tovan, who sat with his restored fingers wrapped against his chest as if they might be taken again by surprise.

Mara solved the argument by writing the pause into policy before Banki found a way around it.

GATE-KING HANDLER RECOVERY HOLDS COMMAND AUTHORITY.

"That makes fear part of the chain of command," Banki said.

"No," Mara replied. "It makes injury evidence."

Sable looked at him then, and Banki understood the real cost. He had not lost a hand. He had lost the easy confidence of people who had believed his designs could be trusted because they were his. They operated as peers, always; he never violated her autonomy, never physically restrained her from fighting or jumped in front of attacks like some overprotective savior. Instead, he provided an invisible safety net, anchoring her mind psionically so she could fight for herself. Her trust in his designs mattered precisely because she chose it freely.

The Gate-King remained Phase Two because the trapped mason lived. The other Stonemaw branches stayed sealed in background files, useful enough to tempt him and dangerous enough to make Hesta keep the keys.

After that, the Stitchroom felt less like power and more like consequence with better locks.

The man strapped into the iron chair had sold names to the Choir for six months and called it survival. He had been useful, which was why he was still alive. His name had been Odran Vey before the restraints, before the mouth-guard, before the room's lights lowered to a surgical green that made every face look drowned.

Close to him, Banki was only a shape. Small hands in human gloves. A calm voice. Eyes that did not need to widen to become monstrous.

Odran tried to pray. The thought went nowhere useful.

"You will not die from this," Banki said.

Odran understood before the first thread touched him that survival could be a crueller sentence than death.

The Tri-Weave entered through the places where fear had already made doors, manifesting as industrialized, horrific High Magic. Bone-dust bolts and marrow runes glowed in the sterile air, a testament to the brutal efficiency of the spellcraft. Ozone burned behind his teeth. Air pressure collapsed against his ears until the room sounded buried. Then the Geometric Lens opened, and Odran felt himself stop being a person in the way people usually understand personhood. He became surfaces. Vectors. Stored skills. A history of betrayals arranged for convenient reading.

Banki did not eat flesh. Not tonight. The Neural Savant process had learned elegance from Mirell and the cold, unyielding logic of the Divine Calculus from him, bound by the strictures of his Asmodean Pact. Mercurial filaments, reeking of industrialized High Magic, slid through the psionic channel between them, bright as wet wire, and found the knots where useful knowledge lived: dead-drop routes, Choir countersigns, the reflexive hand pattern by which Odran could disarm a canal lock in darkness, the exact mental flinch that told him when a victim was ready to confess anything.

Odran tried to hold his mother's face.

The Stitchroom took the disarming pattern first.

His right hand forgot why fingers mattered. Memory unspooled from muscle and ran shining down the thread into Banki's waiting grasp. The loss did not feel like forgetting. Forgetting is soft. This was subtraction performed with instruments.

Then came countersigns. Then routes. Then the talent for hearing lies in a pause. Each extraction left him more present and less inhabited, a house whose rooms had been labelled useful, unlocked, emptied, and swept.

Banki's face remained still.

His stillness was workmanship. The restraint made it worse.

When the last filament withdrew, Odran remained alive in the chair. His lungs remembered labour. His heart remembered rhythm. His eyes tracked light.

He did not remember why he had been screaming.

Banki flexed the new knowledge once. A canal lock opened inside his imagination. A Choir countersign answered him in Odran's voice.

"Archive him," Banki said.

Mirell waited in the dark below Sablehook.

She was a Deepmind: a Mind-Eater, four-tentacled, brilliant, hungry, and far too honest about what her species considered food. Their partnership survived on limited trust, genuine respect, generous compensation, and the shared understanding that either of them would leave the instant the arrangement became ownership.

They had recognised each other years ago by damage. Mirell had fled an Elder Node that called her research heresy. Banki had walked out of the Thessalan Consortium's burning annex with his own definition of heresy already forming.

"Report," he said.

Mirell stood beside the sample tube with two tentacles locked around the table edge. *Deepmind-adjacent neural extract. Liquid obedience. It leaves the body moving and makes the self step aside.*

"The Choir?"

*Connected, yes. Not to a people. To a secretion chain. Elder Node derivative, three likely communities within supply distance. Four days and enough sample will name which one.*

"You will have it by morning."

Mirell hesitated, which was rarer than fear. *There is one additional matter. A divine pulse brushed the defensive field three hours ago. Downward from the surface. Less than one second.*

Seraphine.

The cleric had come close enough for the Stitchroom to feel her hand at the wall.

The wards had held. The secret survived.

The margin had been narrow.

"Triple divine-frequency dampening," Banki said. "Prepare a null-space counter-resonance. If the next sweep penetrates deeper, it finds ordinary stone."

*Sixteen hours.*

"Twelve."

Mirell's tentacles curled. *Then I had better begin.*

For once, she did not sound amused when she turned away.

"Mirell."

She paused.

"If the source is an Elder Node-"

*Then it will not consider me a person. It will consider me a deviation with legs.*

"And me?"

*A thief wearing stolen organs of thought.*

There was no accusation in it. That made the sentence worse.

Banki looked at the Stitchroom apparatus, the preserved tissue, the matrices, the tools that would have made any righteous visitor reach for fire. "Do you regret teaching me?"

Mirell's answer came after a delay long enough to be honest.

*No. I regret that useful knowledge so often arrives covered in victims.*

For a moment, neither of them looked at the chair.

Banki climbed back towards the mundane levels with narrowing margins in his mouth.

Above him, Aldric's party was getting closer. Below him, the Elder Node's possible supply chain had acquired a shape.

That night, in the warded house on Canal Row, Maren asked Aldric about the scar that ran from his left collarbone to his right hip.

He told her about Ashenmere: the marshman who preserved villagers alive in glass, the brilliant compound that could have saved thousands, the gallery of conscious bodies, the mentor who died ordering Aldric to finish it.

"His results had value," Aldric said. "His methods worked. The villagers were alive. And he was evil."

Maren heard the conclusion before he spoke Banki's name.

"Results and motives are not the same," Aldric said.

"You're going back."

"When the protocols support direct action."

"And if they never do?"

The look he gave her carried thirty years of conviction and every danger conviction can become.

"They will," Aldric said. "They always do."

The candle burned between them. Maren did not know whether it made the room brighter or merely showed the shadows more clearly.

Three hours after nightfall, the threat stopped being a matter of whispered reports and became an active restructuring of the streets.

Caspel Rooke was crossing the Canal Row bridge when the air changed. The fog did not roll; it flattened, pressed down by an invisible weight that made his mule refuse the wood and sit back on its haunches. It was not a magical chill. It was the smell—not of brackish water or rotting cabbage, but of deep-lake silt, cold copper, and the sharp, wet scent of things that had never seen the sun.

At the center of the bridge stood Sergeant Vane. He was town guard, an ordinary man who had spent twelve years taking minor bribes to ignore late wagons.

Rooke pulled his mule’s reins, his Mind-Sense pinging automatically under the standing shadow instructions.

Vane did not register as a man.

He registered as a beacon.

The thoughts in the sergeant's head were not the shallow, anxious loops of a guard—they were a single, high-frequency resonance, so loud and wet that Rooke’s own skull throbbed in response. It was the telepathic signature of a thousand minds singing in perfect, flat unison, a chorus with no individuals in it.

Vane’s eyes were wide, the pupils blown so large that the grey iris was only a thin rim. Black, salt-encrusted veins stood out like cords along his throat, bulging beneath the leather of his collar. He did not walk; he was pulled, his legs moving with the jerky, mechanical precision of a puppet on fresh hemp.

He stopped three paces from Rooke. His mouth opened.

When the voice came, it was not Vane’s gravelly bark. It was the light, high-pitched voice of the Belowmarket boy. It was Tovin Vale.

“The metal remembers,” the sergeant’s mouth said. The tongue clicked against the teeth, stiffly, as if learning the shape of the letters. “It does not hurt if it still stirs. You must sort the spoons. You must sort the spoons by usefulness.”

“Vane?” Rooke called, his hand slipping toward the shortsword at his hip.

The guard’s head tilted sideways, a sharp, joint-cracking motion that had nothing to do with muscle. The black veins on his face began to leak. Not blood—a thick, grey-yellow brine that smelled of vinegar and old lead.

“The specimen is not in Cell Nine,” Vane’s mouth announced, the voice rising to a wet shriek that echoed off the damp brick of the warehouses. “The ledger must be balanced. Aureth Voss is at his desk. Aureth Voss is reading the file. Aureth Voss is—”

The name broke. The telepathic resonance surged, a sudden, blinding spike of psionic pressure that hit Rooke like a physical blow, driving him to his knees on the wet wood of the bridge. The mule screamed, thrashing against the traces.

Vane’s face buckled.

It did not bleed; it collapsed. The bone beneath the skin dissolved as the psionic force backfired, the skull flattening inward like a stepped-on egg. A thick mixture of grey wax, brine, and boiled brain-matter sheeted out of his ears and nose, hissing as it struck the wet stones of the bridge. The body did not fall; it stayed upright for three beats, suspended by the telepathic tension, before the connection snapped and Vane folded into a heap of wet wool and broken leather.

The fog rushed back, swallowing the smell of copper.

Rooke lay on his stomach, his nose bleeding, his mind still ringing with the echo of the chorus. He looked at the puddle of brine by his hand. It was already crystalizing, turning into grey salt under the cold rain.

He did not call for the guard. He did not run.

He pulled his journal from his coat with trembling fingers and wrote: *Direction Four compromised. Elder Node active at range. It used Tovin's voice to speak through a guard. The host collapsed. Connection limit estimated at three miles.*

He closed the book and spat blood into the canal.

Banki had spent years building a system that was quiet, legal, and orderly. But the thing in the deep tunnels did not want a system. It wanted a mouth. And if it could use Banki’s own citizens to build one, the compound was no longer a refuge—it was a target.

## Project Second Bite

Banki did want a monster.

The first uncomfortable truth was that the monster he wanted first was not a war beast.

Project Second Bite began as the Caravan Line.

It began with six Dire Rhinos breathing steam into a cold yard, with Darric's handlers saying rest before profit, with Sable watching every gate swing as if hinges had moral character, with Banki understanding that a kingdom which could not move weight through danger was not a kingdom at all. It began as logistics. It became bloodline. Then doctrine.

The Military Line did not belong in that first dream. It arrived later, when protection stopped looking like roadwork and started looking like siege weather.

The old file names stayed on the inner slate because mistakes deserve fossils: RhinoWhip, DDR, Sablehook Beast, Project Second Bite.

The current heading was cleaner.

DIRE DISPLACER RHINO.

Mara hated the file. Not because it was sloppy. Sloppy files could be fixed. This file was precise enough to become a crime with witnesses.

Direction Four opened the black-ring yard two nights after Hesta's stop-condition report. Black wax marked the threshold. Pink thread crossed the inner latch. No one stepped over either without naming which part of the work might make them ashamed later.

The first living proof stood in the centre pen.

Proto-1 had once been a Dire Rhino female with a good chest, a stubborn gait, and enough patience to make handlers mistake tolerance for stupidity. Now she was low and huge and wrong in several directions. Displacer tissue rode along the shoulders in living anchor-plates. Paired tentacles rose above the forequarters, not attached like decoration but braided through muscle, nerve, balance, and spatial instinct. When she shifted her weight, the afterimage arrived late. When she breathed, the pen's shadows revised their opinions.

"Chassis stable," Darric said.

"Barely," Hesta said.

"Displacer integration real," Kessa added from behind the glass. "Not graceful."

Mirell's thought touched Banki's mind. *Tentacle response is noncosmetic. Spatial organs are trainable. Highest instability remains displacement under agitation.*

Behind the glass, Darric's pencil stopped moving. Hesta did not look away from the kill-cord. Kessa whispered the animal's old stable name once, too softly for the file.

Proto-1 watched them all.

Not with human intelligence. Not with animal simplicity. With something in between: appetite organised around mass, territorial patience, herd logic, handler memory, and the new displacer anticipation that kept trying to turn every moving thing into an ambush problem.

Banki stepped to the red salt line.

Hesta's hand moved to the kill-cord. "You cross that line before I say yes and I resign in writing with sketches."

"Sketches?"

"So Mara understands the shape of the stupid."

Sable did not smile. Kestrel's jaw parted by a tooth-width, which was the closest her species came to a smile anyone sensible wanted to see.

"I am not crossing," Banki said.

Arcane shape, written in bone-dust and marrow runes, found load and tendon. This was industrialized, horrific High Magic. Sovereign law followed, dictated by the ruthless arithmetic of the Divine Calculus and his Asmodean Pact—not holy, but possessive. Psionic intent braided last, drawn through scar, hunger, and every cage the Thessalan Consortium had taught him to hate.

The three became Sovereign Thread.

Proto-1 struck the inner gate.

The gate held. The room did not enjoy holding it.

Her tentacles lashed towards the pressure source and missed by exactly the distance her own displacement had lied to her. One hooked empty air. One struck the floor hard enough to crack the shock tile. Her horn dipped towards a handler's heat through two walls and a false scent curtain.

"Predatory override," Hesta snapped.

Kestrel's mind slid into the working like a blade entering a sheath.

Not calm, she thought. Rank.

Banki opened the Geometric Lens and saw Proto-1 as conflict made flesh: mass forward, wrongness sideways, tentacles reaching, training waiting, fear teaching every warm thing to look like wall, wound, or food.

He did not impose human obedience. He translated purpose into her own grammar: probe, brace, carry, catch, redirect, pin without crush, crush only when breach, siege, or active lethal threat made any gentler answer impossible.

Proto-1 stopped striking.

Steam poured from her nostrils. The afterimage steadied. Not vanished. Never that. A Dire Displacer Rhino that did not lie about where it stood was merely an expensive rhinoceros with extra ropes. But the lie now had edges. It no longer lied to itself.

Kessa lifted one hand. "Again. With the child-shape."

Banki looked at her.

"If the line cannot refuse a child-shaped target while agitated," Kessa said, "the line ends here."

Hesta approved with a grunt that sounded like gravel acquiring religion.

The child-sized heat form rolled into the outer lane.

Proto-1 turned.

Inside her, the room did not sort itself into words. It arrived as pressure, heat, handler-salt, gate, horn-space, wrong-space, warm-small. The new organs offered three futures at once: catch, crush, cross. Old herd memory answered with weight and rank. The Sovereign Thread pressed one taste into the noise.

Warm-small was not prey.

Every watcher stopped breathing.

The nearest tentacle opened, wide enough to wrap the target and crush it into warm paste.

Then the tentacle flattened against the floor in front of the heat form and became a barrier.

Proto-1 lowered her whole body across the lane.

Not prey.

Ward.

Kestrel's approval entered the room like weather changing. *Useful. Still not safe.*

"Protector-grade cognitive hybrid," Mirell said through the wall speaker, because Deepminds apparently became academic when everyone else was trying not to shake. "Role-bearing. Engineered. Not ready for breeding."

"Study and refinement," Darric said at once.

"No production," Hesta said.

Banki wanted more. The want was bright and immediate and honest enough to be dangerous.

Sable looked at him.

He signed the slate: STUDY AND REFINEMENT AUTHORISED. BREEDING NOT AUTHORISED.

The next review came after failures, culls, one work-limited survivor, and a week in which Direction Four ate more money than several villages saw in a year.

Ash-Halter was worse because she lived.

She had been meant for the Caravan Line: wide chest, patient eye, clean harness tolerance, the kind of animal Darric could imagine trusting with winter roads and frightened passengers. The military graft took, then took too much. Her tentacles responded beautifully to impact and poorly to kindness. She could stand a gate. She could guard a barn. She would never pull a public wagon, never breed, never become the road monarch Banki had imagined when he first said Dire Rhino and Displacer in the same private thought.

Sable made him put one hand against Ash-Halter's flank and feel the tremor he had bought.

Banki hated the waste.

Sable made him say the other word.

"Cost," he said.

"Again."

Mirell kept the nerve maps and tissue comparisons. Hesta kept the sound Ash-Halter made when she tried to stand. Between them, the room learned which record mattered more.

Only after Ash-Halter could stand without screaming did Proto-1 become founder.

Banki entered the outer pen under full safety law: red salt, kill angles, rescue angles. He kept the Screaming Skull form of his Eldritch Blast primed just beneath his knuckles, the horrific High Magic ready to unleash if the beast broke containment, while Hesta stayed on the cord, Darric reading body, Kessa with recovery light, Mirell below, Kestrel linked, Null-Fang tasting every command for weakness.
