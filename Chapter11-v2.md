# Chapter 11

## Talonscript

"Talonscript."

The dark, scaled tip of Kestrel's tail flicked once in the freezing air of the subterranean pen.

"Mothledge."

Her sleek, armored shoulders lowered by a fraction of a degree—so small a shift that only an observer consumed by total obsession would register it. Obsession was the bedrock upon which the core literacies of Sablehook were anchored.

"Family-only," Banki said, his voice quiet in the stone vault. "Public moniker?"

The response arrived faster this time, slicing through the chill atmosphere of the training pen through their mental link.

*Kestrel.*

A sharp, dangerous joke, perhaps, or a simple preference for a lighter knife when treading among ordinary company. Either way, Banki accepted the designation. Public and private truths were the two lungs by which sovereign institutions drew breath.

He reached into his coat and produced the crest-token—the dark, heavy piece cut specifically for house family seals rather than routine staff assignments. It was forged from unpolished ironwood and iron, worked with the house sign in a form subtle enough that a watcher had to look twice, and honest enough that the second look hurt more than the first. Grand, ostentatious symbols belonged to fools who believed affection required witnesses. He pressed the metal lightly against the scarred lattice along Kestrel's neck.

The reaction was instantaneous. The atmospheric pressure in the chamber dropped sharply. Recognition settled like cold dust upon stone.

"Good," Banki said.

Null-Fang, permitted at last to do more than stand rigid beside the threshold, sent a single, probing pulse of thought across the mental web.

*She is sharp.*

Banki locked his gaze with Kestrel's golden eyes. "She."

The correction struck the sentient blade like an iron finger flicked against a crystal goblet. Slight. Precise. Nonnegotiable.

*She is family,* Null-Fang amended after a brief, vibrating pause.

Better.

The harmonic overlay followed. Sablehook required no more sudden miracles that night, but certain doors, once unlocked, demand to be stepped through before cowardice tallies the cost. Banki crossed the dirt floor of the pen and laid his palm flat against the side of Kestrel's neck where the scar-lattice cooled beneath her hide. She did not flinch. Through her consciousness, the room expanded into vertical geometry first: beam, iron rail, wall stress, air velocity, kill angle. Through his, she tasted the dry, cold ugliness of administrative ownership—and nearly approved.

The primary bond anchored deep into blood, sinew, and marrow. The deeper tether unspooled between them—dangerous, volatile, and maintained entirely by mutual choice. A final depth remained untouched, waiting for a day when the world proved it deserved such a devastating union.

When the mental connection gradually eased back, both stood without embarrassment, making no pretense that the act had been ordinary.

He smiled then—a private, cold expression wholly unsuited for council chambers.

"Welcome home," he said.

Kestrel blinked once, slow and deliberate.

The nearest translation his mind could shape of the pulse surging back from her was this:

*Already was.*

## Audit of the Fourth Vault

The first true audit of the Fourth Vault occurred three nights after Kestrel's verdict.

It commenced with a bell possessing no clapper. The alert resonated through the bedrock instead: three sharp pulses through the stone beneath Banki's boots, a brief pause, then a single sustained vibration communicating to every cleared soul that the creature-side spine had awakened. The signal did not ascend into the upper compound; it did not reach the kitchens, the dormitories, the administrative offices, or any chamber where a timid clerk might confuse controlled violence for catastrophe. The Fourth Vault did not announce its existence to those unequipped to wield its secrets.

A senior night handler fell into step beside Banki as he cut through the torchlit corridor toward Direction Four. The report was delivered in rapid, crisp fragments. A disturbance during the night-cycle observation. No breach. No handler casualties. No structural collapse.

"Dire RhinoWhip Prototype One," the handler reported, his jaw tight. "Abnormal spatial testing. Repeated attention directed at a single containment transition seam. Altered tentacle deployment. Apparent anticipatory movement prior to handler correction."

The handler swallowed hard, his throat dry. "It executed a successful exploitation of chamber timing that should not yet have existed within its learned behavioral pattern."

Kestrel’s eyes narrowed to glowing slits beside Banki in the corridor dark.

"So it did not merely test," Banki murmured. "It solved something."

"Yes, my lord. The smart-room element—a timing-based transition gate, one of our newer containment features. Not fully breached, but predicted. The floor handlers felt the chamber had been audited rather than merely battered."

That was precisely the sentence that roused a sovereign in the small hours. Prototype One remained physically contained, exhibiting no signs of full frenzy. It was not battering blindly; it was already thinking in weather, ambush, yield, and spatial terrain.

Banki descended with Sable on his left—moving with her unassailable autonomy—Kestrel above and ahead in the ceiling shadows, Darric and Hesta Bramblewake waiting at the primary iron portcullis, and Kessa standing behind a reinforced observation slit with her hands folded as if attending a sermon conducted by jaws.

The beast-wards stirred around them, but only specific files commanded the room's focus. The First Vault handled routes. The Second held relics, residue, and dead things refusing to finish being problems. The Third wove doctrine into wearable armor. The Fourth smelled of hot breath and copper.

Tonight, the vault's active center narrowed to the creatures capable of altering a room the moment they entered it.

Choirless Siren Moths clung to copper hoops above the recovery corridor. A single hummed note from a captive hymnist made their pale wings unseal and devour the sound, leaving a silence soft as wool and just as suffocating. Protection, yes; also erasure, if deployed without mercy. Banki suspected Maren would despise them and perhaps understand them first.

Mercy Thorn Graziers lowered warm muzzles over injured handlers, drawing shock out through red buds along their crowns. Kessa had corrected him when he once termed it feeding: *It pulls the agony away. There is a profound difference.*

Mirror-Maw Canal Eels moved where the water reflected them wrong—a mouth appearing in the glass-still surface half a breath before the scaled body surfaced. *No free canal release,* Sable had decreed. Kessa nodded in agreement: *Net gates at every Crown waterline.*

The Stonemaw work remained mostly floor, tremor, brace, and argument. No living Gate-King breathed in Sablehook yet, but the floor learned impact, the braces learned weight, and the iron gates learned the difference between holding against collapse and holding a person in.

Kestrel's file stayed sealed. Banki had written the restraint in his own hand: *Kestrel is not livestock.*

The war-line came last because it had to. Teeth were simple to forge; purpose was difficult to instill. Musk. Blood-warm hay. Salt. A trace of ozone. Stone dust. Wet hide. The particular sourness rolling off creatures large enough to make terror the only rational response.

"Before we proceed further," Hesta said, her voice cutting through the heavy musk, "we review the doctrine."

"I know the doctrine."

"We review them because memory shapes itself around desire, and convenience erodes discipline."

Sable's mouth tightened into a hard line. Banki inclined his head. "Proceed."

Hesta held up a single, calloused finger. "No staff in a lane without two exits. No open gate without three wardens. No warden without a kill angle. No kill angle without a rescue route. No feeding during temper-shifts. No breeding permission until Darric signs body-stability and I sign handler-stability. No command test if the creature has not eaten, slept, and accepted a quiet hour."

"And if I overrule?"

"Then I resign in writing, loudly, with a copy delivered to Mara."

"A devastating weapon."

"The most dangerous kind: paperwork with witnesses."

Kessa's gaze remained fixed on the pen beyond the slit. "She speaks truly."

The pen held three prototypes. They possessed no official designation yet; Hesta's handlers called them RhinoWhips only when far enough away to be foolish. Dire Rhino mass carried an unsettling wrongness through shoulder, horn, and tentacle; the pen survived because pessimists had constructed the Fourth Vault.

Darric passed Banki the slate.

*Bones hold the new weight.*
*The shadow-slip takes root.*
*Lash-vines move with intent.*
*The void-blur falters under pressure.*
*Hunger eclipses command at close range.*
*Loyalty untested.*
*Keep caged.*

Banki read the final line twice.

The largest prototype turned its heavy, armored head toward him. The afterimage lagged behind the motion like a poorly rehearsed lie. For a single breath, the creature's true skull, its shadow-displaced skull, the horn-ghost, and the tentacle-blur all occupied different spaces. A handler behind the secondary gate swore softly and received no mockery for it.

Banki cast his thoughts outward, brushing the edges of the beast's mind. The Tri-Weave followed—Arcane, Divine, and Psionic threads braided into a single, lucid control architecture. Finally, he pushed the Sovereign Thread through it all, not to dominate blindly, but to test the prototypes against the law they were built to answer.

The room changed. Handlers ceased breathing. The shimmer around the pens sharpened.

Inside the reinforced holds, the prototypes answered—not verbally, but through heavy rhino mass, wrong-angle predatory drift, displacement pressure, muscle memory that didn't entirely belong to any natural beast, and the deep biological truth of entities forged to survive correction or perish trying.

The core body-plan correction held. The dire rhino chassis remained dominant and stable.

Kestrel dropped from the ceiling beam without a sound.

All three prototypes reacted. Recognition sharpened into active threat. The largest one's horn dipped; its tentacles spread with the terrifying precision of a killing engine discovering the room had acquired a target worth destroying.

Kestrel did not move. She merely breathed.

The moment arrived when the prototypes registered what most observers missed until it was too late: Kestrel lacked the shape of prey; she lacked the shape of a handler. She was an end-point—a creature that moved when motion proved useful and halted when stopping made the world come to her. The largest prototype held her stare for three heartbeats, four, five.

Then it lowered its head an inch—a clear acknowledgment of rank.

Darric exhaled through his nose. "Well. That answers one question and births six worse ones."

"It recognizes apex hierarchy," Banki said.

"It recognizes Kestrel," Hesta corrected. "Do not transform a single good sign into doctrine by breakfast."

"Noted." Banki turned his attention back to the slate. "We lock the breeding lines tonight. The override holds. Four breeding pairs. Each line-true, each distinct in spirit and intellect, all fully corrected."

Darric blinked, struggling to process the sudden leap from observation to full-scale infrastructure. "Four pairs? My lord, the gestation—"

"Three months," Banki dictated, rewriting biological reality with the casual certainty of a crown. "Working first-generation target of two-to-twenty-four young per female. Magical acceleration is authorized. Start drafting the cohort testing plans."

The researchers forgot to record the order. One of the specialists visibly re-evaluated the scale of the universe.

Sable peered through the observation slit at the three enormous bodies. "You want them."

"Yes."

"For trade caravans?"

"Eventually."

"For the streets?"

"If the city becomes a battlefield."

"For terror?"

Banki let the silence stretch a fraction too long. His hesitation answered for him.

Sable's voice dropped. "No spectacle releases. No proving-point deployments. No using them to make respectable citizens soil themselves because it amuses you."

"Respectable people so frequently require encouragement."

"Banki."

The name arrived quiet and sharp, cutting harder because it lacked the theatricality of a public rebuke. It was private even with five observers present—a dialogue between equals.

He studied the prototypes again. Teeth for Sablehook. Teeth for roads, kitchens, dormitories, children, workers, Blackwater citizens who had entered their names into his ledgers and believed, perhaps foolishly, that the Crown represented shelter. These would be guardians, if he could force the impossible shape of that word onto beasts constructed from siege mass and spatial misdirection.

"Crown Shield rules," he declared, acknowledging her authority. "If they ever leave the Fourth Vault, they protect routes, people, and wards. They interpose before they kill. They do not pursue into homes. They do not hunt civilians. They do not become a lesson I teach because my temper craves a blackboard."

Hesta stared at him. "Write that down," she instructed the empty space where Mara usually stood.

"I will inform her."

"No need," Sable said. "She will know by lunch."

The largest prototype breathed steam against the iron gate. Its afterimage breathed half a second late. Banki stepped closer to the observation line, careful to remain behind the safety mark Hesta had painted onto the floor in red salt.

"Keep them caged," he said.

Darric nodded grimly. "Without question."

"Yet the foundation holds."

"It holds."

The Fourth Vault possessed teeth. They were merely waiting for a mouth.

## The Heroes' Trail

Caelen discovered the primary dead drop on Tuesday morning.

The ranger worked the Dockside sector in a methodical grid, placing his trust in mud, loose cobblestones, and old tracking habits rather than quick cleverness. Banki monitored the hunt through the fragmented minds of the district—a beggar's dull stare, a stray dog's scent-memory, a gull's soaring loop above the harbor. The fractured surveillance annoyed him, yet it served its purpose flawlessly.

The dead drop was housed within a wax-sealed envelope jammed behind a loose granite block in the canal wall beside the Tidewash Steps. The location offered ideal operational utility—glaringly obvious to an operative who knew where to look, completely invisible to the casual observer. The parchment bore no wax crest, no identifying seal.

The enclosed intelligence was impeccable. The handwriting belonged to a ghost: precise, steady, and entirely stripped of personal nuance. Banki had paid an exorbitant price for that absolute absence.

Caelen delivered the envelope directly to Aldric within the hour.

From that moment, Banki reconstructed the heroes' preparations through a mosaic of intercepted perceptions. A street-sweeper catching sight of five figures huddled over a map; a dockhand observing Aldric's massive palm flattening a chart; a runaway child watching the elven ranger pry a loose floorboard and smile at the wrongness beneath.

Aldric refused to be rushed. That methodical, unyielding patience made Banki despise the paladin all the more.

Through the thin psychic tether bridging the district, Banki caught the crisp cadence of Aldric's orders.

"Caelen: verify the safe house locations. Physical reconnaissance exclusively—zero contact, zero engagement, no active scrying. I demand entry vectors, exit routes, and defensive ward signatures."

"Seraphine: commence preparation for restoration rites. If this intelligence is accurate, twelve citizens are currently living with stolen personalities, and we are going to restore them."

"Lyris: audit the song-stone network. I need to understand how the leylines link, which crystal serves as the anchor, and what occurs to the web when we shatter the root."

"Maren: locate the affected families. Quietly. Build trust. When we strike the Choir, these people must know someone is coming to rescue their stolen kin."

Maren spent the afternoon weaving through the shadowed doorways of the lower quarter. By nightfall, a silent, bitter grief radiated off her, bleeding across the psychic tether as she returned bearing six tragic stories and a single brass button torn from a missing clerk's coat.

She offered the button to Seraphine. The cleric brushed the tarnished metal, her consciousness recoiling. *The compulsion did not erase love,* Seraphine realized, the thought broadcasting so loudly Banki caught every vibration. *It built a stone wall around it.*

Maren held the button tightly in her palm long after she should have set it down. Cloth retained physical pressure; human minds did as well. Banki tasted her bitter wonder at what kind of entity could return a family member to their hearth yet leave the home colder for having saved them.

Caelen slid a clean piece of polishing cloth beside her elbow, acknowledging her grief without forcing her to speak it aloud.

Across the room, Seraphine whispered a prayer over the list of victims. The Dawnfather's golden light flared brightly, warming the parchment, yet the dark ink remained untouched—a stubborn stain of raw reality resisting divine erasure. Seraphine shivered, her unease rippling through the link, disturbed by the holy light's failure to cleanse the ink.

"We strike tomorrow night," Aldric announced. "The anchor stone first. We shatter the crystal lattice, sever the song-lines, and sweep the safe houses before the Choir can relocate their stock."

The party committed to preparation. Whetstones rasped against cold steel. Spells were memorized and bound into components. In the dark hours before dawn, while thick harbor fog rolled through the alleys, five companions knelt in a circle and begged their god for the strength to survive the coming slaughter.

Aldric verified every escape vector twice, then audited the side alleys other men had ignored. Mercy, the paladin knew, failed most frequently at the exits.

Six blocks away, Banki absorbed the sudden surge of divine energy—a warm, golden pressure slicing through the fog, conviction given the physical weight of weather.

The heroes would strike the Choir tomorrow night. Banki understood the precise architecture they were hunting. The Choir was no mere fanatical cult; it was an administrative behemoth concealed beneath the skin of religion. It maintained holding pens, illegal carry trails, and false ledgers embedded within the very bones of the lower trade tenements.

Something would inevitably go wrong. Something always did.

On Banki's worktable, his contingency map waited, its eastern corner pinned down by the bare steel of a knife.

## The Choir Burns

The captured lamp observer looked less like a hardened conspirator and more like a terrified clerk who had wandered far beyond the border of his own courage.

Banki discovered his observation post half a mile past Crooked Lantern Pool, where a grove of dead cedar leaned over a shale ridge, offering an unobstructed view of the road below. The man had constructed a small nest from oil-canvas, a long spyglass, a signal mirror, dry rations, and ciphered note slips sealed in red wax. A patient setup. Someone anticipated a long watch with minimal comfort; someone also expected to remain hidden.

Kestrel dropped from the tree canopy downwind without a sound.

She dragged the man backward through the thorny brush with one foreclaw hooked into his collar, her harness blade laid so flat against his jawline it appeared almost affectionate. Vesper followed carrying the mirror kit, her face set in an expression of quiet satisfaction at having proven a point. The observer's boots tore deep furrows in the mud. His frantic thoughts shrieked so violently Banki hardly needed to press his mind forward to catch them.

*Mirrorhouse sings. Count the supply wagons. Count the flesh jars. Map the iron chains. Map the beast lines. Sablehook is growing teeth. Do not let them take you alive.*

"A atrocious set of instructions," Banki said.

The man tried to spit; fear dried his mouth, yielding nothing.

They bound him under the lee of the shale, leaving Banki free to turn his attention back toward the city.

At midnight, Banki cast his mind across the district, anchoring his awareness in a rooftop feline, a wall-rat, two restless dockhands, and the ambient thoughts of a populace unaware they served as his surveillance network.

He sat far enough away to risk nothing, remaining close enough to study what Good executed when time ran out.

In the warehouse, Aldric's sword chimed against the first Choir guard's shield.

The shield shattered. The guard recognized the fatal opening too late.

Aldric turned the strike at the final instant. The flat of the blade, not the edge, delivered a blow with sufficient force to terminate the argument. The guard dropped into the canal runoff and did not rise.

Behind the paladin, Seraphine flooded the chamber with light.

The Choir's illusions dissolved. The warehouse stripped off its mundane disguise. Crates became cages; dusty tarps became operating screens. The space revealed itself as a fully functional holding-node—intake cages, sorting chains, transfer ledgers stacked against the far wall. Banki recognized it instantly: a second-tier custody pen, a false-ledger clearing house hidden beneath a common smuggling front.

Three clerks blinked in the glare, their borrowed, vacant smiles collapsing. Caelen's arrows pinned hands, bows, and alarm whistles to the wood. Lyris's ward swallowed the first hymn before it could burrow into anyone's skull.

Maren played her lute.

She played a steady, grounding line, and the altered clerks latched onto it. One of them looked up with clear eyes for the first time in weeks.

"Help me," the man rasped.

Maren kept her fingers moving. The song anchored the fractured minds by refusing to turn away from the ugliness of the room.

"Seraphine," Maren called out. "Three civilians. Still in there."

Seraphine rushed to them, both hands radiating warmth. One by one, stolen names returned. A clerk sobbed into his own sleeve. Another vomited black, oily fluid onto the floor and apologized profusely for the mess. The third kept asking if his wife knew where he was.

The upper level fell in four minutes.

Aldric found one operative still conscious near the trapdoor leading to the lower chambers.

The man had crawled behind a massive prayer-altar, clamping both hands over his ears as if refusing to listen to his own defeat might somehow delay it. He was young enough to harbor soft fear, yet old enough to have chosen this brutal work. Dark ink stained his fingers—Choir ink, routing ink, the ink belonging to a man who knew exactly where people were sent after their names ceased to matter.

Banki leaned forward into the psychic tether, tasting the man's surface thoughts. *Second Desk... Velis Rowe... Maelis... Harrow Fen...* The mental architecture of a vast administrative conspiracy spilled out in uncoordinated panic.

Caelen dragged him out into the open.

"Where are the altered kept?" Aldric asked.

The operative spat blood and clamped his jaw shut.

Seraphine stepped forward, a binding prayer rising to her lips, but Aldric raised one hand. He moved with terrifying calm.

"We have minutes," Aldric said.

He pressed the flat of his consecrated blade against the man's cheek.

Light flooded the room.

It carried no heat a kitchen would recognize. This was divine pressure—moral certainty made physical, blistering skin wherever it encountered corruption and driving pure agony through every lie the body tried to shelter. The operative unleashed a ragged, tearing scream.

"Where?" Aldric asked again.

"Below the cistern!" the man gasped, thrashing against Caelen's grip. "Left grate! Mercy! Please!"

Aldric kept the blade pressed to the blistering flesh.

"How many?"

"Twelve. Maybe more. I don't know."

"Who purchased the last shipment?"

The man sobbed—a wet, broken sound.

Seraphine's face drained of color. "Aldric."

"Twenty minds hang in the balance," Aldric said, his gaze locked on the weeping operative. "If his pain buys them back, then his pain is a cheap price."

Seraphine's light trembled once before she forced it steady. Her revulsion bled across the tether; the man on the floor was guilty, she reasoned, but that did not render the torture holy.

The names spilled out after that—not all of them, but enough.

When Aldric finally lifted the sword, the imprint of the Dawnfather's light remained seared onto the man's cheek—a second, permanent confession. Maren stared at the recovered map. A cold knot of understanding pulled tight in her chest: Aldric had saved lives, and he had done so by making a helpless man scream.

Banki, sitting in the dark miles away, went very still.

*So,* he thought. *There you are.*

Banki appreciated the revelation. Aldric was no fool constrained by naive pacifism; Aldric was a weapon that simply selected its own targets. The paladin had just shattered a delicate intelligence asset—a third-hand routing clerk, a man who knew where every body was moved and whose seal opened the next door in the chain—for the immediate gratification of recovering twelve missing people. Aldric cared nothing for Outer Carry doctrine or Dead Stone records; Aldric cared nothing that this man could have unraveled the entire ledger of Maelis and the Second Desk. Aldric saw only the suffering in front of him and broke the machine to halt it. It was a devastating, short-sighted goodness—the exact breed of goodness that left the root of the problem alive to regenerate tomorrow.

The descent to the song-stone chamber led through a trapdoor Caelen found in the north corner—the exact location Banki's anonymous intelligence package had specified.

Aldric descended first, his sword's light rendering the stairwell honest and ugly. This was the second throat—the hidden descent. Banki recognized the maintenance scars, the narrow stone, the smuggling passage built for moving bodies between sanctioned hands.

At the bottom lay the lower chamber.

Three crystals sat upon a raised stone dais, humming.

The vibration carried a low, continuous thrum. Lyris's protective ward absorbed the sound, nullifying the compulsion before it could breach their minds. The crystals possessed a sharp, geometric beauty. Through the tether, Aldric's cold pragmatism echoed; the paladin registered this beauty exactly the way a soldier registers the edge of an enemy's blade—as a feature of design rather than a redeeming quality.

"Lyris," Aldric said. "Analysis."

"Three stones," she replied. "The center leads. Shatter the anchor, and the song dies."

"Defenses?"

"We already bypassed them."

Aldric studied the crystals. He glanced at Seraphine, then down at his sword.

"Seraphine," he commanded. "Consecrate."

The cleric stepped onto the dais. She placed her palms flat against the stone. She offered no quick, functional prayer of battlefield healing; she began a deep, sustained invocation of divine authority—a declaration that this ground no longer belonged to the forces that had claimed it.

The consecration struck the song-stones like sunlight hitting black ice.

The crystals shrieked.

The anchor stone cracked down the center.

Silence rushed back into the room.

Aldric studied the ruined shards, then turned toward the prisoners blinking their way back to sanity.

"Secure the operatives," he said. "Document everything. Seraphine, begin the restoration sweeps. Every last one."

They moved to obey.

Later, in the Canal Row safe house, Banki allowed his awareness to linger just long enough to watch Maren attempt to tune her lute. She could not make the third string hold its pitch; the burned operative's ragged scream kept hiding beneath the note.

She loosened the tuning peg until the string went completely flat, then snapped the case shut without weaving their victory into a song.

Banki withdrew his mind, leaving the heroes to their poisoned triumph. He possessed the names now; he possessed the operational architecture. Tomorrow, he would claim the remainder of the quarter.

## The Dry Kiln

The Dry Kiln operated on the simple principle that the ideal hiding place for an illicit workshop was inside a legitimate one.

The building functioned as a genuine ceramics kiln: salt-glazed stoneware crafted on the ground floor, enchanted containment jars manufactured below, taxes paid on the legal wares and silence purchased for the rest. It served as a third-ring waystation—a dead-drop den for the broader smuggling apparatus Aldric had just blindly mangled.

Torren Ashpell managed both sides of the business. Middle-aged, stooped from decades in cramped cellars, permanently squinting from kiln heat, he was exhausted, underpaid, and desperate for someone to recognize the value of his craft without treating him as a replaceable cog.

Banki entered through the main showroom in human guise beneath the midday sun. His awareness expanded, catching Torren's surface thoughts as he walked past rows of pottery and paused to inspect a salt-glazed serving bowl that was, genuinely, exquisite.

"Did you craft this yourself?" Banki asked, turning the heavy bowl in his gloved hands.

Torren blinked. Customers did not, as a rule, inquire about the artisan; customers asked about the price. The fact that this patron had asked about the maker registered in Torren's surface thoughts as a novelty—a small, unexpected warmth in a daily routine composed entirely of transaction and tedium.

"I did," Torren said. "Twenty-seven hours in the salt kiln. Four coats. You can observe the crystal formation in the glaze if you hold it to the light."

Banki held it to the light.

"Preservation effect," Banki said quietly, tracing the crystal pattern with one finger. "It halts rot for a fortnight at room temperature. Possibly three weeks if the seal holds tight."

Torren's thoughts froze. The warmth vanished, replaced instantly by sharp, suffocating terror—the absolute panic of a man whose secret had just been spoken aloud by a stranger in his own shop.

"I am not here to threaten you," Banki said, before the fear could calcify into flight. "I am here to hire you."

He set the bowl down, reached into his coat, and produced a pouch containing one hundred platinum pieces—more coin than Torren's criminal employers had paid him in the last three years combined.

"This is a retainer," Banki said, binding the man with the irrefutable logic of an infernal contract. "It buys your exclusive services, your discretion, and your agreement to operate under my protection rather than the network's. You keep the kiln, the cellar workshop, your tools, your techniques, and your pride. Fewer people try to kill you. More people pay you what your work is worth."

Torren stared at the platinum. His thoughts raced through the rapid, desperate calculation of a man offered a lifeboat, trying frantically to determine whether the lifeboat was also a trap.

*...that's real platinum... a hundred pieces... I've never seen a hundred pieces in one place... but if I take it and the network finds out...*

Torren's fear receded by inches. Hope took its place, cautious and kiln-warm.

"The network," Banki added casually, reading the exact contour of Torren's hesitation, "is currently burning. The Choir is broken. The Outer Carry routes are compromised. The Second Desk will be dismantled before the end of the week. You are standing on the edge of a collapsing building, Torren. I am offering you the only solid ground left in the quarter."

Torren swallowed hard. He looked at the pouch, then at Banki's utterly calm, utterly certain face.

"When do I start?" Torren asked.

"You already have," Banki said. "Close the shop. We have a great deal of work to do."
