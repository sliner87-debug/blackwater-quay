This time the Sovereign Thread did not seize.

It listened.

Banki sank through hide, muscle, displacement organ, tentacle root, horn density, heart rhythm, fear loop, memory, and the place where Proto-1 had learned that handler voices were not weather but relationship. He found the corrected shape holding. Not perfect. Living things are not perfect unless they have been ruined. But stable.

Female founder.

Viable.

Distinct.

Sable read the words over his shoulder. "Do not make her a machine."

"I am making a line."

"That is how men describe machines when the parts bleed."

So Banki made the next act harder than it had to be.

Proto-2 was not a copy.

The working went deep enough to make everyone behind the glass stop speaking: bone stress, tentacle root, displacer inheritance, temperament, fear, and the bruised place where a living creature decided whether command meant trust or cage.

The Tri-Weave rose again. This time it did not form a thread. It formed a cable of industrialized High Magic, heavy with the weight of the Asmodean Pact.

Arcane. Divine. Psionic.

Law, blood, pattern.

Banki drove it through the corrected founder shape and pulled a counterpart into being: male, line-true, viable, and separate enough that his first thought was not echo but challenge.

Proto-2 struck the gate once.

Proto-1 answered by lowering her horn, and the new male stopped because her rank meant something before his pride could learn a more expensive lesson.

Darric laughed once, disbelieving and rough. "That is either the most expensive breeding pair in the world or the stupidest way anyone has ever made a problem twice."

"Both," Hesta said.

Mara's law arrived before the first breeding permission.

FOUNDER-CLASS LIVING ASSETS.

No unsanctioned breeding. No sale. No outside loan. Founder protection over production. Dual-handler minimum. Stress discipline. No proving-point exposure. Mara wrote the rules with her jaw set because every permission in the file could become a future corpse if phrased prettily enough.

"Three-month gestation," Mirell reported. "Projected yield: two to twelve young per female if the reproductive correction holds."

The room went quiet.

Numbers did that. A single monster is horror. A breeding rate is policy.

"Four pairs," Banki said eventually.

Darric closed his eyes.

Hesta said something under her breath that made Kessa pretend not to hear.

Sable's hand settled on the edge of the slate. She stood beside him not as a subordinate to be shielded, but as an absolute equal—a peer whose mind he would anchor psionically if violence erupted, ensuring she could fight for herself. "Why?"

"Because one pair is a miracle. Four pairs are a species."

"And species become history."

"Yes."

The four line-true breeding pairs came through work, not miracle: correction, refusal, handler judgment, Kestrel's stare, Mirell's cold approval, Mara's legal chain, and Sable's final look.

By the end, Direction Four had four founder pairs and no one in the room felt victorious.

Banki did.

Then he looked at Sable and made himself understand the difference between victory and permission.

The branch split followed.

Branch A took the road-shaped ones.

The Caravan Line learned weight before fear. Black-Yoke and Lantern-Rib became the lead pair because they could kneel for harness adjustment, rise cleanly under load, and keep their tentacles from fouling yoke lines even when false ambush banners snapped open beside the lane. They pulled weighted sleds through narrow turns, shouting handlers, dropped barrels, scent confusion, and a staged wheel break without turning the trial into a funeral.

Black-Yoke used one tentacle to brace a tilting wagon.

Lantern-Rib used hers to lift a fallen handler by the coat and set him behind the safe rail with the embarrassed delicacy of a siege tower trying to pick up a teacup.

Darric wrote VIABLE TEAM SET.

Hesta added: DO NOT CONFUSE VIABLE WITH CIVILISED.

The best breeding stock was protected from overwork. Internal convoy trial only. No open-road trade demonstrations. No theatre rides. No merchant touching a horn because money had made him brave.

Branch B took the forward-loaded ones.

The Military Line came from what Blackwater had already shown him: bought guards, cage rooms, workers shaking under blankets, and routes that could be cut by anyone cruel enough to understand schedules. A caravan could move people through danger. It could not make danger kneel.

Sable made him say the rest aloud before the board: he wanted more power, and he wanted more horrific monsters to stand between his people and the world.

They were not failed Caravan stock. Caravan animals were road-monarchs. WAR-LINE bodies were what happened when protection stopped asking the road to behave: shock, formation break, tunnel denial, panic forced into shape by impact. Breakers made barricades forget they were solid. Hunters ran maze lanes, pinned hostile dummies, lifted false child-shapes clear, and refused a spoofed command in Banki's voice.

Kestrel watched from the rail.

*Better. Still too eager to solve fear by reaching.*

Branch C corrected that.

The Refinement Line was quieter: mixed-age Dire Displacer Rhinos, different handlers, wrong bells, shouted orders, fake pain, real pain, hunger, boredom, and one infuriating exercise in which a handler changed coats six times to prove that scent was not authority.

Soft-Chain Mother emerged there: older, steadier, not the fastest, not the strongest, but able to make three younger beasts stop crowding a gate by shifting her weight and touching the stone once with a tentacle tip.

"That one," Kestrel said aloud.

Everyone looked at her because Kestrel rarely wasted sound.

"She teaches."

No Dire Displacer Rhino would move into serious Caravan or War service without passing Refinement. That became sovereign law before Banki could improve it into an exception.

Branch D kept the failures.

Branch D kept what could not be released, sold, bred, or forgiven. Bone, hide, gland, tentacle root, pain loop, command failure. Hesta named each dead thing before the tissue trays opened. Darric stopped joking after the third name.

Mara never visited Branch D twice.

She did not need to. Once was enough to make her write better laws.

## The Military Line

The Military Line did not begin as Banki's dream.

The dream had been Caravan Line: Black-Yoke and Lantern-Rib under harness, road-mud on their plates, tentacles bracing wagons instead of breaking men. It had been medicine delivered through ambush country and rescued children carried past predators too frightened to follow.

Then Blackwater Quay kept teaching him harder arithmetic.

The Choir had proved that a city could be invaded through song. The ledgers had proved cages could wear paperwork. Crooked guards had proved roads could become traps before the wagon left the yard. The child-shaped heat form in Direction Four had proved protection needed teeth that could refuse the wrong bite.

Roads were not enough.

The realisation came in the lower yard, not in a report.

Black-Yoke had knelt to let a small rescued girl touch his plated brow. Lantern-Rib had braced a supply wagon with one tentacle while the road team changed a cracked axle. They were exactly what Banki had wanted: road guardians, rescue-haulers, living proof that something monstrous could carry the vulnerable gently.

Then the girl flinched at a dock bell because some part of her still heard a cage door in every hard sound, and Black-Yoke turned towards the bell with all that impossible mass and no doctrine for punishing the thing that made children afraid.

Carrying them away was not enough.

Sable found Banki watching from the rail and did not ask what he was thinking. She knew. The knowledge tightened her mouth.

"You are about to turn a dream into a weapon," she said.

Kestrel, above them on the gantry, lowered her head until her throat harness clicked once against the rail. Her foreclaws bit the wood. Through the bond came no comfort, only apex assessment: a caravan beast could save a road; a war beast could make the road afraid to become a cage.

Banki needed more power. The sentence shamed nothing in him, which was why Sable kept watching.

From there, the Military Line began.

Fourteen war bodies waited behind the glass: mature violence, young violence, and three volatile exceptions that made even Hesta stand with her weight ready to move.

Captain Maela Thorn-Rusk read the board like a soldier reading weather.

"You have a war-beast corps seed," she said. "Not a corps. A seed. If you field it too early, it becomes a massacre that happens to include your enemies."

"I am aware."

"No," she said. "You are informed. Awareness comes after you watch one step over a line you thought it understood."

Sable liked her immediately.

Banki summoned the Tri-Weave.

The Military Line felt different from Caravan stock as soon as the thread touched it. Less road. More breach. Less patience. More promise of ruin. Kestrel's mind entered with his, bright-eyed and unimpressed by size alone. Null-Fang hovered beside him with its rear haft-mouth faintly open, tasting pressure.

*Bite the weak commands first,* Null-Fang murmured.

Kestrel answered in one hard thought.

*Do not make them calmer. Make them harder to fool.*

The Sovereign Thread thickened into a cable of industrialized High Magic.

Banki sent it through all fourteen, executing the Divine Calculus that measured survival against monstrous adaptation.

The Sovereign Thread found power first. Then flaw. Fury made shimmer lie more convincingly to handlers. Juvenile hunger reached before judgment. Two secondary handlers had used the same cadence under stress, and the herd had almost learned that panic could impersonate command. Several tentacles knew how to crush before they remembered how to release.

Then he triplicated the line.

Not cloning. Not copies. Fourteen became forty-two: original, counterpart, variance. The originals remained themselves except where stabilising resonance could not be avoided. The new twenty-eight carried correction in the places where correction meant survival.

He did not install a catalogue. Channeling the blistering resonance of his Screaming Skull invocation, he burned separations into instinct with marrow runes.

Hit. Halt. Wheel. Redirect. Capture without crush. Crush only when breach, siege, or active lethal threat made mercy room too narrow to stand inside. Kestrel's command sat above handler triads. False voices lost authority. Battle-noise became weather instead of law. The young kept their ferocity, but no longer mistook ferocity for permission.

And, by deliberate choice, he did not accidentally Caravanise them.

Kestrel approved.

*Good. Do not teach a war line to apologise.*

The first batch held.

Banki drove the cable deeper.

They ran drums, bells, shouting, psychic clutter, illithid domination grammar, false pain, and battlefield roar through the pens until the young stopped treating noise as law. One secondary handler panicked and gave the wrong cadence. Nothing moved. Only then did the Crown kill-halt settle into the chain: Banki, Kestrel, Sable, Command Tier, assigned triad, handlers.

Sable looked up sharply when she felt her place lock into the pattern.

"You should have asked."

"Lives are at immediate risk."

"That is an explanation. Not permission."

Banki held her gaze. Then he did the thing she had been teaching him to do since the throne room.

He made the pattern wait.

The Military Line trembled under the suspended command. Forty-two enormous bodies held on the edge of receiving doctrine because the sovereign had paused for one woman to choose whether partnership meant anything when power was already moving.

Sable's anger did not vanish.

It changed shape.

"Operational wartime command," she said. "Not ownership. Not proof of loyalty. Not a leash."

"Command in crisis. Refusal rights in misuse."

"Put that in the herd."

So he did.

The second batch installed only after Sable's correction.

What settled into them was not a list. Kestrel's discipline became the cold point every charge turned around. Tentacles learned the difference between catching, breaking, carrying, and ending. Rage cooled into tool-use. Pain became data before it became policy. Handler triads rooted the herd when one voice failed. Crown-marked allies, protected persons, surrendered captives, bonded handlers, and recovery teams became shapes the body avoided when physics left mercy any room at all.

Mirell did not declare success from the speaker. She made them watch one more drill.

A handler dropped a command-token and swore before fear could become language. One young WAR-LINE unit shifted towards him, tentacles opening. Hesta's hand hit the stop-cord. Kestrel did nothing.

The tentacles caught the token, lifted the handler by the back of his coat, and set both behind the safe rail. Then the beast returned to its lane and lowered its horn towards the hostile dummy instead.

Only then did Darric sit down on a crate as if his knees had filed an objection.

Captain Maela Thorn-Rusk removed one glove and touched the observation glass with two fingers.

"Authenticated," she said. "Not safe. Not civilised. Not moral. But authenticated."

Mara, who had arrived halfway through and had hated every minute with increasing precision, did not write a public version.

She drew two boxes on the slate.

DIRECTION FOUR: CARAVAN LINE. Rescue-haulage. Containment handling. Public-facing creature work. Black-Yoke, Lantern-Rib, Soft-Chain Mother, founder protection, nursery doctrine, road safety, handler welfare.

BLACK/PINK SECRECY SECTION: living black assets. Black wax for sovereign secrecy. Pink thread for breathing, thinking, growing biological danger. No municipal registry. No court-facing classification. No auction language. No public boast. Banki's seal. Sable's counterseal.

WAR-LINE was entering a section that had already existed for uglier reasons.

Mara wrote the older line beneath it in smaller script because even the ink seemed to dislike the words:

ILLITHID PROGRAM: BLACK-TIER. BQW-ILL-LOCKBOX. Owner-only exact counts. Defensive-line observation only. Offensive, sampling, and hibernation lines drafted, not opened. Any anomaly wakes Banki.

"If these step onto public stone," she said, touching the second box, "the city remembers the hoofprints. Witnesses. Songs. Fear. Mothers who will ask whether shelter is still shelter when it arrives with siege horns. I can count broken doors. I can replace tables. I cannot let a court leash what it cannot survive seeing."

"Accepted," Banki said.

"Do not say that like I gave you a toy."

He looked through the glass at forty-two living arguments against helplessness.

Black-Yoke and Lantern-Rib remained in the road branch. Proto-1 and Proto-2 stayed behind founder law. Soft-Chain Mother pressed one younger beast away from the gate with a tentacle tip before it could crowd the handler. The Military Line breathed behind the new seal, each one capable of making a champion rethink the shape of a street.

Banki signed the transfer.

From that moment, Direction Four continued with the Caravan Line. WAR-LINE left the visible ledger and entered BLACK/PINK.

"No," Banki said. "You gave me a future bill."

Mara closed the folder. "And I intend to collect."

Direction Four slept badly that night. BLACK/PINK did not sleep at all.

Neither did Banki.

Somewhere below the Breakfast Hall, forty-two war bodies dreamed in displacement haze, their tentacles twitching through modes they now knew how to name: catch, pin, brace, crush, carry, spare.

Project Second Bite had become more than a file.

It had become a promise with hooves.

## First Contact

The meeting looked accidental. It was not, quite.

The Guttered Lantern was neutral ground, enforced by Darva Wellspring: retired sea captain, enormous, heavily armed, and still reputed to throw troublemakers into the canal with professional velocity.

Banki arrived first. He was meeting Serren Vale—the broker from the expedition, who had returned with three verified buyers for the next auction cycle—and he had chosen the Guttered Lantern because its neutrality made it a safe venue for commercial discussions that he didn't want conducted on ShadowNet property.

He was seated at a corner table, his back to the wall. He kept the Screaming Skull form of his Eldritch Blast tightly coiled in his palm, ready to deploy its horrific High Magic at a moment's notice. His Mind-Sense was running at ambient scan, when the door opened and a man walked in who registered in Banki's telepathic field like a bonfire in a dark room.

Aldric Thorne was taller than Banki had expected, but height was not the problem. Presence was. People looked at him because his certainty entered the room before his boots finished crossing the threshold.

He wore civilian clothes, yet still looked armoured by habit: shoulders set for burden, eyes sweeping not for exits first but for need.

Behind Aldric: Maren. The bard. Not carrying her lute—she had left it at the lodging, a rare concession to discretion. She was dressed for social reconnaissance: attractive but not flashy, approachable but not vulnerable, wearing an expression of warm, open friendliness that was both genuine and strategic.

They sat three tables away from Banki.

Banki's Mind-Sense touched Aldric and met bright purpose.

Choir. Survivors. Twelve restored minds. The ones still lost. Information extracted under the Dawnfather's authority and justified by the lives it had saved.

Beneath that: the source. The deeper evil. The shadow left standing after the Choir fell.

He was thinking about the ShadowNet.

Not by name. He didn't know the name yet. He knew that the Choir's elimination had revealed, rather than resolved, the city's deeper corruption. And he was thinking about what to do next, which was the same thing he always thought about what to do next: find it, confront it, destroy it.

Banki ate his fish stew and watched his enemy's thoughts scroll past like text in a language he understood perfectly and wished he didn't.

Then something happened that Banki had not anticipated.

Maren looked at him.

Not at his table. At him. Directly. With the focused, assessing gaze of a woman who had noticed something in the room that did not fit.

Banki met her eyes. He allowed his expression to show mild, friendly curiosity—the expression of a businessman who has noticed that an attractive woman is looking at him and is not displeased by the attention but is not going to make assumptions about its meaning.

Maren smiled. It was a professional smile: warm, inviting, designed to open a conversational door without pushing the person through it.

"Excuse me," she said, rising from her table and approaching his with the easy, natural confidence of someone who initiated conversations the way other people breathed. "I'm sorry to intrude. I'm new to the city, and I couldn't help noticing that you seem... established. Would you mind if I asked you a few questions about the district? I'm trying to get my bearings."

Behind her, Aldric's surface thoughts flickered with brief, reflexive concern—not for Maren's safety, but for the possibility that the stranger she was approaching might be connected to the darkness they were investigating. His hand, beneath the table, moved towards the hilt of the sword that he was not wearing, a reflexive gesture that told Banki everything about how deeply the paladin's combat training had been integrated into his unconscious behaviour.

Banki smiled back. It was the smile of a man who had nothing to hide—which was, of course, the most dangerous kind of lie.

"Of course," he said. "Please, sit. I'm Banki. I run a salvage and auction business out of the Wharfside. The fish stew here is the best on the canal, by the way—don't let anyone tell you differently."

Maren sat, warm and apparently harmless, and the room began giving itself to her. She asked district questions with musician's timing: never too fast, never too hungry, always leaving a silence wide enough for other people to step into.

Banki gave her true things arranged into useful lies. Sablehook. Auctions. Creature work. Buyers. Nothing she could use, all of it polished enough to feel like candour. Maren smiled as if she had learned something. Banki disliked how close that was to being true.

As she stood to leave, Aldric looked over.

Their eyes met.

Paladin and Pact-Mage. Light and shadow. Three tables and a bowl of fish stew between them.

Aldric nodded. Civil. Exact. Banki nodded back.

Maren, leaving, glanced once over her shoulder and saw both men pretending the moment had been smaller than it was.

Aldric nodded. A small, civil gesture.

Banki nodded back.

Then the heroes left, and Banki finished his fish stew while the door swung shut on the first clean shape of a future conflict.

Not today.

Not tomorrow.

But soon.

## What Maren Heard

Maren Goldstring was not eavesdropping. She was listening, and a tavern was an instrument if one knew where to rest the hand.

She sat at a corner table with ale she did not intend to finish. Local, sour, useful. Her lute case leaned at her feet, making her less bard and more woman with a bad drink and nowhere urgent to be.

Three tables away, two dock workers spoke in the low careful tones of men putting a dangerous shape between them.

She kept her face bored. Interest had weight; people felt it settle. Maren lifted her cup, frowned at the ale, and let a laugh from the next table cover the slight turn of her head.

"...the salvage man's got something underground. Something big. The new builds at Sablehook—the ones they say are for 'creature storage'? The foundations go three times deeper than creature storage needs. My cousin poured concrete for the sub-level. He said there were doors down there that didn't go anywhere he was allowed to follow."

"Doors to what?"

"That's the thing. He doesn't know. They put him on the foundations and then sent him home before the interior work started. Different crew for the interior. Crew he'd never seen before. Not local. Not guild. Just... appeared one morning and disappeared every evening, and nobody on the regular crew was told where they came from or where they went."

"That's not unusual for secure builds—"

"It's not the crew that bothers me. It's the sound."

A pause. Maren's attention sharpened.

"What sound?"

"My cousin went back. After hours. He's nosy—it's a family trait. He said he stood outside the Sablehook fence at two in the morning and he heard something coming up from underground. Not construction noise. Not machinery. Something else. Something that he said sounded like..." The dock worker paused, searching for words that his vocabulary was not equipped to provide. "Like singing. But wrong. Like someone had taken a melody and turned it inside out so that the notes were going backward. He said it made his teeth hurt."

"Teeth?"

"Teeth. And his head. He said he had a headache for three days after. And he said—and this is the part that bothers me—he said he couldn't remember walking home. He remembers standing at the fence. He remembers the sound. And then he's in his bed, and it's morning, and three hours are gone."

Maren's blood ran cold.

She did not frighten easily. Frightened performers missed cues, and missed cues killed people when one travelled with a paladin who treated danger as a vocation. But this was not blade-fear. It was the coldness that came when separate scraps of knowledge found one another in the dark.

Memory loss. Sonic anomalies. Underground facilities with restricted access. Construction that went deeper than its stated purpose required.

The Choir was destroyed. Maren had been there when Aldric drove his consecrated sword through the primary hymn-stone and the resonance collapsed.

Something else used familiar tools from a deeper room: memory, sound, pressure, secrecy beneath the Choir's noise.

Banki: the salvage man with grey-green eyes, a blank aura, a curated tour, rescued children, and doors he would not open.

For one foolish breath she thought: secret cellar, evil man, simple song. Then the kitchen porter spoke, and simplicity broke.

Before she left, another voice caught her.

Not the two dock workers. A kitchen porter at the bar, sleeves dusted with flour, telling the barkeep that Sablehook paid on the day promised and that the breakfast hall had locks on the inside of the sleeping-room doors. He said it with the defensive pride of someone repeating a fact he had already been mocked for believing.

"A lock on the inside doesn't make a clean house," the barkeep said.

"No," the porter answered. "But it's the first lock I ever had that was mine."

Maren looked at him then. Truly looked.

He was not charmed. She knew charm. She knew the loose gaze, the delayed affect, the too-smooth agreement. This man was tired, frightened, and loyal in the complicated way people become loyal to roofs after years in rain. When another patron made a joke about the salvage king owning his workers, the porter touched a folded paper in his breast pocket.

"Contract says the Crown claims service, not the soul," he said. "I can read. I read it twice."

Maren's fingers tightened around her cup.

It should have been easier if they sounded enslaved.

It should have been easier if gratitude always lied.

The words reached her tongue and stopped there, ashamed of themselves. To her, it would have sounded like rescue. To him, another stranger deciding his roof was invalid because she disliked the builder.

Maren left coins, lifted the lute case, and walked into fog that tasted of brine, deception, and a story refusing to end where righteousness wanted it to.

Outside, she did not run. Running made stories. She adjusted the strap of the lute case, paused under a lamp as if deciding which way home lay, and used the reflection in a rain-dark window to see whether anyone had followed. No one had. Or no one good enough to matter had shown themselves.

That did not comfort her as much as it should have.

She needed to talk to Aldric.

She needed to talk to him tonight.

## The Quiet Loft

The Quiet Loft was the kind of place that didn't exist until you needed it to.

It occupied the upper two floors above a legitimate Chandler's Row glassblower, the sort of respectable business that made everyone on the street very careful about what respectability meant.

The upper floors were a different matter.

Banki had reconnoitered the Quiet Loft's exterior two days ago, in pixie form, hovering in Greater Shroud outside the third-floor windows at four o'clock in the morning.
