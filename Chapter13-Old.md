Standard Aether-Sight. Not Banki's Aether-Sight.

His Aura Masking Awareness upgrade cut through the damper like a blade through cloth, revealing the suppressed auras beneath: six distinct magical signatures, at least three of which were active enchantments on living beings, and the rest distributed across objects and structural elements in a pattern that suggested a workshop, a meeting room, and what his Residue Forensics identified as a client consultation space—a place where buyers came to discuss requirements, examine samples, and negotiate prices for the kind of bespoke magical crafting that the Quiet Loft's operators preferred not to advertise.

Tonight, he was going inside.

The approach was vertical. Pixie form, Greater Shroud, ascending the building's exterior wall with the adhesive grip of a creature that had evolved to cling to things that didn't want to be clung to. He reached the third floor in forty seconds and pressed himself against the stone beside a window whose illusion showed an empty room but whose magical reality showed a space occupied by two minds, three active enchantments, and the residual signatures of at least a dozen clients who had passed through in the last week.

The window's ward was sophisticated. Banki spent three minutes studying it with Aether-Sight at maximum resolution, mapping the enchantment's trigger conditions and fail-safes with the patient, meticulous attention of a locksmith examining a mechanism he intended to defeat without tripping. The ward was designed to alert the occupants if any physical object—including air, including light, including the faintest displacement of pressure—crossed the window's threshold without the proper authorisation token.

Physical objects. The ward was calibrated for physical intrusion. Not psionic.

Banki smiled the tiny, sharp smile that his pixie face wore when it was about to do something that the designers of a security system had not anticipated, and pushed his Mind-Sense through the window like a knife through water.

The two minds inside resolved immediately:

The first was an illusionist—female, middle-aged, with the particular mental texture of someone whose consciousness was permanently split between the world-as-it-was and the world-as-she-wanted-it-to-appear. Her surface thoughts were a continuous stream of enchantment maintenance: adjusting an illusion here, reinforcing a glamour there, keeping the Quiet Loft's false face intact with the constant, low-level effort of a woman who had been doing this for so long that the effort had become invisible to her.

The second was a client. Male. Wealthy—the surface thoughts dripped with the brand of entitled impatience that characterised people who had more money than time and who treated the difference as everyone else's problem. He was waiting for something. A commission. A piece of bespoke magical work that he had ordered and paid for and that was, according to his increasingly irritated internal monologue, three days late.

Banki read the client's thoughts in full. Name: Verant Chalice, a merchant from the upper ward whose public persona was "respectable importer" and whose private activities included the acquisition and resale of forged magical identification documents—the kind that allowed a person to pass through warded checkpoints, enter restricted areas, and assume identities that belonged to other people. His most recent order: a set of four identity tokens, each one attuned to a different Harbour Magistracy official, each one capable of fooling standard verification magic for approximately twelve hours.

Banki filed this information with the satisfied precision of a collector adding a particularly fine specimen to an already impressive collection. Verant Chalice was now a piece on his board—not a piece he needed to move immediately, but a piece whose position and capabilities he understood, and understanding was nine-tenths of ownership.

The illusionist's thoughts were more interesting. Her name was Solenne, and she was the Quiet Loft's operator—the last independent node of the criminal crafting ring that had once included the Rinse Below and the Dry Kiln. It was an extinction event.

She was frightened.

Banki was, in fact, coming for her next. But not the way she feared.

He studied her mind for another ten minutes, reading not just surface thoughts but the deeper structures of memory and habit that revealed the full scope of her capabilities and connections.

He did not enter the Quiet Loft that night.

He would make the offer tomorrow. Through Sable, through an intermediary, through the kind of carefully staged approach that combined carrot and stick in proportions calibrated to the specific psychology of the target. The carrot: protection, resources, a place in the ShadowNet's growing infrastructure. The stick: the demonstrated evidence that her colleagues had already been absorbed, and the implicit suggestion that absorption was preferable to the alternative.

The Quiet Loft would be his by the end of the week.

Three nodes. Three acquisitions. In the fog, the Quiet Loft's respectable window kept showing an empty room.

## The Canal Walk

She met him on the canal walk at dusk, in the hour between the day's last operations and the evening's first obligations.

Sable had spent three days knowing exactly where the warmth was and refusing to name it while anyone could use the name against her.

She chose the canal walk because neither of them owned it. No desks. No ledgers. No command boards. Just wet stone, low fog, and water carrying the city's refuse past them without asking permission.





Banki waited by the rail as if he had been part of the architecture all along.

She approached from the east at an ordinary pace and made herself arrive without checking the rooftops twice.

"You're a hard man to find," she said.

Banki turned without haste. He had heard her coming and gave no sign of surprise.

"Only for people who are looking in the wrong places," he said.

Sable looked different when she wasn't working. Her posture was still precise, still controlled, but the control was looser—a spring at rest rather than a spring compressed. Her eyes were the same. Dark, sharp with intelligence, carrying the intensity that Banki had first noticed on the Greybridge Road, when she had been a cloaked traveller counting exits and he had been a stranger who spoke inside her head.

"I want to talk about something that isn't operational," she said.

"Yes."

"Are you reading my mind right now?"

"Yes. I'm always reading. You know that."

A pause. Anger. Fascination. Attraction, suppressed so quickly it became more visible for the effort. Sable wanted in: not into his bed first, though desire had crossed the canal between them like a thrown knife, but into the power he was building and the danger of standing beside it.

"I know what you want," Banki said. "And I know that you're capable enough to be useful and smart enough to be dangerous. Those are the two qualities I look for in every person I work with."

"And which quality do you value more?"

"Dangerous," he said, without hesitation. "Useful people are common. Dangerous people are rare. And the most valuable employees in any organisation are the ones whose employer is never entirely sure whether they're an asset or a threat."

Sable's lips curved. Not a smile—a concession. The acknowledgment that the man in front of her had just described their dynamic with a precision that left no room for the kind of diplomatic ambiguity that most professional relationships relied on.

"If this is going to be what I think it's going to be," she said, "I have conditions."

"You always have conditions. State them."

"When we are together—not working, not operating, not being the ShadowNet—you do not read my mind. Not my surface thoughts, not my deep thoughts, not the thoughts I have about you when I think you aren't looking. In those moments, I am not your operative. I am not your asset. I am not a mind to be scanned. I am a person, and the person requires privacy, and the privacy is not negotiable."

Banki understood the danger. She was not asking for autonomy; she already had that, for they operated as true peers. He would never physically restrain her from fighting or jump in front of a blade meant for her, always respecting her agency. She was asking him to leave the most intimate room in her mind locked even when he knew how to open it.

Choosing her meant choosing blindness. He disliked how close that came to trust.

"Accepted," he said. "With one amendment, which you already know because you know me."

"Which is?"

"In situations where lives are at immediate risk—yours, mine, or those of people we are contractually obligated to protect under the Asmodean Pact—I reserve the right to read your surface thoughts without prior consent. Not to protect you like a child, or to pull you from a fight. I will only anchor your mind psionically as an invisible safety net so you can fight without restraint. The Divine Calculus requires you to be an autonomous peer, and the duration of my reading will not exceed the resolution of the immediate threat."

Sable weighed the amendment. She disliked it. She accepted it because she knew the difference between intimacy and stupidity.

"Accepted," she said.

Banki closed his awareness away from her.

Not the whole city. He was not suicidal. Only the part of him that had rested on Sable by habit: surface weather, intent, the little thefts of certainty that made people tolerable.

Sable vanished from that map.

She remained in front of him: dark eyes, scar at the shoulder beneath the edge of her collar, hands still, body balanced. But the inner door was closed, and for the first time since Greybridge he did not know what she was thinking.

The absence was not emptiness.

It was vertigo.

"What am I thinking?" she asked.

"I don't know."

Her expression changed by almost nothing.

"Say it again."

"I don't know."

The words cost him more the second time.

Sable stepped closer, close enough that he could have read the heat of her body if not the shape of her mind. "That is the point."

"Blindness is an inefficient foundation for intimacy."

"No," she said. "Certainty is an efficient foundation for ownership. Intimacy starts where certainty stops."

Banki considered that and found, with irritation, that it was probably true.

She took his hand then. Not as operative. Not as subordinate. Not as asset or blade or shadow. As a woman choosing contact while keeping the rest of herself unentered.

He held still.

It was the hardest thing he had done all day.

"Now," Sable said, "we can walk."

When she let go, the agreement remained.

"That is the door," Sable said. "The rest, we decide when we reach it."

They walked until the fog took the canal from both sides.

Sable did not return to Sablehook after.

She went west first, through three alleys, over a cooper's roof, down the back stair of a shuttered lace shop, and out through a lane that smelled of rainwater, onions, and old ash. No one followed. She checked anyway because survival was not an emotion but a discipline, and disciplines did not relax because the heart had become inconvenient.

The house stood beyond the city skin, where Blackwater's warehouses thinned into salt grass and low stone walls. It was smaller than memory and crueler for it. A cottage with a sagging roof, a back garden gone to nettle, a well covered by a cracked lid, and a loose shutter on the east window that knocked twice whenever the wind came off the water.

It had been loose when she was six.

Sable stood before it with the key in her hand and did not think about Banki.

She thought about a woman singing while she kneaded dough. A man's hands lifting her to mark her height on the pantry frame. Rain on the roof. A blue cup she had broken and hidden under the hearth because she had believed broken things could be saved by hiding them where heat lived. She thought about the first place she had ever belonged before belonging became dangerous.

Then she went inside and repaired the shutter herself.

The work took twenty minutes. Wedge. Peg. New leather strip. Two nails driven at an angle because the frame had warped and because old houses, like old wounds, refused straight solutions. When the shutter finally held, the silence in the room changed. It no longer sounded abandoned. It sounded waiting.

In the kitchen, under a loose floorboard, lay the letter.

It had been there since spring, folded into oilcloth. The handwriting on the outside used a name she no longer answered to. Inside, her cousin had written three pages of apology, accusation, family news, and a closing line that hurt because it was ordinary:

Come home if there is still a home in you.

Sable read it once.

Then she lit the stove and burned it, all except the corner with the name. That she kept. Not as regret. As evidence.

At dawn she met S3 in the yard behind the cottage.

The girl had come early, as instructed. Too early. She stood with her back to the wall, weight balanced for flight, knife positioned where a frightened child thought a knife should be positioned: visible enough to warn the world, not hidden enough to win.

It irritated Sable because it was almost exactly how she herself had stood at thirteen.

"If you show me the knife," Sable said, "you are asking me to solve the knife. If you hide the knife, you ask me to solve you. Which problem do you want an enemy working on?"

The girl moved the knife.

"Better. Again."

They trained for an hour. Not violence first. Exits first. Shoes. Food. Witnesses. Doors that opened outward. Windows painted shut. Rain barrels that could hold a body under the lip. The difference between silence and stillness. The use of fear as weather rather than master.

"Does fear go away?" S3 asked near the end, sweat at her temples, pride trying not to show.

"No," Sable said. "It learns manners."

When Sable returned to Blackwater that evening, she stopped on a roof across from Sablehook and watched the compound breathe.

Refuge, she thought.

Then, because she was honest with herself in ways that had saved her life more than once:

Prison.

Both answers stood. Banki was building a house for the hunted and a machine for his will, governed entirely by the Divine Calculus and the Asmodean Pact. He would protect His with a ferocity no lawful lord in Blackwater would ever spend on the small, the bought, the broken, or the inconvenient, because the Pact demanded value for value. He would also name the walls protection until someone made him look at the locks.

Sable could leave.

Instead she watched the kitchen lamps come on one by one and thought, with deep irritation, that love was easier to survive when it chose someone less useful.

Then she climbed down and went back to work.

## Open Court

Mara had learned to love rooms that underestimated paper.

The Hall of the Crown's Justice was not one of them. The Hall understood paper perfectly. It had been built by people who believed law was a blade that needed a ceremonial handle so the public would stop looking at the edge. Time bent inside it. Testimony could take a week and spend a day. Evidence could be weighed until liars ran out of breath and advocates ran out of elegant ways to pretend a corpse was a misunderstanding.

Mara entered with three ledgers, two stamped bonds, one emergency writ, and no patience left for men who thought legality was a language only nobles spoke fluently.

Banki watched from the observation gallery. She knew without looking. His attention had weight, even through glass. Sable stood somewhere near him. Tally had the public books ready. Beneath the city, Sable's private teams were moving through tunnels while the public court dismantled the houses that had fed the Choir.

House Velmaren fell first.

The evidence was not dramatic because truth rarely has the manners to arrange itself for theatre. It arrived as receipts, transfer marks, custody lists, cellar diagrams, medical purchases, and testimony from people whose voices shook but did not break. Kidnapping. Slavery. Murder. Espionage against the Crown.

Lord Velmaren was not present. Sable had predicted flight and blocked the tunnel before proceedings began. Mara let the advocate object to the absence for exactly forty-seven seconds before placing the interception record on the table.

"My lord may answer in person when he finishes attempting to escape jurisdiction," she said.

The chamber laughed once, sharply, then remembered the dead and stopped.

House Rennic broke next. Then Hadrake. Merrow. Orseth. Pellor. Veys. Not destroyed. Mara knew Banki's preference for assets too well to pretend otherwise. They were seized, supervised, restructured, fined, marked, and made useful under law they had once purchased by the yard.

Then Velmaren's surviving advocate changed the battlefield.

He rose with a second writ in hand and a smile polished by generations of expensive meals.

"Before this court accepts further accusation from Sablehook-associated witnesses, we ask the Crown to address a matter of immediate civic danger. Does Sablehook intend to maintain unregistered war-beasts in a civilian district under the fiction of household protection?"

The word war-beasts moved through the chamber like dropped oil finding flame.

Mara felt Banki reach for three answers from behind the glass: blackmail, intimidation, and that cold little smile that made people decide they had urgent business elsewhere.

None of those answers could save them here.

If Banki threatened the court, Sablehook became a fortress. If he denied every creature, discovery would make every Crown Citizen look like a hostage. If he pretended the Caravan Line was only private property, the city could seize its visible animals, kill useful stock, or declare the Breakfast Hall an unlawful armed encampment and drag children into the argument.

WAR-LINE was already gone from the public ledger. BLACK/PINK held that secret behind Banki's seal and Sable's counterseal. Direction Four, in this room, meant the Caravan Line, rescue-haulage, containment handling, and the creatures Sablehook could admit without handing enemies a map of its throat.

Mara stepped forward before Banki could spend power in a room built to tax it.

"Sablehook claims no private army," she said.

The advocate's smile widened.

"It claims public-facing Crown rescue and haulage assets under emergency household charter, district disaster practice, and voluntary civic damage bond."

The smile died uncertainly, which was Mara's favourite kind of death.

She laid the first bond on the witness table. Public copy. Stamped. Witnessed. Dull enough that half the chamber underestimated it and precise enough that the other half went pale.

"If a Sablehook asset breaks a bridge, Sablehook repairs the bridge before auction profit is distributed. If a handler injures a citizen, Sablehook pays medical cost before discretionary security expenditure. If Crown-protected people are harmed by Crown force, their claim receives priority hearing in our own books and public notice of payment."

"And who reviews the king?" the advocate asked.

Mara placed the second bond beside the first.

"The people he owes."

Then she named the amount.

The chamber made the softest possible sound: wealthy people discovering that someone else's paperwork had teeth.

The number was obscene. It was also real. Backed by Sablehook accounts, auction receivables, seized Choir assets, and three revenue streams Banki had intended to keep flexible. Once read into Open Court, it could not be unspoken. It did not make WAR-LINE visible. It made the Caravan Line answerable, repairs superior to profit, and civilian harm a public debt rather than a private embarrassment.

Mara had spent one of Banki's options to save his people.

The irreversible cost was not coin. Coin was the smallest blood in Banki's body. The cost was reputation, expectation, and witnesses. The hidden kingdom now had a public promise, and enemies with ink could make that promise expensive.

Behind the glass, Banki went very still.

Good, Mara thought. Feel it.

"This bond also states what Crown Citizenship already promises," she continued. "No loyal citizen may be silenced, memory-altered, oath-punished, or contractually threatened for reporting harm caused by Crown force. No child under Crown protection may be used as leverage in civil defence. No protected worker may be turned into a shield and then called grateful."

Now the chamber looked at Sablehook differently.

The chamber did not see Sablehook as safe.

It saw something worse for Sablehook's enemies: answerable.

The advocate recovered enough to sneer. "You expect this court to trust monsters because you have made them expensive?"

Mara looked at him then, really looked, and let every hour of sleep she had lost arranging witness protection, wages, custody clauses, handler exits, food stores, and resurrection language sit in her face.

"No," she said. "I expect this city to remember damage with names, prices, and witnesses."

Silence held.

Then the presiding authority accepted the bond as public undertaking, not jurisdiction.

Mara did not look up at the observation gallery. She did not need to see Banki's face to know the shape of his anger, or the shape underneath it: recognition. He could have won the room's fear. He could not have won its permission. Not like this.

Sable's slate arrived through a runner three minutes later.

ACCEPTABLE. ALSO, NEVER USE THE PHRASE "MILITARY ANIMALS" IN A PUBLIC DOCUMENT. BLACK/PINK HOLDS.

Mara allowed herself one breath that was almost a laugh.

Then she returned to the evidence table and continued taking noble houses apart.

In the corridor outside, Mara put one hand flat against the wall and let herself shake exactly once. Then she went back in before the tremor could become anyone's leverage.

Outside the Hall, the city was learning what Sablehook would pay when protection broke something. Under Sablehook, Direction Four continued teaching the Caravan Line to carry weight without becoming war. Deeper than that, behind a seal the court had not seen and would not survive naming, BLACK/PINK kept the WAR-LINE breathing.

A kingdom was bread, contracts, roadways, custody rooms, grave records, forge priorities, handler shifts, medical stores, and the terrible question of what force was allowed to do in the name of care.

For the first time, Banki's answer had witnesses he could not fully control.

The Belowmarket was waiting.

## The Red Shelves

Dawn came to Camp Eight without kindness.

Morning came grey and hard between the Brinewood and Shattercliff. The fires breathed under kettle soot, and the camp smelled of leather, rhino musk, axle grease, old blood, and caged creatures kept alive for profit.

Banki stood beside the map crate with coffee in one gloved hand, dressed for travel over hidden armour, knives tucked where a quick morning might require them.

Kestrel came up the slope through the whitening mist with Rinna and Bram behind her. The two human scouts carried the morning on their boots: mud to the calf, dust over that, bramble scratches. Kestrel carried the same route on her body differently: shale dust along the foreclaws, one thorn caught in the harness seam, fog beading on the optics at her throat, tail held low to keep from speaking before she did. Rinna's hood smelled of stone-damp and juniper. Bram had a strip of lichen caught on one shoulder and seemed not to know it.

“The ridges open in two miles,” Kestrel said without preamble. “After that the country breaks. More shelves. More dry cuts. Glass pockets if the sun keeps. One old wash where something big has been feeding.”

“How big?”

“Too big to be worth guessing from spoor alone,” Rinna said. Her voice had that hill-country scrape which made every sentence sound as if it had been sharpened before use. “Heavy drag marks. Shell shards. Lick-stones torn up. Not dragon. Not rhino. Cleverer than a bear and uglier than a magistrate.”

“That does narrow the field,” Banki said.

Bram rubbed sleep grit from one eye with the back of his wrist. “Also found signs on the west edge. Sapients. Two, maybe three. Came close enough to count wheels and then lost their nerve when they saw the outer pickets moving.”

“They did not lose their nerve,” Vesper said as she came in from the opposite angle, bow unstrung but never truly at rest. “They were measuring nerve. There is a difference. I found where one lay flat behind a deadfall. Good discipline. Cheap boot grease. Ink stain on the cuff. Not road thieves.”

Banki sipped the bitter coffee and let his always-open lattice run thin through the camp and outward into the trees, tasting residue, intention, the stale ghost-rime of thoughts already departed. Minds left marks when they came close enough, especially frightened minds. They were wrong. Fear left a greasy tang. Calculation left edges. Lies left a sweetness that curdled the instant one bit into it.

“There were three,” he said at last. “One lay under the deadfall. One by the washstone with the chipped white vein. The third never came within bowshot and hated himself for it.”

Bram blinked. “You can tell that?”

“I can tell enough.”

Kestrel's jaw angled by a tooth-width. The speaking charm set into her throat harness clicked once before her mouth shaped words a human throat would have made softer.

"He reads fear," she said. The voice came out low, rough, and exact, a translation fitted around a predator's mouth. "I read weight. The third watcher favoured his left knee, carried oiled steel, and counted handlers before wagons. Someone pays him for numbers, not courage. He reports to a patient mind. Patient minds are more edible when hurried."

Bram stopped rubbing his eye.

The camp had learned many things about Kestrel since Banki brought her into the house. The most important was that her intelligence was not trained-animal cleverness. It was altered, sharpened, bonded, and armed. She understood route discipline, command hierarchy, fear-sign, profit, insult, and the exact social usefulness of letting humans remember that a creature with foreclaws could also count.

Banki inclined his head by the smallest degree. "Agreed."

He set down the cup. “We take the day in layers. First, secure movement. Second, harvest. Third, reputation. This venture is about business, and business is only murder that learned to file papers. Tally holds the paper line. Gravewire holds the ring. Kessa keeps the cages from teaching one another bad habits. Vesper, I want the watchers taken if they offer it to us. Cleanly. They breathe if possible.”

“Memory work after?” she asked.

“Yes. Same doctrine as the last pair. Thirty wagons. Predator sign. Fear. Distance. Confusion. Nothing clever. Clever lies catch on their own bones.”

The heads meeting broke. Orders moved down the line; rhinos came into harness, pins were checked, cages inspected, and Sablehook's people settled into the practised haste of dangerous work.

Banki took the forward team when the road split into stone shelves and dry cuts.

The land changed before Shattercliff showed its face. Reed gave way to hard grasses, the soil thinned, mineral veins shone in clefts, and the wind cut clean where the marsh road had dragged.

Near midmorning they found the drag marks Rinna had named.

The kill-site lay in a split basin where shale had collapsed into an old runoff bowl. White shell fragments littered the stone, some cracked open by impact, some peeled in long curling tongues as if something patient and strong had gnawed them empty. Blood had dried dark in the shade of a leaning slab. At the basin lip sat a stain of iridescent spit which caught the light with a sick rainbow skin.

Rinna crouched beside it. “Feeder,” she said. “Big one. Mature.”

“Can it climb?” asked Hark.

“Everything worth fearing climbs.”

That proved prophetic sooner than anyone liked.

The thing came not from the basin but from above it, where the rock face stepped back in a ragged overhang veiled by thorn brush and winter-grey ivy. One moment the ledge was ledge. The next it erupted.

The beast had once been some blunt cousin of monitor lizard or cave scavenger, before time and magic grew mineral plates through its hide and gave its combed tongue a blind hunger for the air.

Hark shouted. Vesper was already moving.

Banki opened his hand and spoiled the creature's balance. It hit the shale shoulder-first while Vesper's net dropped over the head and foreclaws.

It was too large for a clean live-taking by force alone.

Banki reached into the beast's furious thought-loom and sharpened the pain in its shoulder until it convulsed. Vesper drove a second net down, Hark got the muzzle-loop over the jaw, and Gravewire came in with chain.

Then the creature found enough of itself to become terrible.

It heaved.

Shale exploded. One of the translucent tongue-combs whipped across Hark’s forearm and stripped skin in three wet ribbons to the muscle. Blood fell bright and immediate. The beast rolled, dragging two people with it, and slammed Gravewire hip-first into the basin wall. The sound that came from him had too much air in it. Banki answered with the industrialized, horrific power of High Magic, his eldritch blast taking the Screaming Skull form.

His blast, a screaming bolt of bone-dust and marrow runes, struck like contempt given structure. The mineral plates along the beast's neck rang, and the flesh beneath them forgot its proper arrangement.

The feeder lurched. Vesper put an arrow through the softer hinge inside its rear leg. Kestrel, who had come running hard from the ridge above at the first shout, dropped from a boulder with both harness knives reversed and buried one to the guard in the seam where mineral growth had split away from muscle behind the jaw. Hot black blood sprayed across her chest harness and stippled the stone with thick coin-shaped drops. The smell of it came in a mineral reek like brine, copper, and struck flint.

“Now!” Banki snapped.

Gravewire, one knee barely working, got the chain around the forelimbs. Hark rammed the muzzle pole crosswise through the torn net. Banki’s telekinetic grip turned from sabotage to compression. He seized four points at once: lower jaw, left forelimb, right hind limb, base of tail. The beast’s body bowed under invisible force. Bones did not like being argued with from four directions. Something cracked. Then something else. The feeder screamed at last, a noise like slate split under winter weight.

“Keep it alive,” Kessa shouted from behind them, because Kessa could look at horror and still remember margins.

“It is alive,” Banki said.

“Badly.”

“That also counts.”

They got it by bleeding, swearing, chain, leverage, and refusal. When the feeder finally lay trussed under canvas and reinforced muzzle, Hark bound his torn arm and Kestrel wiped black blood from her harness knives and the outer curve of one foreclaw.

Banki knelt by the trussed head.

The beast’s mind came at him in pain-spasms now, hunger briefly humbled by injury. Good. Pain was clarity for such things. He tasted its memory-scraps: nests in warm rock, blind hatchlings, the salt taste of shattered shelled prey, flashes of men once taken near an old stair road, one memory of fire that had taught caution without wisdom.

“Unique enough,” he murmured.

Rinna looked over. “What?”

“It remembers steps.”

That turned all their faces a little.

“Old steps?” Bram asked.

“Older than you. New enough to have been used by men in the last season.” He rose. “We mark the place. We do not follow it today.”

“Because it’s a trap?” said Vesper.

“Because everything is a trap,” Banki said. “That one is simply more expensive than the day currently warrants.”

The feeder was lashed to a drag-frame and hauled back to the wagons over the complaints of stone and wounded pride. Before noon, they took the watchers as well.

Vesper found the first by his bladder.

The first watcher had chosen a split of rock badly and endured too much morning coffee. Vesper waited until necessity drove him from cover, then pinned him before he could invent dignity.

The second was better and almost escaped. He broke from scrub at the edge of the drag-line and ran downhill with the low desperate speed of a man who knows he has already waited one breath too long. Kestrel flicked a weighted harness blade into his knee that opened the tendon without taking the leg. He went over in a roll, came up with a knife, and found Banki already in his mind.
