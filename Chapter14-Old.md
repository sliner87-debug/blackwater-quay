Thought is not a locked room when one has the right tools.

The runner’s skull yielded in layers. First fear. Then orders. Count wagons. Count handlers. See whether there is isolation. See whether the story about Blackwater papers is true. Report to a man with lamp soot under the nails and too-clean teeth. Phrase for priority routing: Mirrorhouse sings. Drop if marked or followed. If frightened, do not improvise.

Banki did not take everything. A mind stripped bare became a warning to anyone competent enough to examine it. He took what mattered and left the architecture standing. Then he laid other beams across it.

You saw thirty wagons.

You saw predator sign the size of a gate.

You saw shadows moving where shadows should not move.

Behind her, the confessional stood empty, its curtain half-drawn.

You ran because the thing in the trees had no eyes and knew your name.

The false memory went in easy because fear is hospitable to ugliness. He gave a matched version to the first watcher as well. Then he had the pair escorted miles off line and released separately.

By afternoon the road itself began to understand who travelled it.

By afternoon they reached a sour waystation: sheds, cookfires, tack menders, one clerk, and enough listening strangers to make rumour useful.

Tally laid out the permits one by one, callused fingers careful on the vellum.

Primary. Auxiliary return train. Three-spoke wheel crest. Lower-spoke nick. Good pressure. Dark even ink. Nothing invented in the fees.

The clerk’s pulse changed when he understood the papers were real.

“It’s all in order,” he said.

“Of course it is,” Tally replied.

Banki let the room watch him smile.

“We are building something regular,” he said to no one and everyone. “Regular things live longer. Tell anyone with a legal appetite and sufficient means that Sablehook will be receiving verified interest.”

“Verified for what?” someone asked from the benches.

He let the pause answer before the words did.

“Specialty salvage,” he said. “Regulated acquisitions. The sort of things that improve in value when fools are kept thirty feet away from them.”

It was not a threat. Threats announce themselves too proudly. It was a fact laid on the table like a clean knife.

By the time they rolled out again, the name had gone ahead of them in three different mouths wearing three different versions of the truth. Enough.

Towards evening the country turned redder and steeper. In a shallow cave behind weather-whitened thorn, Banki found the remains of another feeder.

He found not a feeder but the remnant of one.

The corpse lay in the mouth of a shallow cave half-hidden behind a drape of weather-whitened thorn. Something bigger had opened it from throat to vent and eaten only the inner lengths, leaving muscle, shell ridges, and most of the head. Not waste. Selection. The kill had been made recently enough that the blood still held a bruise-dark shine in the deeper places.

He crouched there long enough to know he was not alone.

The spell he used then was drawn from industrialized, horrific High Magic. He crushed a vial of human bone-dust to trace marrow runes in the air. Greater Shroud slid over him like a change in weather. He diminished to pixie truth and left the ground altogether, rising in silence to the level of the cliff face where a crevice ran back black and narrow.

Inside waited the thing that had made the kill.

It was no lizard, wolf, or bat, but one of those bright misfortunes where beast and violence share a skeleton. Its mind tasted of pain, escape, hunger, and cages.

Banki tasted its mind.

Pain. Escape. Hunger. The remembered shape of cages.

Some creatures are more valuable for what has been done to them than for what they are. Buyers love a clean specimen. Empires are built on damaged ones.

When it sprang, it sprang without sound.

The cave mouth flashed with claws and glass-thread whiskers.

It recovered instantly, which told him all he needed to know about its sale potential.

The fight was fast, close, and ugly. Claws opened his sleeve, glass whiskers rang against his marrow-rune wards, and Banki finally channeled a bolt of industrialized High Magic, driving a bone-dust stunning pulse through the creature until it folded into a valuable heap.

The beast snarled even in stupor.

“Good,” Banki said. Blood ran warm down the back of his hand from a cut he had not yet noticed. “I dislike merchandise that surrenders early.”

Night found them with two new high-value problems, three more canisters of harvested cliff-light dust, a packet of suspect papers, a frightened sapient captive with soot under his nails, and the whole train arranged in a ring of competence under the hard glitter of Shattercliff stars.

When the captive would not answer politely, Banki entered his head and found the words there instead.

Mirrorhouse. Nella Quist. Tidepin Steps. Hollow reed post. Twice a day when Mirrorhouse sings. Count wagons. Confirm isolation. Confirm named.

The city was reaching towards them from very far behind.

Banki smiled into the dark.

Better than silence.

Work improved when it was answered.

## The House With Teeth

The return to Blackwater Quay began before the road had quite finished spitting them out.

By the time the wagons left Shattercliff, their load had changed the expedition's pitch: captured feeders, cliff-light dust, suspect papers, and one frightened watcher carrying words from Mirrorhouse.

They were no longer a hunting party on the road.

They were inventory in motion.

Which meant the road had started hunting them back.

More eyes followed after the waystation. Banki answered with small, repeatable lessons until the road began carrying his preferred version of the truth.

Silencing every witness was amateur work and left a quiet people wished to investigate. Better to leave witnesses who believed they had survived understanding. Better to let fear edit the account before rumour carried it onward.

The world kept offering small choices. Banki kept turning them into habits that would one day pass for inevitability.

He learned, on that last leg of the road, how a name could travel ahead of itself and return larger. At a charcoal-burner’s lean camp halfway between the broken shelves and the first swamp-low lanes, a woman with hands blackened to the wrist squinted at the wagons and said, not to him but near enough, “That the Sablehook lot? Heard they pay fair and keep their papers cleaner than bishops keep their bastards.”

Banki turned his head just enough for her to see the profile of the man’s face he wore that day. “We try not to be worse than clergy without charging extra.”

She laughed despite herself.

They reached Blackwater after dusk, dragging mud, blood, new specimens, and rumours large enough to arrive before them.

Sable met them in the Sablehook yard with four full Shadows, two probationers, city backups, and the expression she wore when something had been solved without becoming simple.

She had expanded in his absence. Not visibly; Sable did not waste motion on display. But the yard moved through her before it moved at all. A lifted finger opened the intake lane. A glance sent probationers towards the outer wall. The full Shadows watched her more than they watched Banki, which meant she had spent the intervening hours teaching them where authority actually lived when he was away.

“You took your time,” she said.

“We brought things worth the minutes.”

Her gaze moved once over the new cages and stopped where the canvas-covered feeder groaned against its chains. “That one?”

“Unique enough to require better adjectives before sale.”

“Good.”

The rest of the exchange was pure Sablehook. No welcome worth the name. Only sequence.

Lights were hooded, the outer gate barred, the intake lane opened, and Sablehook moved from greeting into sequence.

Banki loved sequence almost as much as violence. Tonight, sequence had teeth.

They worked until the deep hours.

Banki walked the entire intake: the water tank, the chained feeder, the glass-whisker capture, the jars, the ledgers, and every handler whose fear had not yet learned discipline.

Darric arrived before dawn, long after any reasonable man would have stayed in his own bed, travel mud on his coat and scholarly greed bright in his eyes.

“You paid me five hundred platinum to come by dawn,” he said as soon as he was inside the owner’s lane. “I came before I could misbehave and spend it.”

“I respect initiative.”

Darric looked past him through the screened viewing line and his pupils widened with something perilously close to religious feeling. “You lunatic.”

“Professionally.”

Darric's privilege had rules: owner-lane only, no touching, no unsupervised viewing, no notes leaving unless Banki approved them.

So Banki walked him through it.

Banki walked him through the inventory by rank and risk: Named, Unique, Rare, Common, jar by jar and pen by pen, until even Darric's appetite developed a limp.

“This one,” Darric whispered before the chained glass-whisker capture, “has broken three cages in its life.”

“Only if it survives to be sold badly,” Banki said.

Darric turned to look at him. “You don’t collect creatures. You collect arguments. Each of these is an argument waiting for a buyer foolish enough to believe he understands it.”

“That,” said Banki, “is the most flattering thing anyone has said to me in days.”

They audited the jars until even Darric’s enthusiasm developed a limp.

The settled count came to four hundred eighty jars, a kingdom in glass. Money, risk, and reputation had all become visible.

Darric wrote three provisional categories on the slate and then crossed out two of them with the offended air of a scholar betrayed by inadequate language. Hesta called the entire exercise "counting knives by which one shines prettiest." Sable let both of them speak because useful people sometimes needed to exhaust their vanity before becoming precise.

At some point in those hours, Sablehook stopped feeling temporary.

When the owner's lane had been closed and Darric finally led away under escort with his senses overfed, Banki found Sable at the ledger table with Lysa Varn on one side of her and Hesta Bramblewake on the other.

The table had changed while he was on the road. It was still wood, still scarred, still too practical to be called handsome, but Sable had turned it into a nerve centre. Intake slates to the left. Payment books to the right. Conditional hires in the middle, weighted by a dagger no one had been foolish enough to touch.

A second slate waited under the intake table: south canal rumours, missing children, and Sable's note in the margin. Do not let Banki see this until exits are prepared.

Lysa, oddities vendor and soon-to-be adjacent-market acquisition, carried hidden coin pockets and the wary poise of someone who knew profit could bite.

Hesta looked up from the stop-condition slate as Banki approached. “Your people keep wanting things to love them.”

“They keep wanting not to be killed,” he corrected.

“Same disease with better boots.” She tapped the slate. “For breeding programmes, you stop when the eyes change wrong, the appetite changes too fast, the mothers cease to respond to their own young, or the handlers start saying phrases like ‘it knows me’ in that tone men use when their funerals are only waiting for weather.”

“Noted.”

“And if anybody suggests hand-rearing anything whose mother can still bite, I want them slapped with a shovel.”

“Also noted.”

Lysa slid her own contract across. “Adjacent market placement, twenty-four hour protection during onboarding, first-right on brineglass oddities, and a stall once the outer courtyard exists.”

“It will exist,” Sable said.

“Then I will pretend that comforts me.”

Banki put his seal where it belonged. Another line folded into the house. Another human hinge.

By dawn, Sablehook had become something larger than a holding cell with aspirations: pens, doctrine, consultants, frontage, buyers, and a name bold enough to say aloud.

A house with pens instead of windows.

A market with a throat full of teeth.

Blackwater had room for such things. It had been waiting, perhaps, for one to grow bold enough to say its own name aloud.

Banki intended to oblige it.

Tovin Vale liked the pantry because it was the only room in Sablehook that smelled of nothing but flour and cold stone. The rest of the house smelled of wagons, damp leather, and the sweetish, horrific sting of industrialized High Magic—the stench of marrow runes and bone-dust—that made his teeth tick when the handlers walked past with the containment nets.

He sat on a low three-legged stool near the flour bin, a basket of bent spoons between his shoes.

Most people threw bent things away. In the Belowmarket, where he had lived until his mother stopped breathing and the coal-master decided a narrow-shouldered boy wasn't worth the price of his salt, anything that broke was garbage. If a spade snapped, it went into the canal. If a boy coughed twice before the morning shift, he was left on the wharf to see if his lungs could clean themselves.

But Branna’s kitchen was different.

Tovin picked up a silver-plated spoon. The bowl was pressed flat on one side, crescent-dented as though a heavy heel had stepped on it during a rush. He turned it in his fingers, his thumb tracing the smooth curve of the silver where the base metal showed through in grey streaks.

*Metal remembers force,* Kestrel had told him when she was adjusting the leather on her training harness. *If you bend a buckle too fast, it snaps. If you work it slow, the steel stays strong. It remembers the pull.*

Tovin liked the idea that useful things could survive being bent.

He took his screwdriver—the one Tally had procured for him, with the polished ash handle that fit his hand instead of the giant iron tool he had been stealing from the cooperage—and pressed the flat head against the crescent dent. He worked the wood slowly, using his knee for leverage, pressing the silver back into the shape of a bowl. His shoulder blade clicked.

“A spoon is for stirring,” he whispered to the flour barrel. “If it still holds grease, it isn't ruined. It's just a different shape.”

The kitchen gate rattled. Outside, the breakfast hall was empty, but he could hear the distant, rhythmic clump of Darric’s handlers leading the rhino teams out to the north lane. Tally was in the office, his pen scratching against the parchment like a mouse in the oats.

Sablehook had rules. That was why Tovin liked it.

On the streets, if a man with a knife came toward you, you ran because there was no ledger to tell him he couldn't take your boots. In Sablehook, Banki’s law was written down in columns. A child who had eaten the biscuit belonged to the house. No one could buy him, no one could carry him to the coal-pits, and if a handler looked at him with the grey, hungry eyes of the Belowmarket sellers, Tovin only had to point at the door-post to make them look away.

He had tested the pantry latch six times that morning. It was a good latch, clean iron, grease-soothed. He had written the note under the shelf: *PANTRY LATCH OPENS FROM INSIDE.* He had written it because if the door ever shut and the handlers forgot where the key was, he wanted the child inside to know he could still get out.

He looked back at the spoon. The dent was gone, mostly. It wasn't perfect—the silver was scarred where the heel had bitten—but it would stir the porridge. It would carry the grease.

He set it in the “useful” pile on his right.

A shadow shifted under the bottom shelf.

Tovin did not flinch. He was accustomed to shadows in the pantry; the grease-charms sometimes made the corners flicker when the heat-stones cooled. But this shadow did not stay in the corner. It crept forward across the damp flagstones, a thin, oily smear of black water that smelled of salt-sunk silt and deep copper.

The air turned cold.

Tovin's fingers went to the pocket of his jerkin, where he had tucked half of Branna's morning biscuit. The crumb felt warm against his thigh, the faint soul-address Banki had stitched into the flour humming with a quiet, comfortable vibration.

“I am here,” Tovin whispered, his voice small against the stone. “Tovin Vale. I am sorted.”

The shadow touched his boot.

He expected pain—the hot bite of a coal-whip or the cold pinch of iron filings. Instead, there was only a wet, heavy weight that filled his mind with the sound of a thousand voices singing in the dark. A chorus with no individuals.

He dropped the spoon. It hit the flagstones with a dry, silver click.

He reached for the pantry latch, but his hand refused the iron. The fingers wouldn't close. They had forgotten how to make a fist.

*The metal remembers,* the chorus sang in his head, using his own voice to build the notes. *It does not hurt if it still stirs.*

Tovin Vale did not scream. He only looked at the empty basket between his shoes and wondered who would sort the rest of the spoons by usefulness now that his screwdriver was on the floor.

## The Belowmarket

The Black Stair descended into the kind of darkness that had opinions.

Not clean passage-dark, not warehouse-dark, not cellar-dark. This was engineered darkness, institutional and sour.

The air tasted of iron, salt, and stale fear. Banki knew the difference between a threatened mind and one that had learned surrender.

Banki descended the stairs with Mirell at his side, Jeren and FateShear controlling the room's angles behind them, and Kestrel somewhere below in the deeper darkness, holding the saltward flank so that nothing—and no one—could escape through the tunnels that connected the Belowmarket to the canal network and ultimately to the sea.

His Mind-Sense swept the space below. Guards, operators, clerks, and captives registered in layers.

There were guards: professional, cold, accustomed to procedure. Men who knew how far a chain could tighten before breathing became expensive.

There were operators and clerks whose efficient thoughts made the place worse, not cleaner.

And there were the captives.

Banki had read thousands of minds. The children's quiet nearly broke his concentration.

The first thing he noticed was not fear.

It was waste.

Not moral waste. Structural waste. Growth interrupted before it could become height. Muscle mass thinning at the wrist. Chemical compliance slowing the reflex arc. Eyes too large in faces that had learned not to ask the room for anything. A child is a future with a pulse, and someone below had taken futures apart for inventory.

Then the quiet reached him.

They were not screaming. They were past screaming. The children's minds had retreated into the final defensible rooms of themselves, places so small that even hope had to crouch to fit.

Banki stood on the Black Stair and felt Cell Nine open.

The observation window. The restraint light. The bite-block. The cold silver scale that had weighed a three-inch body as if value and mass were the same question. Aureth Voss's patient voice, explaining preservation rules while Banki's own hands shook against straps made by people who had called him specimen because the word child would have indicted them.

This was not abstract law. It was not noble rescue. It was the Thessalan Consortium wearing another city's face.

The children below had not chosen their cages any more than Banki had chosen his.

He knew the shape of that helplessness in his bones.

He had escaped. He had burned the Consortium to the ground and walked out through the ashes and had spent every year since then ensuring that he would never be caged again.

These children were still in the cage.

The cage would end.

Not reform. Not redirection. Not a profitable rearrangement of vice into policy.

End.

"Mirell," he said. "Full suppression. Every guard. Every operator. Every conscious mind in this facility that is not a captive."

Mirell's tentacles unfurled, precise and silent.

Mirell's pulse was not theatrical. It was a keyed suppression wave that struck only the hostile minds below.

Every guard in the Belowmarket collapsed.

Every clerk. Every administrator. Every thug, every lookout, every person whose mind held the greasy comfort of repeated complicity.

Twenty-three perpetrators fell unconscious, alive, and available.

The captives were untouched. Mirell's targeting was precise enough to pass over terror without mistaking it for guilt. Cortisol, starvation, shock, and obedience-drug residue flared in her psionic field like wounds under blue light; she moved around them with a delicacy that made the violence elsewhere feel cleaner by comparison.

Banki descended into the Belowmarket.

He moved through the rooms with controlled urgency, opening cages and assigning hands before panic could spread.

Children. In cages. In restraints. In states of chemical compliance that Mirell later confirmed were achieved through the same Elder Node derivative compounds that the Choir had been distributing through the southern canal district.

Banki opened Gates.

He opened Gates and moved the captives through.

Children went through one by one, carried when they could not walk and steadied when terror made movement impossible.

Twenty-three captives. Twelve adults. Eleven children.

Among the children: a girl named Wren.

Wren was eight years old, taken four days ago by the market's twisted reckoning and much less by the world's.

When Banki opened her cage, she shrank from him harder than she had shrunk from the bars. The human skin he wore did not help. Children know monsters by pressure, not shape. Her bladder gave way before she could stop it, and shame hit her so violently that for one second it outranked fear.

Banki did not tell her not to be afraid. That would have been a lie told for his comfort.

He read only the surface, only what rescue required: Wren, mother alive, home still intact beneath the drugged fear. Then he set one precise instruction in the air, not in her mind.

"Through the light. The woman there is safer than I am."

Wren went through the Gate. On the other side, Sable was waiting—Sable, who did not smile and did not perform rescue as theatre, but who took off her own cloak, wrapped it around the girl without asking for gratitude, and stood between Wren and every shape that might become a door back into the dark.

When the last captive was through, Banki closed the Gate and turned his attention to the operators.

For one breath, the lawful shape of his mind held.

It held because law had kept him alive: Cell Nine, ledger, oath, menu, route, contract, breakfast, kingdom. Law was where rage waited until it had a door.

Then one of the unconscious operators twitched on the floor, and the man's surface thought rolled loose through Mirell's suppression field: not guilt, not horror, but irritated calculation over damaged merchandise.

The locked place opened.

Not broke. Opened.

The room found out what had been kept inside it for centuries.

He did not begin with questions. He began with hands.

The first operator woke to his fingers being unfolded one joint at a time. Banki separated choice from instrument: thumb from grip, grip from trade, trade from the ledger that had taught the man to write child-height beside resale value. Names came out before the second hand was ruined.

The second man had swallowed a charm-key. Banki found it ticking beneath the sternum, opened a narrow red line, and lifted the glittering mechanism through the breach. Then he used the man's terror to unlock the memory attached to it.

The room filled with screaming.

Kestrel, down in the saltward dark, sent no objection through the bond. Only one clean pressure: tunnels held. No escape. Her foreclaws were on stone. Her patience had teeth.

Mirell said his name once.

Banki heard her. He simply did not stop.

Varen Pell gave inventory routes and storage names before he lost the privilege of secrecy. He had been proud of the way he organised children by obedience response. Banki made him watch his own memory unfold on the wall in wet red sigils, each route written in blood pulled from capillaries without killing him. Varen tried to look away. Banki corrected the muscles in his neck.

"Read," Banki said.

Varen read until his voice broke.

Sella Vane survived because her House Velmaren connection was worth more than her death. Survival did not make her fortunate. Banki peeled her protection from her piece by piece: name, seal, patron, lover, blackmail chain, the remembered smell of a nursery she had passed three times without opening because the children inside were not hers and therefore did not matter. He left her skin mostly whole and made her understand, with perfect clarity, that usefulness was the only wall between her and the appetite in the room.

Dhorik Vrenn came under false authority and left as evidence.

He had carried a judge's signet in his boot and a child's milk tooth in a pouch for luck. Banki found the tooth first. Then the screaming changed.

The lawful part of him catalogued what followed. The rest of him let Cell Nine answer. Bone and blood became punctuation only where they bought routes, names, buyers, clerks, priests, and houses. The Black Stair drank what ran down it; the Belowmarket learned a new flavour of fear.

No child heard it.

The Gates had closed. The captives were gone. Sable's cloak was around Wren. Branna's kitchen would smell of butter instead of terror. Above, the rescued were being counted, wrapped, warmed, named. Below, Banki let the part of himself that had survived the Consortium step out from behind the throne.

It did not wear a crown.

It wore Cell Nine.

He took the Belowmarket apart person by person. Every cut asked a question. Every answer opened another door. Some died once emptied. Some lived because evidence needed a mouth. One invoked whatever gods he thought might still claim him, and Banki pinned the prayer to the wall without the tongue that formed it. Another tried to bargain with three hidden addresses, so Banki gave him a contract written in his own blood: one address for each finger he kept. The man lied on the second. Banki smiled then, small and bright and fey as a knife seen underwater, and let the contract collect interest before the third address came screaming out. When he finished, the living evidence shook in its bonds, the dead warned literate predators from the counting table, and every ledger that had priced a child carried a red handprint no clerk could file away cleanly.

He stood there briefly, while the children waited above and the wet room behind him stank of policy, opened bodies, and old rage given permission.

Then he climbed the stairs and emerged into daylight that tasted like freedom and smelled like rain.

The Belowmarket was gone.

The children were safe.

Wren would not sleep unless Sable's cloak stayed within reach. Two of the older captives refused bread until Branna ate from the same basket first. One boy hid under a bench whenever a door opened too quietly. Rescue had not made the room bright. It had only moved the dark far enough away for breathing to begin.

By afternoon, another layer of the city belonged to him. Not because the rescue had made him gentle. Because the rescued lived, the predators did not, and even children who feared him now knew which door had opened away from the dark.

Wren's mother arrived at the Hall of the Crown's Justice at nine o'clock in the morning, and she ran.

Wren's mother ran as only a parent runs when the impossible message says: alive, safe, come now.

Wren is alive. She is safe. Come now.

Banki was not present. He watched through borrowed eyes at the family reunification chamber.

He watched through the mind of a guard stationed at the family reunification chamber's entrance—a young woman whose surface thoughts were a mixture of professional composure and the particular emotional strain that came from watching reunification after reunification and trying not to cry because crying was not in the duty manual but because the duty manual had not been written by anyone who had ever witnessed a mother finding a daughter she believed was dead.

The guard's eyes—Banki's borrowed eyes—saw Wren's mother enter the chamber.

The chamber was small, warm, and deliberately unlike an institution.

Wren sat in a blanket, eating bread with the solemn focus of a child whose body remembered hunger faster than safety.

She looked up when the door opened.

Through the guard's eyes, Banki saw recognition arrive.

Then Wren dropped the bread and ran.

Her mother caught her with the ferocity of a person reclaiming the world by force.

Wren clung for three breaths, then looked past her mother's shoulder towards the door. Not for Banki. For the possibility of him. Her fingers tightened in Sable's cloak until the knuckles went white.
