"Is the shining man gone?" she whispered.

Her mother did not know who she meant. Sable, standing by the wall with rain still on her sleeves, answered instead.

"Yes."

Wren believed the word enough to cry harder.

Banki watched through the guard's eyes, and the guard's eyes were wet, and the guard's thoughts were a single, repeating phrase that was not a thought so much as a prayer: thank the Sovereign thank the Sovereign thank the Sovereign.

Banki disconnected from the guard's mind.

In the Sablehook secure room, Banki looked at his hands.

He tried to call it inventory.

Wren was safe. Eleven children out. Twelve adults out. Twenty-three perpetrators processed. One market gutted. The case should have closed.

Then his fingers remembered the size of her cage bars, and the line item would not stay still.

He was not a hero. He had moved because the cage wore his oldest enemy's face, because rescue broke the market's spine, because loyalty followed protection whether he liked the word or not, and because predators who touched children were not negotiations. They were endings.

All true.

Still, the child was safe.

Banki closed both hands until the tremor stopped.

## Rooms Beneath Rooms

Markets keep two mouths: the loud one made of haggling and lies, and the quiet one made of looks, taps, placements, and names unsaid. Banki went in business skin while Vesper and Gravewire watched the angles; Sable remained in town command.

The reliquary peddler's stall was wedged between incense and bad knives, too orderly to be innocent.

He was worry-thin, with a forked beard, one clouded eye, and shelves of saint bones, curse nails, teeth, and brineglass arranged like a confession.




“You’re the one paying for discretion now,” the peddler said.

“I pay for many things,” Banki answered. “Discretion merely tends to survive long enough to be paid twice.”

The man’s surviving good eye twitched to the left and back. Someone listening nearby. Fine. Let them hear a safe conversation.

Relics first. Then the hidden drawer.

The reliquary splinter was old and mean. The brineglass lens shard in the brass frame had once belonged to an instrument meant to read submerged enchantment residue, a byproduct of the industrialized, horrific High Magic of the era. Useful. Expensive in the correct hands. More useful still because the wrong hands did not know what they had. Banki bought both without making them the true purpose of the visit.

The true purpose came wrapped in oilcloth that smelled faintly of sulfur and barn heat.

Ashscale thread.

High-grade dragonkin shed scales no larger than thumbnail crescents, matte on one side, faintly iridescent on the other.

And a single waxed fang, not draconic in the vast ancient sense but too close to comfort for anyone who wished dragon-lines to remain theoretical.

“Where?” Banki asked.

The peddler smiled with only half his mouth. “Where is a sacred word.”

“Sacred things burn nicely.”

The smile faltered. A little pressure. No more. Mind-Sense brushed the edge of him and felt the shape of the reluctance. Not principle. Fear. Good. Principle is more expensive to work around; the Divine Calculus always favoured fear over principle.

Banki bought the man's first-right lane with coin, carefully worded protection bound by an Asmodean Pact, and enough silence to make the peddler understand ownership without hearing the word.

Hesta Bramblewake came later that same day and did not waste a syllable on manners.

Hesta Bramblewake inspected the lanes, pens, nursery threshold, and breeding outlines with executioner patience.

Banki hired her on the spot for exactly one day, because that was how long she agreed not to hate everyone in the building.

Stop-conditions. Safety gates. Sightline limits. Mothers not stressed for spectacle. No mixed scent-trails near nursery compartments. No sentimental names for breeding pairs; stupid handlers made widows.

When she left, the house was safer and less romantic.

That pleased Banki almost as much as profit would have, for it aligned perfectly with the Divine Calculus.

The true violence of the chapter, however, belonged not to breeding doctrine or peddlers but to glass.

Rinse Below hid under Gullwax Chandlery: soot above, managed stink below, and functionaries with knives instead of theatrical guards.

Banki went through them like a thought that had already made up its mind.

The first signal came from Sable. Tail identified. Pattern confirms. The second came from Whisperstamp, shadow-thin and pleased with the work. Client marks match. The third was silence, which in a city like Blackwater is the nearest thing to a bell.

He entered Rinse Below by not using the door.

Greater Shroud folded him out of sight. Polymorph took him small and true, and he entered through a vent slit no larger than a priest's conscience.

The place was beautiful in the way murder can be if done by educated hands.

Benches, clamp rigs, lens stands, residue bowls, cooling racks, and Glasswright at the central station: artist, thug, and expensive problem.

Banki listened first.

Thoughts in such rooms go loud because the hands are busy. Shipment cadence. Coil work. Clean wipe. Client mark six wants no residue. Warm room route on third bell. Quiet Loft takes the faces. Dry Kiln takes the dirt. Rinse Below takes the delicate sins.

Useful. But not enough.

He waited until the conductor at the far table bent over a tray and the nearest apron worker turned to stir the acid wash. Then he struck.

Banki flipped the acid bowl into the runner's face. Vesper ended the scream; Gravewire entered through the rear hatch and broke the conductor into the bench.

Glasswright moved faster than anyone with sane elbows had a right to.

Glasswright snapped on a ring and raised a prism wall. Banki's first bone-dust bolt split seven ways, burning the air with marrow runes that made the room uglier.

Glasswright smiled.

Banki hated him instantly.

The second exchange was craft against countercraft: slivers, buckler, wards, telekinesis, and one prism wall turned sideways into a weapon.

Vesper put an arrow into Glasswright’s thigh.

The man looked down at the shaft as if insulted by its cheapness, snapped it off, and reached for a second ring.

Banki was already there.

Mindthrust staggered Glasswright. Then the blade went in, and the room lost its cleverest hand.

Then his Eldritch Blast struck him square in the chest.

His Screaming Skull form hit like a visible verdict and folded Glasswright out of pride.

Still he lived.

“Good,” Banki said, calculating the remainder of the Asmodean Pact.

He landed beside the wrecked craftsman and laid a hand on the man’s brow.

The extraction was ugly. Deepmind theft made a library of the body and looted it while the owner remained inconveniently alive.

Banki took everything.

When he was done, the body still had a little life in it, which made the last decision more elegant under the Divine Calculus.

“Gems,” he said.

Sable’s people had already secured the room. Gravewire produced two from the lockbox. Roughly equal in value. Clear enough to hold contempt. Banki set them left and right of the dying man’s head, cut his palm, and began the bind.

The ritual that followed was not respectable wizardry. It was industrialized, horrific High Magic; the gems drank, the fang-core woke, and the room learned a new, mechanical appetite fueled by marrow runes.

When it ended, the body on the floor was a husk with the look of a puppet after the hand has left it.

Between the two gems something remained.

Twin-Gem Glasswright Reliquary.

Sapience contained.

Useful forever or until broken by a better villain.

Vesper stared down at it. “That is foul.”

“Yes,” Banki said. “Which is why it will be helpful.”

They salvaged everything.

They salvaged what mattered, burned what lied, and left Rinse Below as an expensive accident with no reliable witness.

Dry Kiln came after. Not by fire. By contract governed by the Divine Calculus.

If Rinse Below was the delicate hand, Dry Kiln was the knuckle: dirty transport engineering, cage work, and concealed movement.

The terms were simple: no outside jobs, first asks to Turnkey, second asks to Banki, one leak and nothing left but the street. Perform well and profit like a prince with no pedigree.

The crafter listened, calculated, and submitted. By the time the outpost was installed, Turnkey and Whisperstamp had six Rank-One Shadows and a doctrine clear enough to fit on bone.

One node shattered. One node collared. One still hidden behind illusion and clean-room abjuration in Quiet Loft.

The city's second mouth had begun to answer him in full sentences. He had not yet reached the darkest room waiting for him.

The first sound the forgotten cache made was not a scream, a hiss, or a psychic pulse.

It was the quiet bureaucratic clack of Sable setting one more inventory board down harder than necessary.

After Dry Kiln, Banki let Glasswright's stolen knowledge settle into usable order.

That alone was enough to sour the air.

“What.”

“Come look.”

Sable led him past the known route: shutter wall, admin turns, black-door corridor, sealed threshold. Then beyond it, to a supply room everyone had trusted because the seals had never been broken.

Supply rooms are where empires misplace their souls.

Inside waited thirty-eight sealed vials of unknown Deepmind-adjacent media, thirteen remnant containers, and twenty-seven unopened crates packed two-sided like a bureaucrat's idea of a nightmare.

The black-pink custody marks under the dust were older than the WAR-LINE transfer: black wax, pink thread, witness-wax cracked but still watching. Before war-rhinos needed a hiding place, BLACK/PINK had meant the illithid programme: living psionic contagion under a label polite enough to fit on a shelf.

For a moment even Banki did not speak.

Then he said, very softly, “Only the three of us.”

Sable had already summoned Mirell.

Mirell entered in containment habit, saw his face, and allowed herself one breath of fear. “What did we fail to remember?”

Banki pointed.

They worked from distance: long tongs, sealed lenses, spoken tests, no shared air, no hopeful shortcuts.

By dawn the room had counted itself into an accusation. Manuals. Rigs. Discipline cards. Sealed specimens. Live units. Fifty-five breaths that should not have been waiting under the city.

Mirell sat hard on an empty crate. "We were sleeping over a second war."

"No," Banki said. "We were sleeping over evidence of a first one."

The older BLACK/PINK labels were tidy enough to make Mara's future reports look obscene before she wrote them.

They tested by distance and phrase.

Veil opens on the half-bell.

Kin-lost signal.

Hunger below.

Most specimens shifted or twitched once along seam lines. Three did more.

One tilted before the sound had crossed the room. One responded to Mirell's ward cadence itself, as if listening to the listening. One seemed, for half a breath, to recognise absence.

PINK-LIVE-051 opened one milky eye.

The slate beside its cradle carried the cleaned auction name from the old ledger: LIVE-02. Mid-high risk. Identified on-floor as Illithid. Sold to Banki. The price sat below the name in a clerk's neat hand, obscene enough to prove the room had understood value and not holiness.

It did not speak. Its thoughts did not reach. The cradle held.

But the witness-wax recorded a pressure change, and Mirell's mouth tightened around a word she did not say.

Kin.

They stopped.

Not because curiosity had been satisfied. Because only amateurs continue a pattern once they know the pattern is being observed from the other side.

Cooling discipline followed. Logs sealed. Units left to settle. No conditioning effects invited by repetition. Even Banki respected that.

It was during the second night of the audit that the artifact began to take shape in him.

Not the idea of an artifact. The need.

Some discoveries demanded an answer, not merely a catalogue.

He chose to forge in public.

Not public to the city. Public to ShadowNet.

There is power in mystery, yes. But there is a deeper power in allowing your people to witness something impossible and then teaching them the rules by which it will not be used against them without cause, in strict accordance with the Asmodean Pact.

He summoned them to the yard under lamps and braziers: full Shadows forward, probationers behind, Sable at his right.

Then he called the three customs items forward as part of the rite.

Latchwarden’s Hinge-Sigil Aegis.

Wickshade’s Lantern-Null Cloakpin.

Greyslip’s Seam-Skin Ring.

Ironmote’s Pressure-Stay Buckle.

Not because they were ingredients. Because he wanted the forging to mean inheritance as much as armament. The rite would give, not merely take.

When he cut his hand and let blood fall into the crucible, the whole yard drew breath and forgot to spend it.

The Tri-Threaded Weave answered first.

It rose as three braided pressures: war, veil, and hunger given a handle.

A sceptre.

It was not ornate. The shaft held a dark inner grain, the fang-core flared, and the channels down its length looked almost vascular, dripping with the horrific High Magic of industrialized creation.

The artifact was hungry from the instant it arrived in the world.

Everyone felt that.

Even untrained minds know when an object looks back.

When Banki took it, the weapon answered with preference: wards, thresholds, hidden locks, and living circuitry.

Null-Ward’s CoreFang, he named it.

Null-Fang.

Its appetite turned.

Not towards iron. Towards living beings marked by magic, psionics, divinity, or engineered thread.

The watchers in the yard shivered almost together.

Good, Banki thought. Let them know the thing has standards dictated by the Divine Calculus.

He set its first laws at once: growth rails, caps, permissions, and no appetite without doctrine.

Then he paid the house.

Twenty-two ranked shadows. Seven hundred fifty platinum each.

Sixteen probationers. One hundred twenty-five each.

Eighteen thousand five hundred platinum made the promise visible. Appetite-backed reward was harder to doubt than rhetoric.

He named the rest that same night.

S1 became Wickshade.

S2 became Greyslip.

S4 became Ironmote.

S7’s old designation went back into the probationary pool because Nullstep had earned a name that fit tighter than a number ever had.

They received their items one by one. Public amends. Public favour. Public reminder that if Banki built a house out of ambition, he intended to furnish it.

Only Bosk remained on his shorter chain.

“Three days,” Banki told him. “Standard stock only. No Rare. No Unique. No Named. You perform within lane and your lane may widen. You step outside it and the lane becomes a ditch.”

Bosk swallowed and bowed exactly the right depth.

Later, in the quiet after the rite, Mirell showed him the staged Feed Circle rail in Pink Lab.

FEED-01: PINK-LIVE-027.

FEED-02: PINK-LIVE-051.

FEED-03: reserved.

Null-Fang leaned in his hand towards 051 with a concentration that made even Banki uncomfortable.

“Why that one?” he asked.

Mirell did not lift her gaze from the containment board. “Stitched. Engineered. Maker-marked. It wears thread over thread. Your sceptre prefers things touched twice.”

Then she tapped a second annotation.

KIN-LOST SIGNAL: latent.

HUNGER BELOW: referenced; no local proof.

"That is not proof of an Elder Brain," she said.

"No," Banki answered.

The cradle clicked once in the silence.

"It is a doorbell."

They scheduled the controlled feed and delayed it. Wisdom, sometimes, was only the courage to insult one's own curiosity.

Observe-only recon went ahead instead: Nullstep, Latchwarden, salt stair, sluice bend, quiet grate. No strike. No improvisation. No glory.

Before they left, Banki brushed their minds once, not to command but to bring them into step.

Return with truth or return with better questions.

The below-route took them. Above, Blackwater turned in its sleep. Below, the market's deeper rails waited.

## The Buyer Rail

The Buyer Rail breathed through eleven numbered doors and a clerk's bell that had rung for too many sales.

Below Gate Tally, the tunnels widened into stations built to turn kidnapped people into commodity transactions.

The Buyer Rail was the penultimate station.

It was where the buyers came.

These were not snatchers or street intermediaries. These were buyers, specialists, and clients with enough money to be careful.

Banki was wearing Vosk's face.

The polymorph was precise enough to borrow Vosk's face, gait, damage, and bad temper.

He descended into the Buyer Rail with Vosk's authority, Vosk's access tokens, and Vosk's reputation, and the guards at the entrance—two men whose surface thoughts Banki was reading as he approached—saw nothing unusual. Their minds registered: Vosk is here. Vosk has the tokens. Vosk belongs. Let him through.

They let him through.

The Buyer Rail was a low corridor lined with numbered doors and clinical alchemical light.

Along the other wall: a clerk's station. Ledgers. Inventories. The bureaucratic infrastructure of human trafficking rendered in ink and paper with the meticulous, soul-crushing thoroughness of people who had decided that the systematic documentation of atrocity was not merely acceptable but professionally necessary.

Banki approached the clerk.

Dhorik Vrenn was no longer at the desk, which made the desk more useful.

A rail hand sat in his place, surface thoughts already offering her name, schedule, fear, and ignorance.

"Vosk," she said, not looking up from her ledger. "You're early. The next rotation isn't for three hours."

"Schedule change," Banki said, in Vosk's voice. "The Prefect wants the current manifest verified before the next delivery."

Jessa's thoughts flickered with mild irritation—the annoyance of a clerk whose carefully maintained schedule has been disrupted by management—but no suspicion. Vosk made schedule changes. Vosk was management. This was normal.

"Current manifest is on the desk," she said. "Active lots in chambers three through seven. Chamber eight is maintenance. Nine is the phrase-wash."

Phrase-wash meant stripping names and replacing them with market keys.

Banki had the key. The phrase-wash was not unbreakable. It was merely unbroken—a distinction that disappeared the moment someone with sufficient telepathic capability and sufficient motivation decided to apply the former to the problem that the latter had identified.

"I'll start with the manifest," Banki said. "Then chambers three through seven."

Jessa handed him the manifest without hesitation. Her thoughts were already returning to the routine that the interruption had displaced—the comforting, anaesthetic rhythm of columns and rows and numbers that meant things only if you chose to think about what the things they meant actually were.

Banki read the manifest.

It was a list of codes, each one a person reduced to sale-state, body condition, and buyer preference.

Eleven codes were listed as active. Seven were adults. Four were children.

The manifest did not call them children. It called them juvenile stock, low-weight, compliant, premium risk, buyer-sensitive. One had a notation beside the code: teeth loose. Another: cries silently. A third had been priced higher because the buyer wanted "unmarked terror," a phrase neat enough to make the ink seem proud of itself.

Banki memorised the list in the time it took to turn the page, weighing the grim realities of the Divine Calculus against these crimes.

Then he made himself look at the buyer notes.

The first child was behind door four, tucked behind an adult woman whose body had remembered protection after the phrase-wash had taken almost everything else. She was thin enough that her wrists looked like mistakes. The child had one hand fisted in the woman's sleeve and the other pressed over her own mouth, as if sound were the thing most likely to get sold next.

She saw Vosk's face and stopped breathing.

Then Banki let the face become not-Vosk, and that frightened her too.

Good, some old, ruined part of him thought. Be afraid of strange men in rooms with locks.

A brass coat button lay beside the cot, shaped like a pear and polished at the edge where a thumb had worried it for comfort. Banki left it where the child could see him not taking it.

He did not smile. He did not soften his voice into a lie. He crouched outside arm's reach and let her see his hands were empty.

"No one buys you today," he said.

The woman moved between them by half an inch. Phrase-wash glaze dulled her eyes; the protective motion survived underneath it anyway. Banki respected that more than he respected most governments.

The child watched his face for kindness and did not find anything she recognised. Better than false warmth. False warmth had brought too many children to rooms like this. Banki gave her exactness instead: distance, open hands, a door behind him that would not remain locked.

He scanned without touching minds deeper than necessity. Dehydration. Bruising. Chemical compliance. No active obedience hook in the child. Severe one in the woman. He sent both through the extraction gate before Jessa had finished pretending not to watch.

By the third chamber, the Rail no longer felt like a corridor. It felt like a throat being emptied.

Then he began the extraction.
