"I am moving around the strike," Banki said. "Not abandoning the people behind it."

"Then tell the beasts the same thing."

She rose. At the door, she stopped.

"The children," she said. "The families. The people Seraphine restored because we pointed the heroes at the Choir. The noble houses we exposed. The network we broke."

"What about them?"

"Those things happened because we exist."

Then she left him with the sentence.

Banki sat alone over the map until it looked less like a city than a sleeping body full of doors.

The collision was no longer approaching.

It was here.

## Caelen's Arrow

The elf found the footprint at dawn.

A boot impression in mud behind the Sablehook Cold Shed, sheltered by the eave and preserved at the heel. Caelen knelt before it the way priests knelt before relics: without haste, without romance, with the humility of someone who knew truth could be ruined by wanting it too quickly.

Caelen Greenarch did not think of tracking as finding marks. Young rangers found marks and announced them like trophies. Real tracking was remembering what the ground had been before someone lied on it.

He had learned that lesson before any of the others had been born, on a border where snow kept better accounts than kings. The best trail was not the one that showed itself. It was the one the world tried to return to normal around.

He took off one glove and touched the mud beside the print, not in it. The water had seeped into the heel but not the toe. Eight hours, perhaps less. Military-grade tread, machined rather than cobbled. A heel worn on the left outside edge. Someone who moved often in confined dark, leaning through a passage that curved right until the body learned the turn.

The print pointed towards a section of foundation that looked exactly like every other section of foundation, which was how Caelen knew it had been made for human eyes and not patient ones.

He knelt.

Someone was accessing a space beneath this building. Frequently. The entrance was hidden well enough to fool eyes and common wards. It could not fool pressure. A scuffed heel, a softened edge of packed dust, the faint settling where stone had been lifted too often: Caelen read them the way other men read fresh ink.

He did not approach the entrance. He marked the location—a nick in the stone that his elven eyes could identify from fifty feet but that human eyes would pass over as natural weathering. He noted the time. He catalogued the boot print's characteristics for later comparison with any personnel connected to the Sablehook operation.

Then he withdrew without leaving prints of his own, not from vanity, but from habit older than the city wall. Snow, ash, moss, mud: all of them kept accounts if you pressed too hard.

He reported to Aldric within the hour.

"I found their door," Caelen said. "Or one of them. Underground access. Behind the Cold Shed. Recent traffic, regular pattern, multiple personnel. Whatever they're hiding, it's below the building, and they're accessing it through a concealed entrance that's protected against magical detection but not against the oldest detection method in the world."

"Which is?"

"Looking at the ground."

Aldric listened without interrupting, which was how Caelen knew the report had landed. Commanders who interrupted wanted reassurance. Aldric wanted the shape of the wound.

"Not from the surface. But the boot print tells me whoever goes down there does it often, does it in the dark or near-dark, and comes back with stone dust on their soles. They've cut chambers down there. Recently. The construction is ongoing."

"Chambers for what?"

"That," Caelen said, "is what we need to find out."

Aldric looked at his party: Seraphine pale from prayer, Lyris with ink on two fingers, Maren too quiet beside her lute, Caelen still carrying mud under one nail like a witness.

"We have been careful," Aldric said. "We have waited. We have gathered enough."

His hand found the place where his sword should have been and rested there anyway.

Caelen marked a second route and crossed it out. Too many sleeping families. He would not turn pursuit into a blade dragged through ordinary walls, no matter how cleanly the trail invited it.

"Tomorrow we knock. If the answer is false, we go below."

"And if his explanation is insufficient?" Lyris asked.

"Then we go down there," Aldric said. "And we deal with whatever we find."

Maren strummed a chord on her lute—a minor chord, pensive, the sound of a story approaching its climax.

"I liked him," she said quietly. "The salvage man. Banki. When we talked in the tavern. I liked him."

"I know," Aldric said. His voice was not unkind. But it was absolute. "That doesn't change what we have to do."

"I know," Maren said. "That's what makes it hard."

The party prepared in the old ways: weapons checked, prayers offered, spells laid out, routes argued down to the last turn.

After the others left the table, Caelen stayed with the map.

The footprint bothered him. Not because it was hidden well. Because it was hidden honestly. Whoever had built the door knew that wards failed before mud did.

Caelen respected that.

He disliked respecting it.

At dawn, they would knock.

Caelen cleaned mud from beneath one fingernail and left the last dark crescent there. A clean hand forgot too easily. He wanted the earth's report with him when Aldric raised his fist to the door.

## The Confrontation

They came at noon.

Aldric wanted witnesses. Not theatre. Not secrecy. Witnesses. Light did not make him gentle; it made him accountable, and accountability, in Aldric Thorne, had always been one step away from judgment.

Banki watched them approach through the eyes of six different minds along their route—a street sweeper, a cart driver, two dock workers, a child playing in a doorway, and Edric Mallon, who was watching from the Gull & Gasket's upper window and whose surface thoughts were a nauseating mixture of loyalty, fear, and the frantic calculation of a man trying to determine which outcome of the approaching confrontation was least likely to result in his tavern being burned down.

Five figures. Walking abreast. Not in formation—not the military column that Banki had expected—but in a line, shoulder to shoulder, like a wall. A wall of intent. A wall of conviction. A wall of five people who had decided, collectively and individually, that they were going to walk through whatever was in front of them and that the whatever's opinion on the matter was irrelevant.

Aldric was in the centre. Armoured. Not the civilian clothes of the Guttered Lantern meeting—full plate, burnished to a mirror sheen, the Dawnfather's rising sun embossed on the breastplate in gold that caught the noon light and threw it back at the world with the aggressive generosity of a religion that believed light was a weapon. His sword was sheathed but his hand was near it, and his face—Banki studied that face through six simultaneous perspectives and found, in every one of them, the same thing: certainty.

Seraphine flanked his right. Caelen flanked his left. Lyris and Maren anchored the ends. Each one armed. Each one prepared. Each one radiating, in Banki's telepathic field, the particular flavour of purpose that Good-aligned beings produced when they were about to do something they considered important: a warm, golden pressure that tasted like sunlight and smelled like clean steel and felt, against the edges of Banki's psychic awareness, like the approach of a storm.

They stopped at the gate of the Sablehook compound.

Banki was waiting for them.

He stood in the courtyard, human-faced, business-dressed, calm.

Fight was too expensive. Deception was incomplete. Refusal would become proof. So he chose the one weapon dangerous enough for the moment: a selected truth.

Banki walked forward to meet them.

"Paladin Thorne," he said. His voice was respectful without being deferential, calm without being dismissive, open without being exposed. "I've been expecting you."

Aldric's eyes—blue, clear, burning with the intensity of a man who was actively channeling divine power through his visual cortex—studied Banki's face with the focused, penetrating attention of a searchlight examining a dark room.

"You know who I am," Aldric said. Not a question.

"I know who you are. I know what you've done in this city. I know that you destroyed the Choir's operation, rescued over a hundred affected citizens, and restored ninety-one minds that had been altered by psionic manipulation." He paused. Let the facts settle. "I also know that the intelligence that allowed you to locate the Choir's infrastructure was delivered to you anonymously, through dead drops, by someone who had been fighting the Choir for months before you arrived."

Aldric's expression did not change. The paladin had suspected that the anonymous intelligence had come from a source within the city's criminal ecosystem. He had used it anyway, because the intelligence was accurate and the cause was just and refusing to use good information because it came from a bad source was the kind of moral purity that got people killed.

"You," Aldric said.

"Me. Through intermediaries. Through channels that could not be traced. Through methods that I am not going to describe to you because describing them would compromise operations that are still ongoing and that are producing results that you would approve of if you knew about them."

"Results like what?"

Banki gave him the one truth sharp enough to matter. He had kept it since the Belowmarket, not because it was clean, but because a paladin of the Dawnfather would have to look at it before he looked away.

"Twenty-three people rescued from an underground trafficking market beneath the southern canal district. Eleven of them children. The youngest was five. The market has been destroyed. Its operators have been captured and transferred to the Crown's judicial authority. The noble houses that enabled its existence are being prosecuted through Open Court proceedings that I initiated, supported, and provided evidence for. House Velmaren. House Rennic. House Hadrake. House Merrow. You can verify all of this through the public court records."

Silence.

Seraphine was the first to speak. "The children. Where are they?"

"Safe. Reunited with their families. Under the care of the Crown's child welfare authority, which has been funded by the proceeds of the asset seizures from the prosecuted houses. I can provide names. I can provide locations. I can provide verification through any channel you consider sufficiently trustworthy."

Seraphine wanted to disbelieve him. Her face said as much. Then the facts made a quiet, stubborn thing of her silence.

"You're not telling us everything," Lyris said. The wizard's voice was sharp, clinical, the voice of an analyst who recognised a partial dataset when she saw one. "The concealment enchantments on this compound are military-grade, powered by industrialized High Magic and marrow runes. The false-positive signatures in the district are deliberate countermeasures designed to blind divination sweeps. And there's something beneath this building—" she pointed at the ground "—that my detection spells can't penetrate but that Seraphine's divine perception identified as significant."

"Yes," Banki said. "There is."

"What is it?"

"Underworks," Banki said. "Secured stores. Creature holding. Workrooms. The kind of rooms a business needs when rare animals, rare materials, and violent competitors all insist on existing in the same city."

"A business," Caelen said. The ranger's tone carried the particular dryness of an elf who had heard euphemisms for six centuries and had developed an allergy. "With military-grade concealment."

"With competitors who have demonstrated willingness to use violence, espionage, and magical sabotage to disrupt my operations. Yes. The concealment is defensive, not offensive. I am hiding from people who want to destroy what I've built. You are not those people."

Aldric raised his hand. The gesture silenced his party with the immediate, unquestioning compliance that came from years of trusting the man who made the gesture.

"I want to see," Aldric said. "Below."

The moment stretched. Refusal would prove too much. Admission would reveal too much. Banki chose the lie with the most truth inside it.

"I'll show you the holding facilities," Banki said. "The creature containment. The research space. The legitimate infrastructure that supports the auction business."

"All of it," Aldric said.

"The court-facing areas," Banki said. "Creature containment, research space, holding facilities, and nothing that exposes clients, trade routes, or protected citizens."

Aldric studied him. The Moral-Sight pulsed against the ring's blankness and returned no answer: not good, not evil, only absence.

"Show me what you're willing to show me," Aldric said. "And understand that I will be back."

"I would expect nothing less," Banki replied.

He led them into Sablehook.

The tour lasted two hours and showed the heroes exactly enough: holding cells, observation rooms, preservation equipment, Darric's cleaned research space, public ledgers, fair wages, legal permits, creature work with every ugly edge scrubbed until it could pass as commerce.

He did not show them the Stitchroom. He did not show them the deep vaults. Aldric saw the omissions anyway, the way a veteran sees where a battlefield has been swept too clean.

The heroes left Sablehook at two in the afternoon.

At the gate, Aldric stopped beside the outer auction rail, where Sablehook's seal had been carved into blackwood and silvered for the benefit of clients who liked their legitimacy visible. The rail was legal property. Registered. Taxed. Protected by municipal statute and two clauses Mara had personally bullied into shape.

Aldric looked at it for a long moment.

Then he drew a line of dawnfire through the seal.

The blackwood did not catch like ordinary timber. It resisted first, wards shivering, silver brightening to white. Then the Dawnfather's light bit through everything Banki had paid for and left the emblem split from crown to hook. Smoke rose sweet and sharp. No one was hurt. Nothing necessary was destroyed.

Aldric did not look at the damage as vandalism. He looked at it as a sermon he could afford to pay for.

Seraphine's lips parted as if prayer might step in and repair the gesture before it became doctrine. No prayer came. Aldric had not asked the Dawnfather to forgive the line. He had drawn it in the god's light.

The restraint made it worse.

Mara inhaled through her nose. Banki felt three legal remedies assemble in her mind before anger could reach her mouth.

"That is private property," she said.

"Yes," Aldric replied.

"You have no warrant."

"No."

"Then you have committed a chargeable offence in front of witnesses."

Aldric's eyes did not leave Banki. "File it."

Banki had modelled him as lawful, honourable, procedural. Accurate. Incomplete. Aldric Thorne would burn a monster's sign in daylight, accept the charge, and teach the city that paperwork could not domesticate judgment.

The move was not irrational.

It was expensive virtue weaponised into vandalism.

"The children," Aldric said. "The ones you rescued. Real."

"Yes."

"The intelligence you provided about the Choir. Accurate."

"Yes."

"Yes," Banki said. The truth served better than any lie available.

Aldric stepped over the ash of the seal.

"Then understand me," he said. "Law is not a kennel you can lock righteousness inside. If your statutes shield victims, I will use them. If they shield you, I will break what I can afford to break and pay the fine in open court."

Aldric's jaw tightened. Seraphine watched the decision settle into him and felt, for the first time that day, less afraid of Banki than of what goodness might become when it stopped asking permission.

"I will be watching," Aldric said.

"I know," Banki replied.

"And I will be back."

"I know that too."

They looked at each other across the smoke: the man who would save the city and the man who would own it.

Then Aldric walked away, and Banki stood in the courtyard while one piece of his empire cooled into ash.

The smoke kept rising after he stopped pretending the day had ended well.

## The Night of Knives

The stalemate broke at three in the morning.

Banki felt the break like a spider feels its web shiver: not as one event, but as a run of tremors racing through the ShadowNet's watch-lines with the urgency of a crack crossing ice.

The first disturbance was Mirell.

Mirell's signal changed pitch: not alarm, not breach, but warning. Something was wrong. Something was coming.

Banki was in his room above the Gull & Gasket, Sable asleep beside him, when the signal changed. He was awake in an instant. No grogginess. No humane confusion. His Mind-Sense opened to the city, and the city's sleeping minds answered as scattered, dim constellations.

Except for five minds that were not sleeping.

Five minds that were moving. Through the canal district. Towards the Wharfside. In a formation that Banki recognised because he had studied it, modelled it, war-gamed against it in a hundred mental simulations:

Aldric in the centre. Seraphine on the right flank. Caelen on the left. Lyris at the rear. Maren between Aldric and Seraphine.

They were coming.

Not at dawn. Not at noon. At three in the morning, in the fog, without announcement or legal ritual. Something had given Aldric evidence, conviction, or both.

Banki had three seconds. In three seconds he had their vector, probable objective, readiness, and state of mind. Determined was worse than angry.

He also processed: his own capabilities, partially depleted; his people, scattered across a city that had become a map of sleeping vulnerabilities; the Breakfast Hall, where Crown Citizens would begin lining up in less than two hours; the children in the protected dormitory above the kitchen; Mara asleep with ink on one hand and three law books open beside her; Tally's ledgers; Kessa's lanes; Branna's ovens banked but warm; Mirell in the Stitchroom, alert but not combat-ready; and Sable beside him, asleep, though never helpless—a peer even in rest.

He instantly deployed an invisible safety net, anchoring her mind psionically so she could fight with full autonomy if awakened.

Three seconds.

"Sable." His voice was low, controlled, carrying the particular urgency that communicated danger without communicating panic. "Wake up. Now."

She was awake in two seconds. Not the groggy, confused awakening of a civilian but the combat-ready snap of a professional who had spent years sleeping with one hand near a weapon. Her eyes found his in the dark. Read his expression. Read the situation in his expression.

"How many?" she asked.

"Five. The heroes. Full party. Three minutes out."

Sable was out of the bed and dressed in forty-five seconds. Banki used the time to send five telepathic signals simultaneously:

*To Shadow command: Crown Shield. Heroes approaching Sablehook. Full party. Nighttime assault posture. Seal black corridors. Open legal corridors. Protect people first, evidence second, pride last.*

*To Mara: Breakfast Hall becomes lawful sanctuary. Front doors open to inspection; citizen doors close by contract. No Crown Citizen leaves without named charge, signed warrant, and your witnessed reading. Feed everyone. Record everything.*

*To Tally: Public ledgers forward. Private ledgers deep. Be boring enough to cause moral fatigue.*

*To Mirell: Seal. Maximum defence posture. Do not engage the divine caster. BLACK/PINK authorised. Release WAR-LINE to Crown Shield street patrol: defend Crown Citizens, wards, children, kitchen staff, and civilians inside Crown-protected routes. No hunt. No homes. Interpose first, maim second, kill only against active lethal threat.*

*To Kestrel, above the city: Exposed assets to ground. Watch routes, not heroes. If panic starts near the Hall, stop the panic.*

The responses came in rapid sequence:

*Mara: Awake. Hall law active in two minutes. Branna is already angry.*

*Tally: Public books staged. Private chains buried.*

*Kestrel: Roofs moving. No one touches the children.*

*Mirell: Sealing. BLACK/PINK accepted. WAR-LINE waking. Direction Four remains Caravan-active. PINK-LIVE-051 just answered something it should not have heard. The Elder Node is awake. Something is happening in the deep places. Unrelated to the surface threat. Will explain when able.*

Then the kitchen channel screamed without sound.

Not Branna. Not Mara. Not any voice Banki could name.

A soul-address went white.

Tovin Vale was crossing the pantry threshold with a basket against his hip. Seven bent spoons. Two cracked cups. One biscuit he had been saving because Branna had told him useful boys still needed to eat. He had Crown breakfast in him, Crown shelter over him, and the faint warm mark of belonging written where Banki's emerging law insisted no child under his roof would ever be unfindable.

The Elder Node touched that mark from below.

It had not found Sablehook through a traitor, a prayer, or a careless map. It had found the house through the kin-lost pressure sleeping under BLACK/PINK: PINK-LIVE-051 answering below before anyone in the room understood that hibernation was not silence.

It found him because Banki had made him findable.

The biscuit had written an address on a child's soul and called the address safety.

Banki turned all of himself towards the child.

He was late by less than a breath.

Tovin Vale existed.

Then existence forgot how to hold him.

No blood. No body. No scream. The basket struck the floor with seven spoons in it, then six, then no one in the kitchen could remember why a basket had fallen. One crescent-dented spoon rolled under the prep table and stopped against nothing anyone could see. The biscuit vanished from Branna's hand between one heartbeat and the next, except for a dusting of salt on her thumb that made her start crying before she knew why. Mara's ledger opened three rooms away to a line no ink would occupy.

The soul-address Banki reached for tore out of the Crown's metaphysical book and left no whole thread, no corpse, no ghost, no clean afterlife-hook, no name a resurrection could answer.

Not gone cleanly.

The cruelty had no body. Death left handles. This left a torn warmth too thin to grasp and too present to call nothing.

It had arrived through the address he had called safety.

Something in him split.

Whatever had taken the child would learn pain.

He found the Elder Node's feeler in the Deepmind residue under Sablehook and in the answering bruise around PINK-LIVE-051. He drove his Eldritch Blast, howling in its Screaming Skull form, through it like a horrific High Magic bone-dust hook through an eye. In the Stitchroom, three stored proxy-minds convulsed in their sealed jars as the foreign signal tore backward through them. In the Pink Lab, the illithid cradle rang once without moving. In the dark below the city, something vast recoiled. Banki followed. He poured command, hunger, grief, and every lesson the Consortium had carved into him down the line until the connection shrieked with borrowed nerves.

*All channels,* he sent, and the voice that went out was not calm enough to be law. *Deep breach. Kill every unregistered psionic signature. Burn every compromised route. Release BLACK protocols. No containment. No salvage. No prisoners.*

Under Sablehook, doors began to answer.

The WAR-LINE shifted in their stalls from patrol cadence towards hunt cadence. Gate-King frames ground against their braces. Choirless Siren Moths opened their wings too wide in the Breakfast Hall rafters, drinking fear until the air thinned. Mirror-Maw Eels struck the canal gates hard enough to bend iron. The kingdom heard its king lose shape.

Sable hit him.

Not hard enough to injure. Hard enough to make the body remember it had a face.

"Banki."

He did not hear her.

She caught both sides of his head and stepped into the place no sensible person entered willingly: close enough that he could have read her, broken her, owned every secret she had ever kept.

"Read me," she said.

The words cut through the fracture because they were impossible.

Sable opened one narrow door in her mind. Not all of it. Never all. A blade-width only, offered by choice and held by discipline. Through it came fear, fury, grief for a child she could no longer remember correctly, and something worse for Banki than accusation: refusal to leave.

You are here, her mind told him. You are not back in the cage. I am not your handler. I am not your jailer. I am standing beside you, and I am telling you no.

His revenge tried to keep moving.

Sable held.

"If you make the city pay for what took him," she said aloud, "then he becomes the first stone in the prettier prison. His cannot mean that. You hear me? His means answerable. You are answerable to me now."

Tovin's name flickered once in Banki's mind, already losing edges.

He seized it with everything he had.

Tovin Vale.

The name burned. One syllable snagged under the Crown Mark like cloth on a nail.

Then the universe took even that from conscious reach.

Banki made a sound no human throat had been designed to make.

Sable did not let go.

Slowly, brutally, as if lowering a blade onto his own hand, he recalled the order.

*All channels: Crown Shield only. Interpose. Defend. Record. No hunt. No BLACK release without my spoken confirmation and Sable's counterseal.*

The doors stopped answering.

The WAR-LINE returned to patrol cadence.

In the kitchen, Branna began crying without knowing why. Mara stared at a blank place in the ledger and understood enough to be afraid of understanding more. She set her pen to the empty line and could not make ink choose a category. The law had boxes for death, treason, disappearance, and refusal. It had no box for a child the world had unfiled.

Banki stood in the dark room above the Gull & Gasket with Sable's hands still on his face and a child's impossible absence lodged under his ribs where no resurrection could reach it.

## Law With Hooves

Under Sablehook, Mirell opened the BLACK/PINK Secrecy Section.

The order passed under black wax and one pink thread wound through the seal: black for sovereign secrecy, pink for living danger. Public Direction Four stayed Caravan Line. BLACK/PINK opened beneath it.

No public files called the military branch anything. The elite armour slates called six of them Mithril Spine Guard. The handler boards called the whole branch WAR-LINE.

The forty-two-beast Military Line woke in black stalls, each massive Dire Displacer Rhino body resolving through displaced afterimage: horn here, shoulder there, armoured bulk half a pace from where the eye insisted it should be. Paired displacer tentacles uncoiled from living anchor-plates, testing the air with cruel, independent intelligence. They could bind, whip, hook, brace, carry, pin, crush, spare, and strike from the angle the eye had just finished trusting.

For the first breath, even the handlers felt monster-awe.

Then Kestrel arrived above them.

She came along the upper gantry without hurry, claws setting into blackwood, optics dark at her throat, family seal low against her harness where the lamplight caught the edge of it and made the metal look crowned. She was not larger than the WAR-LINE. She was not stronger. She did not need either lie. The herd knew height, nerve, bloodline, command pressure, and the old truth of predators: apex is not size. Apex is the thing every other dangerous body checks before it moves.

One foreclaw struck the rail.

Every tentacle in the first row stilled.

Kestrel's speaking charm clicked, and the voice that entered the stall gallery was rough, royal, and utterly without plea. "Route-hold. Child-first. No hunt without spoken seal. Break weapons. Spare rooms. Obey handler triad unless I countermand."

A Mithril Spine unit lowered its horn by three inches.

Then the next.

Then the line.

The herd did not wake as one appetite. The Refinement Line had made sure of that. Command-noise died against Crown authentication. Handler triads settled. Kestrel's apex discipline moved through them like a cold moon over black water, and under it Sable's wartime authority sat beneath Banki's sovereign mark, not as ownership, but as the operational no that prevented power from mistaking urgency for permission.

Kestrel crossed to the highest rail after the command settled, and the WAR-LINE tracked her without challenge. Monster-awe remained for everyone else. For her, the herd held rank.

Inside one Mithril Spine unit, the city arrived without names: heat-lanes, stone-throat, bridge-bone, hostile-bright, child-near, door-thin, route-hold. Its tentacles tasted three futures. Catch the blade. Break the weapon. Do not enter the room. The collar did not make those truths gentle. It made them clear.

Mirell did not unleash them as beasts.

She released them as a secret forced to leave the dark.

They entered the streets by warehouse mouth, drover's arch, and preplanned route, Crown sigils burning low on collar plates. Fog bent around them badly. Windows opened and closed again very quickly.

They did not charge civilians. They did not chase drunks, beggars, dockhands, or children at shutters. They moved to intersections, clinic doors, worker dormitories, kitchen approaches, canal bridges, and the streets between Sablehook and districts already under Crown care. Their tentacles made restraint more frightening: one hooked a falling lantern out of a woman's path; another pinned a knife-man's wrist to brick without tearing it off; a third flattened along a wall so frightened citizens could pass behind the horn.
