Other things woke too, quietly enough to frighten the people who knew what quiet meant. In the Breakfast Hall rafters, a Choirless Siren Moth opened pale wings over three children dreaming badly and drank the old song out of their sleep. In the clinic yard, a Mercy Thorn Grazier pressed its warm muzzle to a handler's bandaged arm until red buds opened along its crown. Behind the netted canal gates, a Mirror-Maw Eel surfaced in the wrong reflection and waited without biting.

Public Direction Four stayed Caravan-active. BLACK/PINK moved under black wax and pink thread.

Banki felt the patrol pattern settle across Blackwater Quay.

Custody, he thought, though the city's fear would not forgive the word.




The Elder Node had already made it true.

The Underdark was paying attention to Blackwater Quay. The supply chain beneath the Choir was no longer passive. Banki's most dangerous secret—Mirell, the Stitchroom, the Neural Savant work, every hidden compromise between survival and monstrosity—now faced two fronts at once: the light above and the intelligence below.

Worse, the intelligence below had learned where to hurt him.

Banki looked at Sable. She was dressed, armed, ready, and still close enough that the ghost of her hands remained on his face.

"We need to move," Banki said. His voice sounded scraped empty. "Not away. Around."

"Around what?"

"The blade aimed at Sablehook."

"Explain."

"Aldric came for me. The Elder Node is reaching for Mirell. If I stand in the Breakfast Hall to prove I am not afraid, every force aimed at me lands with my people behind me."

Sable's eyes narrowed. "And Sablehook?"

"Stays alive. Mara holds the legal face. Branna feeds the Hall. Tally gives them paperwork until their teeth ache. Kessa keeps the lanes closed. The children stay under Crown protection. The wards stay warm. The public house remains a house."

"And the deep levels?"

"Sealed, cut, and misdirected. Rooms we can afford to lose become rooms they are allowed to find. Doors we cannot afford to lose become stone, oath, and absence."

"You're not giving them Sablehook."

For the first time since waking, Banki looked offended.

"No." His voice went soft enough to become dangerous. "Sablehook is not a shell. Sablehook is breakfast, beds, wages, contracts, children sleeping without chains, and a city learning that someone dangerous will answer when predators knock. I am not abandoning that. I am refusing to turn it into a battlefield because a paladin wants a clean target."

Sable held his gaze.

"Good," she said. "Then what are we doing?"

"Drawing the hunt off the doorstep. Aldric follows tracks. The Elder Node follows signal. We give both of them movement that leads away from the people."

"To the Dry Kiln."

"First. Then somewhere older than my maps."

She understood before he asked. Her expression changed by almost nothing, but the change mattered.

"My fallback," she said.

"Only if you choose it."

"Lives are at immediate risk," she said. "The terms adjust."

They left through the window. Banki in human form, Sable beside him, moving through the fog with the coordinated, silent urgency of two people who had practised escape routes because escape routes were the kind of thing you practised before you needed them rather than after.

Behind them, the Gull & Gasket's upper room was empty. The bed was still warm. The candle on the windowsill had been extinguished.

Ahead of them, five heroes moved through the fog. Behind them, the Breakfast Hall lamps stayed lit, the inner doors locked, and the first hoofbeat answered the street.

## Fifth Canal Bridge

Aldric met the first patrol at Fifth Canal Bridge.

Caelen saw it before the others did. Not the beast itself. The street's refusal to make sense. Hoofprints appeared in the mud in two places at once: one deep enough to cup rainwater, one shallow and offset by half a body length, both belonging to the same impossible weight. The fog ahead bent wrong. Gaslight slid sideways across something too broad to be a wagon and too still to be weather.

He raised one fist.

The party stopped.

Fifth Canal Bridge narrowed the district into four bad choices: bridge mouth east, laundry alley north, canal arch below, market lane west. Aldric's party stood where all four could become a trap at once.

The Military Dire Displacer Rhino stepped out of the fog.

It was larger than the draft rhinos Aldric had seen in war caravans, heavier through shoulder and head, built not for haulage but impact. Black plate banded its chest. A Crown sigil burned low on the collar brace. Its horn was a steel-fluted lance of dark keratin. From the armoured ridge above its forequarters rose two displacer tentacles as thick as ship hawsers and quick as striking eels. One curled in the fog beside the beast. The other moved three feet to the left of its own shadow. Body here, afterimage there, tentacle-tip already waiting where a frightened body would dodge next.

For one stunned second, Aldric did not understand what he was seeing.

Then understanding arrived, and with it came horror.

This was not an armoured animal. This was integration. Plates seated under hide. Tentacles braided into shoulder, spine, ward, and command response. The collar was not restraint but interface. The displacer effect breathed through muscle and spatial instinct, as if someone had taught a dire rhino's honest mass to lie.

Aldric had seen battlefield abominations before: war-ogres with iron jaws, hellhounds chained to siege towers, men with alchemical rage pumped into their blood until the blood forgot mercy.

This was cleaner. Worse.

"Dawnfather preserve us," Seraphine whispered.

Aldric's grip tightened on his shield until leather creaked. "He built these."

Not bought. Not found. Built.

Banki had taken a living siege engine and made discipline part of the graft.

Savage would have been easier to hate. This was disciplined.

Then the second one appeared on the bridge parapet above the canal stairs.

Then the third behind them.

Not surrounding like animals.

Triangulating like soldiers.

Lyris whispered something very impolite.

The lead hybrid lowered its head until the horn pointed at Aldric's breastplate. It did not charge. It breathed steam through plated nostrils and struck one hoof against the stones.

Once.

The street answered like a drum.

Maren felt the vibration through her boots and understood, with the part of her that had spent years reading rooms, that this was not intimidation for pleasure. It was a command. Halt. Identify. Do not advance.

"They are guarding the route," Seraphine said.

"They are abominations," Aldric replied.

Seraphine's divine sight flickered over the lead beast and caught no simple evil. Violence, yes. Conditioning. Pain edited into obedience. Military purpose braided through animal terror and something stranger, something spatially wrong. But not malice. Not cruelty for itself.

"They are not attacking," she said.

"They are blocking access to evidence."

Aldric stepped forward.

The lead hybrid moved.

No beast that large should have moved so quickly. It did not sprint; it translated violence across distance. One instant it stood at the bridge mouth. The next its shoulder filled the street. Aldric got his shield up because thirty years of combat had made his body wiser than surprise, but the impact struck like a siege ram finding a chapel door.

The shield held.

The street did not.

Cobbles burst under Aldric's boots. The paladin slid backward six feet, then ten, then struck a fishmonger's stone table hard enough to split it in half. White divine fire flared along his sword as he answered with a cut meant to take the beast across the cheek.

The blade passed through an afterimage.

The real horn came from the wrong side.

The right tentacle came from no honest side at all. It wrapped Aldric's shield-rim and yanked, not to pull him down, but to open his guard for the horn. Aldric burned it with divine light before it could take his sword arm. The tentacle recoiled, smoking black ichor across consecrated steel, and struck the cobbles where his knee had been.

Caelen's arrow hit first. Not the eye. The collar hinge. The shot was perfect, and perfection failed by three inches because the creature was not where sight insisted it was. The arrow clipped a ward-plate, passed through a false tentacle-shadow, and sparked away into the canal.

Caelen watched the miss instead of the beast. Misses told the truth if pride did not interrupt. The false shoulder lagged. The real weight pulled mud half a heartbeat before the image admitted it. He filed the rhythm and moved for height.

"Scatter!" Caelen shouted.

The WAR-LINE did it for them.

The second hybrid came off the parapet in a controlled drop that should have broken the bridge and somehow did not. Its hooves hit stone with disciplined ruin. Its tentacles hit first. One lashed low across the street and cut the party's formation at the knees without touching flesh; the air itself seemed to trip around it. The shockwave took Maren off her feet and threw her through a curtain of fog into the mouth of a laundry alley. Seraphine's prayer caught her before her head struck brick, but the cleric paid for the save when the third hybrid's tentacle hooked the edge of her ward and the shoulder followed.

The ward turned white.

Then gold.

Then cracked like ice under a hammer.

Seraphine went to one knee. Lyris stepped in front of her with both hands up and built a wall of blue force across the lane.

The lead hybrid struck it.

The wall did not shatter. It bent.

Worse.

Lyris screamed through clenched teeth as the force plane bowed inward. She stopped thinking in spells and started thinking in vectors: mass, angle, give, survive. Tentacles found the spell's edges as if force had handles. The beast angled its horn aside at the last instant and drove the wall backward with its shoulder until Lyris lost footing and slid twenty feet down the street.

From the roofline, Caelen read the pattern by impact and restraint: break formation, split caster from healer, drive commander off line, hold intersections, deny route.

"Aldric!" Maren called.

The paladin was already back on his feet.

Blood ran from his mouth. His shield arm trembled once and stopped trembling because Aldric did not permit his body to have opinions during battle. He drove forward beneath the lead hybrid's horn, sword blazing, and this time he did not strike the afterimage. He struck the shadow beneath the wrongness, reading displacement by faith rather than sight.

The blow landed.

Divine fire opened a bright seam across the hybrid's front plate.

The beast roared.

Aldric saw the opening and took it, because thirty years of war had trained him to spend pain faster than doubt. He stepped inside the horn-line, shield high, sword angled for the collar.

The tentacle he had burned returned from the wrong place.

Not the same tentacle, his mind insisted. The same tentacle, the street answered. Displacement made a liar of both.

It wrapped his shield wrist and tightened. Consecrated steel groaned. Leather straps snapped one by one. Aldric drove light through the gauntlet, enough holiness to blind a lesser thing, and the WAR-LINE unit did not release because it had not been ordered to win painlessly. It had been taught to deny commanders.

The horn did not take his chest.

That mercy was the horror.

It turned at the last instant, not goring, not killing, using the steel-fluted side and a tentacle-hook together in one brutal, precise motion. Shield, gauntlet, wrist, and certainty separated from Aldric in a sound so small Maren almost missed it beneath the roar.

The hand stayed inside the consecrated gauntlet.

Aldric did not.

He hit the stones on one knee, left arm ending in a spatially wrong blaze of blood and divine light. For one heartbeat his face went utterly empty. Then command returned before pain could make a throne of him.

"Move," he said.

Seraphine screamed his name.

The WAR-LINE did not finish him. That restraint was somehow more obscene than slaughter. The lead hybrid shouldered him sideways, hard enough to throw him out of the direct route, then wheeled back to the bridge mouth.

The sound hit every window on the street at once. Glass burst inward above them. Shutters slammed.

Somewhere a child cried out.

The WAR-LINE changed.

Every head snapped towards the sound with such violent unity that the fog itself seemed to flinch. Tentacles that had been opening for another strike froze midair, tips spread like black hooks listening. The lead hybrid's roar died in its throat and came back lower, not battle-rage now but something older and more terrible: nursery-rage, threshold-rage, the kill-law Banki had carved into blood and gland and displacement nerve until no order beneath his own could make the herd ignore a child's fear.

Child-near.

Child-threat.

Shield.

The command was not language. It was genetic scripture. Muscles bunched under armour. Horns angled away from the heroes and towards the unseen source. One tentacle struck a falling shard of glass out of the air before it reached the alley mouth. Another flattened against a doorframe, not entering, only sealing the gap with living force while the child inside sobbed once and then went silent from terror.

If an enemy had stood over that child, the WAR-LINE would have opened the street to reach them.

No enemy did.

So the herd held.

Aldric saw it through the white edge of shock.

So did Maren.

For one bright, terrible instant she understood that the monsters had been made to hear children before command, before pain, before victory. She hated Banki for making that matter.

That half-breath was all Caelen needed. "North stair!" he shouted. "Now!"

Maren rolled through laundry water, came up with her lute half-drawn, and struck three hard chords. Not a battle song. A rhythm break. The sound snapped across the street like thrown coins, and for one blessed second the hybrids' displacement fields stuttered between beats. Their tentacles became visible in all the wrong places at once: one real, one echo, one future strike aborted by music.

Lyris used the second.

She slammed a grease-slick of conjured force under the lead hybrid's front hooves, not to topple it—nothing short of a collapsing building was going to topple it cleanly—but to change one step by four inches.

Four inches mattered.

Aldric staggered clear of the horn. Seraphine threw a curtain of dawnlight between the party and the second hybrid, not because the light would stop it but because even trained eyes blinked when sunrise opened in an alley at three in the morning. Caelen dropped from the roof, caught Maren by the back of her coat, and hauled her towards the north stair as the third hybrid's tentacle struck the place where her spine had been.

Stone powdered.

The stair rail vanished. The tentacle recoiled from the house wall without entering the room beyond it, obeying the Crown Shield rule more cleanly than anything that monstrous had any right to obey.

The party scattered along the four bad choices Caelen had marked: Aldric into bridge shadow, Seraphine and Lyris through the laundry alley north, Caelen and Maren under the canal arch below. The market lane stayed blocked by horn and plate. The WAR-LINE did not chase into houses. It flowed back into intersections and held the route.

Behind them the bridge mouth belonged to horn and black plate. To the north, the stair remained barely open. To the west, the laundry alley steamed under Seraphine's fading dawnlight. The monsters had not won the street by killing everyone in it. They had won by deciding where everyone was allowed to stand.

Maren kept hearing the child's cry inside the hoofbeats. The monsters had turned for it before any human command could form.

Later, Hesta would underline one sentence until the BLACK/PINK report tore: Tentacles recalled under pain. Returned to route after injury.

For one night, the wall had held.

When the five regrouped two blocks north, no one spoke for nine breaths.

Lyris's hands shook badly enough that she hid them in her sleeves.

Maren had blood at one temple and brick dust in her hair.

Seraphine had both hands around Aldric's left wrist, pouring prayer into a wound that refused to become a simple wound. She sealed arteries. She closed skin. She forced shock back from the heart by the old rites of battlefield mercy.

She did not restore the hand.

There was no hand to restore in the ordinary sense. Divine sight found the place where it should have been and recoiled from an absence that sat half an inch to the left of itself, as if the WAR-LINE had taken flesh, consecrated steel, and spatial certainty in one bite.

Caelen looked back the way they had come, not afraid exactly, but with the grim respect of a master tracker who had just discovered that the terrain itself had learned tactics.

Aldric spat blood into the gutter.

"Report," he said.

Maren stared at him.

That frightened her more than if he had screamed.

"Military line," Caelen answered at once. "Not loose beasts. Not pets. Denial patrol. Formation-breakers. They were trying to scatter us, not finish us."

"They took your hand," Lyris said.

Aldric looked at the bandaged end of his arm as if it belonged to a soldier under his command.

"They failed to take the mission."

Seraphine's face went white with a fury that had nowhere holy to go.

"They could have killed us," Lyris said.

"Several times," Seraphine said.

"The tentacles knew where we were going before we moved," Maren said, voice flat with delayed fear.

Maren wiped blood from her temple and looked towards Sablehook's distant roofs. "They stopped when civilians were at risk."

Aldric's face hardened.

Somehow, that made it worse.

"Then he has built an army that can choose restraint," he said. "Good. We know more than we did."

Maren realised then that Aldric would spend his own body the way Banki spent monsters, and both men would call it protection.

The thought stayed under her ribs. She had sung men steady after battles, softened hard choices into courage, made pain survivable with melody. For the first time, she feared what a song could make bearable.

"We also lost the direct route," Caelen said.

"Then we take the canal maintenance run."

"And if another patrol finds us?"

Aldric adjusted his grip on the sword with one hand.

"Then we scatter faster."

They moved.

Behind them, the WAR-LINE resumed its patrol.

Below everything, the Elder Node opened its awareness towards the surface.

## The Empty Room

Aldric Thorne stood in the sub-level of Sablehook at four-seventeen in the morning and looked at an empty room.

His left sleeve was pinned above a bandage Seraphine had remade three times and still distrusted. The stump did not ache like ordinary flesh. It rang. Every pulse arrived half an inch from where his body expected it, as if the WAR-LINE had not merely taken the hand but taught the wound to disagree with creation.

He had refused a litter. He had refused rest. He had accepted exactly enough healing to keep command from becoming theatre.

In the corridor before the others caught up, he had reached to tighten his shield strap and found only air. For one private second he stood with his remaining hand open, waiting for the missing one to correct the mistake.

Then he closed the hand he still possessed and entered the room.

The room had not been empty long.

He knew it without spell or prayer. Some knowledge lived in the body after enough doors had been opened at sword-point. The floor was too clean. The air was too recently disturbed. The room had the stillness of a throat after the lie had already been swallowed.

Salt-treated stone. Clean floor. Faint chemical bite. Old blood, fresh copper, and the sour edge Aldric had learned to associate with psionic work during the Choir campaign.

No chains remained. No table. No cage. The absence did not comfort him. People removed chains when they wanted innocence to look portable.

On the south wall, four bolt scars had been filled with matching stone paste. Too careful. On the floor, a circle had been scrubbed until it was cleaner than the stone around it. Too clean. Near the ceiling, one copper hook remained because whoever stripped the room had missed it or had not considered it worth the seconds.

Aldric considered the hook worth the seconds.

Everything evil forgot became testimony.

Behind him, Seraphine paused at the kitchen threshold and went pale.

"What?"

"There is a hole here," she said.

Not a corpse. Not a ghost. Not even a soul beyond reach. An absence without a dead, and no prayer in her training knew what to do with that.

Aldric looked back at the room.

"Document it."

Her hands shook once before the preservation light steadied them.

Caelen traced the far wall with two fingers. "Door here. Gone now. Stone fused to stone. We can break through, but it will take hours and wake anything watching."

"Not yet."

Lyris knelt by a dark seam in the floor. She scraped residue into a vial, sniffed, regretted sniffing, and sealed it. Then she uncapped a second vial with her teeth because her left hand had not entirely stopped shaking and she refused to give the room the courtesy of noticing.

"Deepmind family," she said.

The word settled hard.

Aldric's missing hand tried to close around a shield that was not there. Pain rang up the shortened arm, bright enough to make the room narrow. He let it narrow. Then he made it widen again by force.

"Mind-Eater," Aldric said.

"Close enough that I would rather be wrong from a distance." Lyris held the vial to the light. "Purer than the Choir compound. This room did not receive diluted product. It housed the source, or something close to it."

Seraphine made the sign of the Dawnfather without looking away from the empty room.

"Do not sanctify it yet," Lyris said.

"I was not."

"Good."

"I was asking not to hate what we find before we find it."

No one answered her.

Aldric saw the salvage man again: grey-green eyes, polite answers, a businessman's coat, children rescued, traffickers broken, every accusation accepted with a calm that had revealed nothing useful.

The man had been living above this.

For one ashamed instant, Aldric wished the room had offered him a body. Bodies gave grief shape and justice a target. Empty rooms demanded faith before proof had finished speaking, and he feared how ready he was to provide it.

His remaining hand rose to make the Dawnfather's sign and stopped at half a shape.

Aldric tried to pray for certainty. The prayer would not form. Certainty had already taken a hand and still wanted more.

So he prayed for endurance instead, and hated that it sounded so close to permission.

The darker answer came easier.

Mercy had knocked at Sablehook's door at noon and been shown clean rooms while the true rooms fled. Mercy had waited for warrants and forms while a child's name vanished from the world. Mercy had left him with one hand and a room full of absence.

Aldric felt the thought arrive and did not cast it out.

Good did not have to be soft. Light cauterised. Fire sterilised. The Dawnfather's dawn was beautiful from a distance, but up close it burned rot out of wounds and left scar behind. If darkness wore children as shields, then goodness would learn to cut around children and through everything else.

Seraphine watched him and felt her own prayer recoil. The Dawnfather's silence had wounded her; Aldric's certainty frightened her more.

The empty room gave too little and too much at once.

"Caelen. Track him. The proprietor. He left recently."

The ranger vanished up the stairs.

"Seraphine. Preserve everything. Every trace."

She nodded.

Her preservation light held. Her restoration prayer remained ash-cold. The Dawnfather gave her enough to witness and not enough to mend, and Seraphine felt the rebuke in the difference.

"Lyris. Could a mortal connect himself to Deepmind tissue?"

Lyris did not answer quickly. The pause chilled the room.

"There is a forbidden discipline," she said at last. "Neural Savant. Rare. Usually fatal. Always obscene. The practitioner takes Deepmind neural matter into his own body. Not studies. Takes. If he survives, his mind stops being only his."

Aldric waited.

"Enhanced telepathy," she said. "Mind extraction. Psionic force. Blind spots in normal detection. Everything we have been failing to name."
